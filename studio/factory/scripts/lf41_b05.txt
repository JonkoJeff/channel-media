@chapter A19 bedroom_night | The perfectionism question
The perfectionism idea is very appealing. It turns procrastination into something almost flattering. I'm not lazy, I just have very high standards. And many self-help books have repeated it for years. Procrastinators, they say, are people who care so much about doing things perfectly that they freeze. It sounds right. But when researchers gathered the evidence together, the picture looked rather different.

@char A10 living_room_day | Back to Steel's review | What the numbers showed
Remember Piers Steel's large review from 2007, the one that gathered hundreds of earlier sources? Perfectionism was one of the things it looked at. And the link between perfectionism and procrastination turned out to be much weaker than many people expected. Across the studies, perfectionism on its own didn't seem to explain very much of why people put things off.

@quote A13 cafe_sunset | Perfectionists actually procrastinate less, but they worry about it more. | Piers Steel, 2007
In an interview at the time, Steel put it in a memorable way. Perfectionism, he said, is not the culprit. Perfectionists actually procrastinate less, but they worry about it more. That's a lovely twist. People with very high standards may feel more anxious about their delays, and talk about them more, which might be part of why the two ideas got tied together in the first place.

@char A18 attic_rain | A little nuance | Some kinds matter more
Other researchers have added some nuance. Perfectionism comes in different flavours. Having high standards for yourself is one thing. Constantly fearing other people's judgment, or worrying that any mistake is a disaster, is quite another. That second, more anxious kind may be more closely linked to delay. So for some people, fear of getting it wrong really can play a part. It just isn't the whole story for most of us.

@chapter A15 cafe_sunset | The stronger link
So if perfectionism isn't the main thread, what is? In Steel's review, one of the strongest and most consistent links was with something called impulsiveness. That word might sound surprising. We usually think of impulsive people as acting too quickly, not too slowly. But in this sense, impulsiveness means being easily pulled toward whatever feels good or interesting right now.

@char A12 street_day | Pulled toward now | The nearest reward wins
Think about Ben's evening again. Every time the speech felt uncomfortable, something closer and easier was waiting. A tidy shelf. A message on his phone. An article about confetti. Each one offered a small, immediate reward. A person who's more easily pulled by those small rewards, and more easily distracted, will tend to drift away from hard tasks more often. That's not laziness. It's a strong pull toward now.

@study A10 cafe_sunset | Daniel Gustavson and colleagues | University of Colorado, published 2014 | A study of identical and non-identical twins on procrastination and impulsivity
A study of twins, published in 2014 by Daniel Gustavson and colleagues in Colorado, explored this link from another angle. By comparing identical twins, who share all their genes, with non-identical twins, who share about half, researchers can estimate how much of a trait runs in families. They looked at procrastination, impulsivity, and how well people manage their goals.

@char A13 living_room_day | Shared roots | Procrastination and impulsivity
They found that procrastination was partly heritable, like many traits. But the most interesting part was the overlap. The genetic influences on procrastination and on impulsivity seemed to be very closely shared. In other words, the same underlying tendencies that make some people act on the spur of the moment may also make them more likely to put things off. Both are, in a sense, about being pulled toward the present.

@photo lf41_b05_01.jpg | Shared roots, different leaves
Of course, genes are never destiny. Two plants with the same roots can grow very differently depending on light, soil and care. The twin study tells us that some people may start out with a stronger pull toward now. It doesn't tell us anyone is stuck. Habits, surroundings and small tricks still matter a great deal, and we'll come to some of those later tonight.

@list A19 rooftop_dusk | So far tonight | Not mainly laziness ;; Short-term mood repair ;; Future self as a stranger ;; Perfectionism, a weaker link ;; Impulsiveness, a stronger one
Let's pause and gather what we've found so far. Procrastination isn't mainly laziness. It often works as quick mood repair, getting rid of a bad feeling right now. We tend to treat our future selves a little like strangers. Perfectionism turns out to be a weaker link than many people think. And a pull toward immediate rewards, impulsiveness, seems to be a stronger one.

@char A03 cabin_fireplace | One more piece | A formula for motivation
And that brings us to one of the neatest ideas in the whole field. A way of putting several of these pieces together into a single picture. Piers Steel and his colleagues even wrote it as a kind of formula, a little like a recipe for motivation. Don't worry, there won't be any sums. But it helps explain why Ben's speech will probably get written on Friday night.
