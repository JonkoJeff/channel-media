@char A02 street_day | In the supermarket | Muttering on aisle five
You're in a supermarket, scanning a long shelf for one particular thing. Without meaning to, you start saying its name under your breath. Oat milk, oat milk, oat milk. Somebody walks past and you feel a little embarrassed. But is the muttering just a quirk, or is it helping you find the carton?

@chapter A05 autumn_overlook | Saying it out loud
In 2012, two researchers decided to test exactly this kind of moment.

@study A10 living_room_day | Gary Lupyan & Daniel Swingley | Wisconsin and Pennsylvania, 2012 | Saying a target's name out loud helped people find it faster in a visual search
Gary Lupyan, whom we met earlier, and Daniel Swingley, at the University of Pennsylvania, published their study in the Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology. In the first experiment, people looked at screens of twenty pictures of different objects and had to find a particular one. Sometimes they simply read the name of the target. Other times, they also said its name out loud while they searched.

@char A14 cafe_sunset | The result | Faster with words
Saying the name out loud helped. People found the object faster when they spoke its name. The researchers' idea was that hearing the word helps the brain call up what the object looks like, and that sharpens the search.

#ai lf29_b07_01.jpg | Editorial still-life of a long tidy grocery shelf with plain unbranded jars and paper bags of grains in soft rows, warm shop lighting, calm everyday mood, muted palette, no people, no text, no logos

@photo lf29_b07_01.jpg | Scanning the shelf
Then they tried something closer to real life: a virtual shopping trip, with photos of products on supermarket shelves. People had to find every example of a particular item as quickly as possible. And here the story got more interesting.

@list A12 living_room_day | When muttering helps, and when it doesn't | Very familiar item: saying its name helped ;; Less familiar item: saying its name slowed people down
For very familiar products, saying the name out loud helped again. But for a less familiar product, whose name didn't bring a clear picture to mind, saying it out loud actually slowed people down. So the trick works when the word and the picture are strongly linked in your memory. When they aren't, the word may just get in the way.

@char A18 cabin_fireplace | A fair summary | Not magic, a tool
That's a nice, honest result. Talking to yourself is not magic, and it doesn't always help. It's a tool, and like any tool, it works best for the right job. When you're searching for your keys, saying, keys, keys, may help your brain hold the picture of your keys in mind as you look.

@chapter A16 rooftop_dusk | Self-talk in sport
Some of the best-studied self-talk in the world doesn't happen in a supermarket at all. It happens on running tracks, tennis courts and swimming pools.

@char A11 street_day | Athletes talk to themselves | You've seen it
If you've ever watched a tennis match, you've probably seen a player muttering between points, or shouting a word of encouragement at themselves. Sport psychologists have studied this for years, and many coaches now teach athletes to plan what they say to themselves, the way they plan their training.

@study A03 autumn_overlook | Hatzigeorgiadis, Zourbanos, Galanis & Theodorakis | University of Thessaly, 2011 | A meta-analysis of 32 studies found that self-talk improves sport performance
In 2011, Antonis Hatzigeorgiadis and his colleagues at the University of Thessaly in Greece published a meta-analysis in the journal Perspectives on Psychological Science. A meta-analysis gathers many studies together to see what they show as a whole. They combined 32 studies of self-talk in sport.

@stat A12 cafe_sunset | Studies combined | 32 | sport studies of self-talk (Hatzigeorgiadis et al., 2011)
Their overall conclusion was clear: self-talk improves sport performance. But the details are where it gets useful, because not all self-talk worked the same way.

@list A15 living_room_day | Two kinds of self-talk | Instructional: short technical cues ;; Motivational: energy and confidence
They looked at two kinds. Instructional self-talk is a short technical cue, such as a swimmer telling themselves, elbow up. Motivational self-talk is about energy and confidence, such as, come on, you can do this. Both helped, but they helped in different situations.

@list A20 autumn_overlook | What worked where | Fine skills: instructional cues helped more ;; Strength and endurance: motivational talk helped more ;; Practice made self-talk work better
Instructional cues worked better for fine skills that need precision, like a golf putt. Motivational self-talk worked better for tasks that need strength or endurance. Self-talk had a larger effect on fine skills overall, and the researchers thought that was mainly because it helps people concentrate. And self-talk that people had practised in training worked better than self-talk tried for the first time.

@char A02 street_day | For the rest of us | Short and specific
You don't need to be an athlete to use this. When you're learning something fiddly, a short instruction, slow and steady, or, thumb under, may help you focus. When you're tired and need to keep going, a few words of encouragement may help more. And like any skill, it gets better with practice.

@chapter A05 autumn_overlook | A question instead of a promise
Now for one of the most surprising studies about self-talk, which is about the smallest of changes: the order of two words.

@study A10 cabin_fireplace | Senay, Albarracín & Noguchi | Psychological Science, 2010 | Asking Will I? led to more anagrams solved than declaring I will
In 2010, Ibrahim Senay, Dolores Albarracín and Kenji Noguchi published a study in Psychological Science. In the first experiment, people about to work on anagrams, those word puzzles where you rearrange letters, either asked themselves whether they would work on them, or simply told themselves that they would.

@char A14 cafe_sunset | The surprise | The question won
You might expect the confident statement, I will, to work better. It didn't. The people who asked themselves the question solved more anagrams. In later experiments, people simply wrote the words Will I or I will, as part of what they thought was a handwriting task. Writing Will I again led to better anagram performance, and to stronger intentions to exercise.

@char A18 attic_rain | Why a question? | Your own reasons
The researchers' explanation was that a question invites an answer. When you ask yourself, will I, your mind starts looking for reasons, and the reasons it finds are your own. That kind of motivation, the kind that comes from within, tends to last longer than a command. Their analysis suggested that this inner motivation was what made the difference.

@chapter A12 living_room_day | Talking to yourself as you
A few years later, Albarracín was part of another study that changed just one more small word.

@study A03 autumn_overlook | Sanda Dolcos & Dolores Albarracín | European Journal of Social Psychology, 2014 | Encouraging yourself as you led to better performance than encouraging yourself as I
In 2014, Sanda Dolcos and Dolores Albarracín published a study in the European Journal of Social Psychology. People gave themselves advice before solving anagrams, either in the first person, as I, or in the second person, as you. Those who addressed themselves as you solved more anagrams, and reported more positive intentions about the task.

@char A19 cabin_fireplace | Why you? | The voice of a helper
Think back to Vygotsky. Children first hear guidance from other people, who talk to them as you. You can do it. Careful now. Perhaps when we speak to ourselves that way, we tap into that old, supportive voice of a helper. It's as if a little bit of the coach or the parent comes back to stand beside us.

@char A10 bedroom_night | Next | Talking to learn
So talking to yourself can help you search, perform and get going. But there's another place where it seems to do something even more interesting: when you're trying to understand something new. Some of the best learners, it turns out, are the ones who explain things to themselves.
