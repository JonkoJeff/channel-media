@chapter N09 living_room_day | Stage three: shared problems
Now we reach the final stage of our case file. Pleasant contact had failed. So Sherif tried something different. He created problems that mattered to everyone, and that neither group could solve on its own.

@char A13 living_room_day | A shared goal | Bigger than either team
Psychologists call these superordinate goals. A superordinate goal is a goal that both groups want, but that neither can reach alone. It sits above the rivalry. To get it, the two sides have no choice but to work together.

#ai lf15_b03_01.jpg | Editorial illustration of an old metal water tank on wooden stilts beside a dusty camp road, a single water faucet below it, hot summer afternoon light, warm ochre and green palette, calm documentary mood, no people, no text, no logos

@photo lf15_b03_01.jpg | The water stops
The first problem was water. The camp's water supply suddenly stopped. The staff had quietly blocked it, with a sack stuffed into the faucet. For a group of thirsty boys in an Oklahoma summer, this was not a game anymore.

@char N20 bedroom_night | Working side by side | Over 45 minutes
Boys from both groups went looking for the problem, and they worked on it together. According to the account of the study, it took over forty five minutes to clear. When the water finally ran again, the Eagles, who had no canteens with them, were allowed to drink first. Nobody fought about it.

@list A10 bedroom_night | The film night | Treasure Island or Kidnapped ;; The film cost 15 dollars ;; Each group paid 3 dollars and 50 cents ;; The camp paid the rest
The next problem was a film. The boys had to choose between two films, Treasure Island and Kidnapped, and they chose Treasure Island. The film cost fifteen dollars, which was more than the camp would pay alone. Each group put in three dollars and fifty cents, and the camp covered the rest. To watch together, they had to pay together.

@char A19 bedroom_night | A truck that won't start | Everyone pulls the same rope
The last problem was a truck. The truck that was supposed to bring food for both groups wouldn't start. The boys pulled it with the same tug of war rope they had once used against each other. The rope had not changed. What had changed was which direction everyone was pulling.

@stat A18 bedroom_night | Friendship choices at the end | about 2 in 3 | crossed group lines, by the study's account
And the results were striking. By the end of the camp, roughly two thirds of the boys' friendship choices crossed group lines, according to the study's account. The rivals had become friends. And when it was time to leave, the boys asked to go home together, on a single bus.

@char N09 living_room_day | A happy ending | Peace, engineered
That is how the story is usually told in textbooks. Conflict was created, and then it was solved. Gina Perry described the message like this: it was a very optimistic message for its time, because it said peace could be engineered. But our case file isn't quite closed yet.

@chapter N14 conspiracy_board | The camp before the camp
At the start, I asked you to keep one thing in mind. A different camp, one year earlier, that almost nobody heard about for decades. Now it's time to open that file.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Muzafer Sherif's team | Middle Grove, New York | An earlier camp study, summer 1953
In 1953, Sherif ran a very similar camp in Middle Grove, in the state of New York. Again there were two groups of boys. Again there was a tournament, with knives as the prize. And according to reporting on Gina Perry's book, the staff even split up friendships to encourage the rivalry.

@char N13 conspiracy_board | The boys noticed | Something didn't add up
At Middle Grove, the plan did not work. The tournament became fierce, and one boy, Doug Griset, was knocked out by a baseball. Later, he was sent home because he was so homesick. And the other boys began to sense that the adults were behind the trouble.

@char N22 conspiracy_board | The groups united | Against the adults
Instead of turning on each other, the two groups turned toward each other, and united against the adults. The experiment collapsed. It was abandoned, and for decades it was barely mentioned. Perry brought it back to light in her book, The Lost Boys, published in 2018.

@quote A10 bedroom_night | Smart adults manipulating 10 and 11-year-old children, way too easy. | Doug Griset, looking back
Years later, Doug Griset looked back on it with clear eyes. He said the counsellors had set up a plan to prove a point, rather than letting things play out. In his words: smart adults manipulating ten and eleven year old children, way too easy.

@chapter A19 bedroom_night | Rethinking the case
So let's return to the question from earlier. Was the rivalry at Robbers Cave natural? The honest answer is that it was helped along. The staff rigged the tournament, staged the picnic, blocked the water and stalled the truck.

@list A13 living_room_day | The main criticisms | The conflict was engineered ;; The boys were deceived ;; Only 22 very similar boys ;; The researchers were part of the story
Critics point to several problems. The conflict was engineered, with no real dispute behind it. The boys were deceived, and some were exposed to fights and theft. The sample was small, just twenty two very similar boys. And the researchers were not neutral observers. They were part of the story.

@study A18 bedroom_night | Henri Tajfel and John Turner | Social identity theory | Published in 1979
Other research also complicated the picture. In work published in 1979, Henri Tajfel and John Turner argued that competition is not even needed. Simply being sorted into a group can be enough for people to favour their own side. You may remember how the Robbers Cave boys wanted to challenge the others before any prize existed.

@study N10 living_room_day | Paluck, Green and Green | A review of contact experiments | Published in 2019
And what about bringing groups together? A review by Elizabeth Levy Paluck and colleagues, published in 2019, looked again at twenty seven experiments that randomly assigned contact and measured prejudice at least a day later. The effects were weaker for ethnic and racial prejudice. And the largest, most careful studies tended to show the smallest effects.

@study A10 bedroom_night | Tyerman and Spencer | English scout groups | Published in 1983
A study by Tyerman and Spencer, published in 1983, looked at English boy scouts. Unlike the Robbers Cave boys, these scouts already knew each other, they were split into four patrols, and they had a cooperative leader. Under competition, they stayed friendly. So the people, their history and their leaders seem to matter too.

@char N09 living_room_day | Not the same everywhere | Culture matters
Another researcher, Wetherell, writing in 1982, found that Polynesian children in New Zealand were far more welcoming towards other groups. Results like these suggest that what happened at Robbers Cave was not a universal law. It was one group of boys, in one place, in one particular decade.

@quote A19 bedroom_night | They carry those experiments round with them for the rest of their lives. | Gina Perry, on the former campers
And there is a human side to the story. When Perry spoke with former campers decades later, she found that the memories had not simply faded. In her words: they carry those experiments round with them for the rest of their lives. The boys were not just data. They were children having a summer they would never forget.

@char A13 living_room_day | Two views of Sherif | Fair criticism, or too harsh?
Not everyone agrees with Perry's tough verdict. The psychologist Nick Haslam, reviewing her book, praised it but felt that parts of her treatment of Sherif were a little ungenerous, and that 1950s research should not simply be judged by today's ethical rules. Both views can help us read the study more wisely.

@chapter N09 living_room_day | What we can still take from it
So is Robbers Cave just a broken story? Not quite. Some of its lessons hold up well, if we hold them gently, as ideas rather than laws of nature.

#ai lf15_b03_02.jpg | Editorial illustration of a thick old rope lying across a sunny grassy field in front of a quiet wooded camp, warm late afternoon light, soft green and gold palette, hopeful calm mood, no people, no text, no logos

@photo lf15_b03_02.jpg | The same rope, a different direction
The rope is a good picture to keep. When people pull against each other for one prize, every small thing becomes a reason to resent the other side. When they share a goal that matters to all of them, the same people can pull in the same direction.

@list A13 living_room_day | Ideas you can use | Notice when a situation sets people against each other ;; Contact alone may not be enough ;; Look for a goal both sides really share ;; Ask who benefits from the rivalry
Here are a few ideas to take with you. Notice when a situation sets people against each other, at school, at work or online. Remember that simply putting people in the same room may not be enough. Look for a goal both sides really share. And sometimes, ask who benefits from keeping the rivalry going.

@char A03 living_room_day | The jigsaw classroom | Shared goals in practice
One well known example is the jigsaw classroom, developed by the psychologist Elliot Aronson to ease tensions in desegregated American schools. Each student holds one piece of the lesson, so the group only succeeds when everyone shares. It is a quiet echo of that rope in Oklahoma.

Let's go back to where we started. A summer camp in the hills, with cabins, a creek and a ball field. Twenty two boys who arrived as strangers, became rivals, and left on a single bus. And another group of boys, a year earlier, who saw through the adults and stood together instead. Both stories say something hopeful about us.
