@chapter A19 cabin_fireplace | Feelings and reason
For a very long time, people have pictured reason and emotion as opponents. The cool head and the hot heart. The wise rider and the wild horse. The idea is old, and it's comfortable. Good decisions come from reason. Bad decisions come when feelings get in the way.

@char A13 living_room_day | Mara's advice | Don't let feelings decide
If Mara asked a friend for advice tonight, she might well hear exactly that. Don't let your feelings decide this. Write down the facts. Be sensible. And it sounds wise. But one of the most famous stories in neuroscience suggests that a mind without feelings doesn't become perfectly sensible. It becomes stuck.

@study A03 cafe_sunset | Antonio Damasio | University of Iowa, 1994 | Descartes' Error: a patient called Elliot, frontal lobe damage, intact intelligence, unable to decide
The story is told by the neuroscientist Antonio Damasio, in his 1994 book Descartes' Error. Damasio describes a patient he calls Elliot. Elliot had been a successful professional, a good husband and father. Then a tumour grew just behind his forehead, pressing on the frontal lobes. Surgeons removed it, along with some damaged tissue.

@char A10 cabin_fireplace | Clever as ever | On every test
After the operation, Elliot seemed perfectly fine. His memory was good. His language was good. On intelligence tests he scored normally, or better than normally. He could discuss politics and current events. By every standard measure, his reasoning was intact.

@char A04 attic_rain | And yet | Life fell apart
And yet his life fell apart. At work, a simple task like sorting documents could take an entire afternoon, because he couldn't decide whether to sort them by date, or by size, or by relevance. Each option seemed as good as the others. He weighed and weighed, and never quite arrived.

@char A12 rooftop_dusk | Flat inside | Telling a sad story calmly
Damasio noticed something else. Elliot could talk about the disaster his life had become with complete calm, as though describing a stranger. When he was shown upsetting pictures, he said himself that he knew he should feel something, but didn't. The feelings that colour our choices had gone quiet.

@char A18 cafe_sunset | Another patient | Two dates for an appointment
In the same book, Damasio describes another patient with similar damage. At the end of a visit, he was offered two possible dates for his next appointment. And for nearly half an hour, he listed reasons for and against each one. Other plans, the weather, possible meetings. A perfectly logical list. With no end.

@char A13 living_room_day | The lesson | Reasons are not enough
Eventually, someone simply told him to come on the second date. He thanked them, and left. The lesson Damasio drew is a striking one. Reasons alone don't settle anything. You can always find another reason. Something else is needed to tip the scale, and that something is feeling.

@chapter A05 autumn_overlook | Markers in the body
Damasio called his idea the somatic marker hypothesis. Somatic simply means of the body. The idea is that, through experience, situations become tagged with bodily feelings, a little tightening, a little lift. When you imagine an option, the tag comes back, before you've worked anything out.

@char A20 cafe_sunset | Mara again | A lift and a sinking
Think again of Mara. When she imagines walking along the sea with her sister's children, something in her chest lifts. When she imagines her empty flat with boxes in every corner, something sinks. Those aren't distractions from her decision. According to Damasio, they're part of how a decision gets made at all.

@study A01 living_room_day | Bechara, Damasio, Tranel and Damasio | University of Iowa, 1997 | Four decks of cards: the body warned people before they knew
To test the idea, Damasio's group designed a card game, published in a well known paper in 1997. Players picked cards, one at a time, from four decks. Each card brought pretend winnings or pretend losses. Two of the decks gave big wins, but even bigger losses now and then. The other two gave small wins, and small losses. Over time, the modest decks were the better choice.

@char A15 rooftop_dusk | Sweaty palms | Before the hunch
The researchers also measured tiny changes in sweat on the players' skin, a sign of arousal. And they found something remarkable. Healthy players began to show a stress response just before reaching for the risky decks, and began to avoid them, before they could say what was wrong with those decks. The body seemed to know first.

@char A10 cabin_fireplace | And Elliot's group | No warning signal
Patients with damage like Elliot's behaved differently. They kept going back to the risky decks, even after losing heavily, and their skin showed no warning signal before they reached for them. Without the bodily alarm, they seemed unable to learn from the pattern of losses.

@chapter A16 autumn_overlook | A careful doubt
It's a beautiful story. But here, as promised, is the doubt. In 2004, Tiago Maia and James McClelland repeated the card game and asked players much more detailed questions as they went along. They found that people often knew more about the decks, and earlier, than the original questions had revealed.

@char A13 attic_rain | Knowing more | Than they said
So perhaps the body wasn't quite so far ahead of the mind as it first seemed. Researchers still argue about how much the somatic marker idea can explain. But the broad lesson has held up well. Feeling and reasoning are not opponents. In a healthy brain, they work together, and decisions suffer when either one goes missing.

Picture those four decks of cards on the table. Two of them look generous at first. The other two look dull. And long before anyone can explain it, something in the body begins to lean away from the generous ones.

@chapter A19 cabin_fireplace | Borrowed feelings
Feelings help us decide, then. But they can also wander in from somewhere else entirely. Psychologists call these incidental emotions, feelings caused by one thing that spill over into a judgement about something else.

@study A12 cafe_sunset | Norbert Schwarz and Gerald Clore | Illinois, United States, 1983 | Rainy days, sunny days and how satisfied people said they were
In 1983, Norbert Schwarz and Gerald Clore telephoned people on sunny days and on rainy days, and asked how satisfied they were with their lives as a whole. People called on sunny days tended to report more satisfaction. But when the interviewer first casually asked about the weather, the difference shrank. Once people noticed where the mood came from, they set it aside.

@char A03 rooftop_dusk | A later check | The effect looked tiny
That study became very famous. But in 2013, Richard Lucas and Nicole Lawless looked at data from over a million people across the United States, and found that day to day weather had almost no link with how satisfied they said they were. So the classic weather effect, at least, may be much smaller than it first appeared.

@char A20 living_room_day | The idea survives | Mood can leak
Still, the deeper idea has support from many other studies. A mood caused by one thing can leak into a decision about another. A bad night's sleep, a rude message, a beautiful sunset. They can tilt how a whole option looks, without you noticing that they've done it.

@char A18 cabin_fireplace | Mara's evening | A long day at work
Perhaps Mara had a hard day at work today. Perhaps that's part of why the sea looks so inviting tonight. That doesn't make her feeling wrong. But it might be worth asking, gently, whether she'd feel the same way on a good day. It's one of the simplest questions you can ask about any big choice.

So, feelings aren't the enemy of good decisions. They're part of the machinery. And in the next part of our story, we'll meet another part of that machinery, the shortcuts the brain uses to answer hard questions quickly, and the two psychologists who spent years cataloguing them.
