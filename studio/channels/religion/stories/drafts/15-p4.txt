CHAPTER: What the Earliest Believers Said
The oldest written words about the cross are not in the Gospels. They are in the letters of Paul, written about twenty years after the events. Paul quotes a tradition he says he received, that Christ died for our sins in accordance with the Scriptures, was buried, and was raised on the third day. || 1 Corinthians 15:3–4 || text-card
Notice how early this is. Most scholars date the formula to within a few years of the crucifixion, which makes it one of the oldest pieces of Christian teaching we have. It shows that from the beginning, the death of Jesus was not only remembered but interpreted. || Papyrus 46, Chester Beatty, Pauline letters || wikimedia
Paul also admits how strange the message sounded. He writes that the word of the cross was foolishness to those who were perishing, a stumbling block to Jews, and folly to Gentiles. A crucified Messiah was, to many ears, a contradiction. || 1 Corinthians 1:18–23 || text-card
To see why, remember what the Hebrew Scriptures were thought to say. The Torah describes one who hangs on a tree as under a curse. Some early Jewish readers could not imagine a Messiah ending that way, and Paul answers the objection directly in his letter to the Galatians. || Deuteronomy 21:23; Galatians 3:13 || text-card
So the first Christians went looking in their Scriptures for ways to understand what had happened. They found the suffering righteous person of the Psalms, and the servant in the book of Isaiah, who is wounded for the transgressions of others. || Isaiah 53, Great Isaiah Scroll, Shrine of the Book || wikimedia
Isaiah fifty-three, written centuries earlier and probably about the nation or a prophetic figure in exile, became one of the main lenses through which Christians understood the cross. How far Jesus himself used it is debated, but its language is everywhere in the early writings. || Isaiah 53:5 || text-card
Other images appeared too. A sacrifice, a ransom, a Passover lamb, a king enthroned in an unexpected way. The New Testament does not give a single theory of what the cross accomplished, and later Christian thinkers developed several. || Lamb of God, Ghent Altarpiece, van Eyck || wikimedia
For Christians, then, the cross is where the story's meaning is concentrated. For historians, it is the best-attested event in the life of Jesus. These two views meet in the same place, though they ask different questions. || Crucifixion, Fra Angelico, Convent of San Marco || wikimedia
CHAPTER: Other Voices
It matters that the story of the cross has been told, and retold, by people who did not share the Gospel writers' faith. For many centuries, the passion narratives were used to blame Jews collectively for the death of Jesus, with terrible consequences in persecution and violence. || Medieval passion play manuscript || wikimedia
Modern scholarship and the churches have moved far from that reading. As we saw earlier, the Gospels were written decades later, in a setting of rivalry between communities, and the Roman governor was the one who ordered the execution. The Second Vatican Council rejected the charge of collective guilt in 1965. || Nostra Aetate, 1965 || text-card
Jewish writers across the centuries have also told the story in their own way. Some modern Jewish scholars, such as Geza Vermes and Amy-Jill Levine, have placed Jesus firmly within the Judaism of his time, and have read the trial accounts as sources to be handled with care. || Geza Vermes, Jesus the Jew || text-card
The Talmud has a few passages that are sometimes taken to refer to Jesus, but their date and meaning are uncertain, and historians treat them with caution. The earliest non-Christian witnesses we have are Roman: Tacitus, and perhaps Pliny and Suetonius. || Babylonian Talmud, Sanhedrin 43a || wikimedia
Islam gives yet another account. The Quran honours Jesus as a prophet and the Messiah, born of the Virgin Mary. Of the end of his earthly life, it says that they did not kill him and they did not crucify him, but it was made to appear so to them. || Quran 4:157 || text-card
Muslim interpreters have read this in different ways over the centuries. Many held that God took Jesus up to himself before his death, and that someone else was made to look like him. Others have read the verse differently. Here a historian simply notes that it is a different tradition, with its own account. || Quran 4:158 || text-card
In the second century, other writings circulated among Christians. One is the Gospel of Peter, of which a fragment survives. It tells the passion in a different voice, and in it Herod, and not Pilate, takes charge of the execution. Scholars debate how it relates to the canonical Gospels. || Akhmim fragment, Gospel of Peter || wikimedia
The point to take from all this is that no tradition lacks its own way of telling the story. The historian looks for what the earliest sources agree on, and is open about what each community added in the telling. || Manuscript fragment, Oxyrhynchus papyri || wikimedia
CHAPTER: The Cross Becomes a Symbol
At first, Christians did not use the cross as their emblem. For a few centuries, the symbols found in their homes and graves were a fish, an anchor, a shepherd, a dove. A crucifixion was too shameful and too recent to display. || Fish and anchor, Roman catacombs || wikimedia
The earliest known image that mocks the idea is a piece of graffiti scratched into plaster in Rome. It shows a man with a donkey's head on a cross, and a figure beside him, with the words, Alexamenos worships his god. It is usually dated to around the third century. || Alexamenos graffito, Palatine Museum, Rome || wikimedia
That drawing shows how outsiders saw the claim. A god who died as a slave was a joke. The drawing survives, and so does the ridicule, but so did the faith, and the believers it targeted took the very thing it mocked as their central sign. || Palatine Hill, Rome || wikimedia
Early Christian art was reluctant to show Jesus on a cross. One of the earliest surviving carvings is on the wooden doors of the church of Santa Sabina in Rome, from around 432. The scene is small, in a corner of a panel, with Jesus and the two others shown standing, with arms outstretched. || Santa Sabina doors, Rome || wikimedia
Another early image is on a small ivory plaque, now in the British Museum, made around the same time. It shows the crucified Jesus alongside the death of Judas, and is one of the oldest crucifixion scenes that has survived. || Ivory casket, British Museum, c. 420–430 || wikimedia
A fuller image comes from Syria. The Rabbula Gospels, copied in 586, include a painted crucifixion with the soldiers casting lots, the two other men and the women looking on. By then the scene had become a familiar way of showing the Gospel story. || Rabbula Gospels, Biblioteca Medicea Laurenziana || wikimedia
Something else had changed. Under the emperor Constantine, who favoured Christianity from the early fourth century, crucifixion as a punishment was brought to an end, according to later writers. So the cruellest Roman penalty faded just as the cross became a sign of honour. || Constantine, Capitoline Museums || wikimedia
Over the following centuries, the cross appeared on churches, coins, crowns, flags and tombstones. The Eastern and Western churches developed different styles of crucifix. The image of a victim became, in Christian practice, an image of love, sacrifice and hope. || Byzantine cross, Hagia Sophia || wikimedia
CHAPTER: What History Can Say
So what can a historian say with confidence about the crucifixion? Very few scholars doubt the core of it: that Jesus of Nazareth was crucified in Judea under Pontius Pilate, around the year thirty. Tacitus, Josephus and Paul all point the same way. || Tacitus, Annals 15.44 || text-card
Most would add that the execution followed some conflict in Jerusalem during Passover, perhaps involving the Temple, and that Jewish leaders played a part in handing him over. Beyond that, scholars disagree on the details of the hearings, the timing and the exact words. || Temple Mount, Jerusalem || wikimedia
The Gospels give a theological as well as a historical account. Their authors wanted to tell what the death meant, and not just what occurred. A fair reading keeps both in view, and notices where the stories are close, and where they part company. || Four Evangelists, Book of Kells || wikimedia
The tradition also shows a remarkable honesty. The death was shameful. The disciples had fled. Peter had denied him. A movement that wanted to flatter its founder would hardly have told the story this way. That is part of why historians trust the outline. || Garden of Gethsemane, Mount of Olives || wikimedia
What happened afterwards is a different kind of question. Christians believe that on the third day the tomb was found empty, and that Jesus appeared to his followers. Historians can study the claim, and the earliest reports of it, but they cannot settle it. That belongs to our next episode. || Empty tomb, Church of the Holy Sepulchre, Jerusalem || wikimedia
For now, we end where the Gospels end the day: with a body in a borrowed tomb, a few women watching, and the Sabbath coming on. For the people who were there, it looked like an ending. || Jerusalem at dusk, 19th century lithograph || loc
