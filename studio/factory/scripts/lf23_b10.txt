@chapter A03 autumn_overlook | Who reacts most
Think of two friends getting the same advice. You really should get more sleep. One nods and says, you're right, I'll try. The other bristles, folds their arms, and stays up even later that night, almost on principle. Same words, same kind intention, completely different reactions. And the advice was perfectly reasonable.

@char A12 living_room_day | You know the type | Maybe you are the type
Most of us know someone who simply can't bear being told what to do. Maybe it's a sibling, a colleague, a partner. And if you're honest, maybe it's you. So far we've treated reactance as something that happens to everyone. But does it happen equally to everyone?

@char A10 cafe_sunset | The question | Is reactance a personality trait?
That was the question that occupied researchers in the years after Jack Brehm first described reactance. Brehm had seen it mostly as a response to a situation. A rule appears, a freedom is threatened, and we push back. But it seemed obvious that some people pushed back far harder than others. Could that be measured?

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Hong & Page | Psychological Reports, 1989 | A psychological reactance scale: development, factor structure and reliability
In 1989, the psychologists Sung-Mook Hong and his colleague Page published a questionnaire designed to measure reactance as a trait, a lasting tendency rather than a passing mood. People rated how much they agreed with statements about rules, advice and being told what to do.

@list A05 cafe_sunset | The kind of statements on such scales | I resist when someone tries to influence me ;; Advice can feel like an intrusion ;; Rules make me want to do the opposite ;; I like to make my own decisions
You can probably guess where you'd land just by hearing statements like these. Some people agree with almost all of them. Others hardly recognise themselves at all. And that difference, it turned out, could be measured fairly reliably.

@char A13 rooftop_dusk | A lasting tendency | Some of us are built to push back
With a scale like this, reactance could finally be studied as part of who we are. People who score high tend to react more strongly to pressure, rules and persuasion in general. People who score low tend to take advice more calmly, even when it's delivered a little bluntly. Neither is good or bad. They are simply different temperaments. A person high in reactance may be harder to boss around, but also more likely to stand firm when standing firm really matters. A person low in reactance may be easier to guide, and also easier to work with. Each has its strengths.

@chapter A15 cabin_fireplace | Age and the urge to resist
So where does reactance come from? One of the first places researchers looked was age. After all, we all know the stubborn toddler and the eye-rolling teenager. But does reactance really change as we grow older?

@study A04 attic_rain | Hong, Giannakopoulos, Laing & Williams | Journal of Social Psychology, 1994 | Psychological reactance: effects of age and gender
In 1994, Sung-Mook Hong and his colleagues used the reactance scale with people of different ages, from young adults to older people. They wanted to see whether the tendency to push back was stronger at some stages of life than at others.

@char A03 living_room_day | What they found | Younger people pushed back more
Younger people tended to score higher in reactance than older people. The urge to resist seemed strongest early in adult life, and to soften as people grew older. That fits with what many of us feel. In our teens and twenties, independence is new and precious, and we guard it fiercely. Later, it's more secure, and needs less defending. You no longer have to prove to anyone that you can make your own choices.

@char A19 bedroom_night | A caution | Snapshots, not life stories
It's worth adding that a study like this compares different people at different ages, rather than following the same people over a lifetime. So it can't tell us exactly how one person changes. But the pattern, that reactance runs high in youth, has appeared often enough that most researchers take it seriously.

And age isn't the only thing that shapes how strongly we push back. It also seems to depend on where we grow up, and on what kind of freedom we've learned to care about most.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Eva Jonas and colleagues | Journal of Experimental Social Psychology, 2009 | Culture, self, and the emergence of reactance: is there a universal freedom?
In 2009, Eva Jonas and her colleagues compared people from more individualist cultures, which prize personal independence, with people from more collectivist cultures, where the group often comes first. They presented situations in which a freedom was threatened, either the person's own freedom, or the freedom of their group.

@char A12 cafe_sunset | The finding | Different freedoms, different triggers
People from more individualist backgrounds reacted most strongly when their own personal freedom was restricted. People from more collectivist backgrounds reacted more strongly when the restriction was aimed at their group. Everyone had a freedom worth defending. What differed was whose freedom it was.

@char A18 attic_rain | An everyday example | Me, or us?
Imagine being told you personally can't choose where to sit at a family dinner. For some people, that would sting far more than a rule applying to the whole family. For others, it would be the other way round. A rule placed on the family, by an outsider, would feel like the real insult, while a personal rule would barely register.

@char A10 cabin_fireplace | What it means | Reactance speaks many languages
That's a lovely detail, because it suggests reactance isn't just a Western habit of stubbornness. It seems to be a very human response. But each culture teaches us which freedoms matter most, and so each one has its own way of feeling cornered.

@list A20 living_room_day | What shapes how strongly we react | Our personality ;; Our stage of life ;; The culture we grew up in ;; How much the freedom matters to us
Brehm himself had pointed to that last one early on. The more important a freedom is to us, the more strongly we react when it's threatened. That's why someone might happily accept a rule about where to park, and then dig in their heels over a rule about who they're allowed to see.

@char A13 attic_rain | Reactance in the therapy room | Where it really matters
There's one place where knowing someone's reactance level turns out to be especially useful. The quiet room where people go to talk about their lives, and to try to change them.

@study A04 bedroom_night | Larry Beutler and colleagues | Journal of Clinical Psychology, 2011 | Resistance/reactance level
In 2011, Larry Beutler and his colleagues reviewed studies of psychotherapy that looked at how reactant clients were, and how directive their therapists were. Did a firm, guiding style work better, or a gentler, more open one? It's a practical question, because therapists have to choose a style every single day.

@char A03 cafe_sunset | The finding | Match the style to the person
For clients high in reactance, less directive therapy tended to work better. When therapists gave fewer instructions and left more room for the client to lead, these clients made more progress. For clients low in reactance, a more structured, guiding approach often suited them well. In other words, there was no single best way to help. The right style depended on how the person in the chair felt about being guided.

@char A15 rooftop_dusk | For everyday life | Know your listener
That's a useful idea far beyond therapy. If you know someone hates being told what to do, the kindest thing may be to offer options and step back. And if you know that person is you, it can help to notice the moment the push-back begins, and ask yourself whether you're resisting the advice, or just the feeling of being advised.

@char A12 living_room_day | A bridge | The years when it all comes to a head
And of course, if reactance runs highest in our younger years, there's one stage of life where it all comes together. A growing need for independence, parents who still set the rules, and a world full of advice. So let's turn to the teenage years, and to the quiet, powerful need to feel in charge of our own lives.
