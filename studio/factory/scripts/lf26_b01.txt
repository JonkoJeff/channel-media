What the research does say is both more modest and more hopeful: that people are far more resilient than they fear, and that there are real, tested ways in which a broken view of the world can be rebuilt.

@chapter A12 living_room_day | The assumptions we live by
Think about a normal morning. You get into a car, expecting to arrive. You say goodbye to someone, expecting to see them later. You eat food, expecting that it's safe. You don't think about any of this. It runs beneath awareness, like the foundations of a house. Psychologists call these deep, mostly unspoken beliefs our assumptive world.

@char A06 cafe_sunset | A useful phrase | The assumptive world
The term was used by the British psychiatrist Colin Murray Parkes in the 1970s, to describe the set of beliefs that each of us holds about how the world works and about ourselves. It's our internal map, so deeply taken for granted that we don't know it's there, until something tears it.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Janoff-Bulman | Social Cognition, 1989 | Assumptive worlds and the stress of traumatic events: applications of the schema construct
Ronnie Janoff-Bulman, a psychologist at the University of Massachusetts Amherst, developed the idea into a theory. She proposed that most people hold three core assumptions. That the world is benevolent. That the world is meaningful, in the sense that what happens follows some kind of order. And that the self is worthy. A traumatic event, she argued, can shatter all three at once.

@list A03 living_room_day | Three core assumptions | The world is mostly a good place ;; The world is meaningful: things happen for understandable reasons ;; I am a good, worthy person who deserves safety
Notice how ordinary these are. Most of us would say yes to all three if asked, even though we've never put them into words. And notice that they are not claims about certainty. They're a kind of background trust, that makes daily life possible.

@chapter A13 rooftop_dusk | Why we need them
Why would we hold beliefs that are, strictly speaking, optimistic? The world isn't always benevolent, and it isn't always meaningful. Janoff-Bulman argued that these assumptions are not mistakes. They are a kind of psychological shelter, and they let us act, plan and love without being paralysed by the knowledge of what could go wrong.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Taylor & Brown | Psychological Bulletin, 1988 | Illusion and well-being: a social psychological perspective on mental health
Shelley Taylor and Jonathon Brown reviewed a large body of research and argued that mentally healthy people tend to hold mildly positive illusions. They see themselves a little more favourably than is strictly accurate, they feel a little more in control, and they're a little optimistic about the future. These small illusions, they argued, are linked to wellbeing and to resilience, not to delusion.

@char A04 attic_rain | A surprising point | Healthy minds are slightly optimistic
This overturns an old assumption of its own, that mental health means seeing the world exactly as it is. The research suggests that moderately positive beliefs help people to keep going, to try, and to bounce back. The danger is not that we hold them. The danger is how hard they break when reality arrives all at once.

@list A12 living_room_day | Words to keep from this chapter | Assumptive world ;; Core assumptions ;; Positive illusions ;; Schema
Four terms to carry through the evening. The assumptive world is our set of deep beliefs about how things are. Core assumptions are the three main ones: a benevolent world, a meaningful world and a worthy self. Positive illusions are mildly optimistic beliefs that support wellbeing. And a schema is a mental framework that organises what we expect.

@list A03 living_room_day | A small reflection | What do you simply assume about the world each day? ;; What do you assume about your own safety? ;; What would you struggle to imagine being untrue? ;; Notice how quiet these beliefs are
Here is a small reflection, which you can do right now. Think of what you take for granted on an ordinary day. That the people you love will be there tomorrow. That your body will work. That if you're kind, things will mostly go well. Notice how quiet these beliefs are, and how rarely you've examined them. There's no need to be alarmed by this. It's simply the foundation that we all stand on. And understanding that it's there is the first step to understanding what happens when it shakes, and why the shaking feels so enormous. Picture this scene. A man walks home from work on an ordinary Tuesday and, a few hours later, receives a phone call that changes the shape of his life. In the weeks that follow, he finds that he cannot concentrate, and that the streets he knows feel slightly unreal. He is not weak, and he is not exaggerating. He is a person whose assumptions about safety and order have been knocked sideways, and who has to work out, slowly, what he can still count on. Naming that experience is the first step in recovering from it.

@char A15 cabin_fireplace | A gentle thought | You are not fragile for feeling shaken
If you've been through something that shook your sense of the world, it's worth knowing that this is one of the most human reactions there is. It doesn't mean that you're weak, or broken. It means that you had a world that you trusted, and that something touched it. That trust was healthy, and it can be rebuilt.

@char A08 cabin_fireplace | Looking back | What the foundations taught us
So what did we learn? That we live by deep assumptions, mostly unspoken. That Janoff-Bulman proposed three: a benevolent world, a meaningful world and a worthy self. That mildly positive beliefs seem to support wellbeing. And that these beliefs are a shelter, not a flaw.
