In 1995, the psychologist Dacher Keltner published a study in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology called Signs of Appeasement. He carefully coded the faces and movements of people in embarrassing moments, frame by frame. He found that embarrassment has its own recognisable display, distinct from amusement and distinct from shame. It isn't just a messy jumble of reactions. It unfolds in a typical order.

Here's roughly what Keltner described. First, the eyes look down. Then a smile appears, but it's a smile that tries to hide itself, with the lips pressed or pulled to the side. The head turns down and away. And quite often, a hand comes up to touch the face. You've almost certainly done every one of these, maybe in a single second, without ever deciding to.

@study A03 cabin_fireplace | Keltner and Buswell | Psychological Bulletin, 1997 | Embarrassment works like an apology
In 1997, Keltner and his colleague Brenda Buswell published a review in Psychological Bulletin that pulled the research together. Their idea was that embarrassment has an appeasement function. In plain words, it works like a small, wordless apology. It tells the people around us that we know we slipped, that we care about the rules, and that we're not a threat to the group.

And here's something even stranger. You don't even need to be the one who slipped. Have you ever watched someone on television make a painful mistake, and found yourself wincing, or looking away, or reaching for the remote? Many of us feel embarrassed for other people. Psychologists call it vicarious embarrassment, and some of them have looked at it inside the brain.

In 2011, a team led by Sören Krach in Germany published a study in the journal PLoS ONE with a memorable title. Your flaws are my pain. They showed volunteers in a brain scanner pictures and short descriptions of people in awkward, embarrassing situations. When the volunteers felt embarrassed on someone else's behalf, two regions were especially active, the anterior cingulate cortex and the anterior insula.

Those two regions come up again and again in research on empathy, including our response to other people's pain. That doesn't mean watching an awkward moment is the same as being hurt. But it does suggest that the brain handles other people's social stumbles in some of the same places where it handles feeling for them. The cringe, in other words, is partly a form of caring.
