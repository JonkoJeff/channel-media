@chapter A16 rooftop_dusk | A camera with one sharp spot
So, is your eye really like a camera? Let's start with the part of a camera everyone cares about most, how sharp the picture is.

@char A03 living_room_day | Sharp only in the middle | A tiny patch
A camera captures the whole scene in focus at once, corner to corner. Your eye does not. Right in the middle of your retina there is a small pit called the fovea, packed tightly with the cells that see fine detail and colour. Only the light that lands there is seen sharply. Everything else is seen with far less detail.

@stat A18 cabin_fireplace | Your sharp vision | ~2° | the central area seen in fine detail by the fovea
How small is that sharp area? Roughly two degrees of your field of view. A common way to picture it is to hold your arm straight out and look at your thumbnail. The patch you see in real detail is about the size of that thumbnail, give or take. Everything around it is gradually softer, until at the edges you can see movement and light, but not much detail at all.

@char A13 attic_rain | Then why does it look sharp? | The whole room seems clear
And yet, when you look around a room, it doesn't feel like a thumbnail of sharpness in a sea of blur. The whole room seems clear. That is the first sign that what you experience is not the raw signal from your eye. It's something your brain has assembled.

@bars A10 living_room_day | Light-catching cells in each eye | million cells | Rods:120 ;; Cones:6
Here's another way the eye differs from a camera sensor. A camera sensor has the same kind of pixel everywhere. Your retina has two main kinds of light catching cells. There are roughly a hundred and twenty million rods, which are very sensitive in dim light but don't see colour. And only about six million cones, which see colour and fine detail, and are crowded into that small central area.

@char A19 cabin_fireplace | Uneven by design | Not a flaw
So your eye is uneven by design. Colour and detail are concentrated in the middle, while the edges are tuned for dim light and movement. It's a clever trade off, but it means the raw signal looks nothing like a photograph.

@chapter A05 autumn_overlook | Eyes that never stop moving
So how does your brain get a sharp picture of a whole room? The answer is that your eyes never stop moving.

@char A02 living_room_day | Little jumps | Saccades
Your eyes move in quick little jumps called saccades. They dart from one point to another, pause for a fraction of a second, and then jump again. Each pause puts a new spot of the world onto your fovea, so a new patch can be seen sharply.

@stat A12 cafe_sunset | Quick eye jumps | ~3 per second | saccades during everyday looking
On average, while you're looking around or reading, your eyes make around three of these jumps every second. That's many thousands of jumps every waking day, and you are aware of almost none of them. It feels as if your gaze glides smoothly, or simply rests on the world. It doesn't. It hops.

@study A14 street_day | Alfred Yarbus | Moscow, book in English 1967 | Recorded where people's eyes jumped while they looked at paintings and faces
In the 1960s, the Russian scientist Alfred Yarbus recorded these jumps with great care, and his book, published in English in 1967, became a classic. He tracked people's eyes while they looked at paintings and faces, and drew out the paths their gaze took. The drawings looked like tangled threads, gathering around eyes, mouths and anything important.

@char A15 cafe_sunset | Looking with a purpose | The question shapes the jumps
Yarbus found something else too. When he gave people different questions about the same painting, such as how old are these people, or what were they doing before the visitor arrived, their eye movements changed. The same picture, looked at with a different purpose, was scanned in a different way. Your eyes don't just collect the world. They go looking for what you want to know.

@photo lf30_b02_01.jpg | A camera keeps still. Your eyes don't.
So already the camera comparison is wobbling. A camera stays still and captures everything at once. Your eyes jump around several times a second, collecting small, sharp samples. And your brain stitches those samples into one calm scene.

@chapter A18 cabin_fireplace | Where the blur goes
But this raises a puzzle. If your eyes are jumping all the time, why doesn't the world look like a shaky home video?

@char A04 attic_rain | Moving eyes, still world | No smearing
When you move a camera quickly, the picture smears. When your eyes jump, the world doesn't smear at all. It stays perfectly still. Something is hiding the movement from you.

@char A10 living_room_day | Saccadic suppression | A tiny dimming
That something is called saccadic suppression. During each quick jump, your brain turns down the visual signal for a moment, so the blur never reaches your awareness. Then, when your eyes land, it shows you the new view as if it had always been there.

@list A03 living_room_day | Try it in a mirror | Look at your left eye ;; Then at your right eye ;; You never see them move ;; A friend watching sees it easily
There's a lovely way to notice this. Stand close to a mirror and look at your left eye, then at your right eye, back and forth. You'll never catch your own eyes moving. A friend watching you can see the movement clearly. But you can't, because during each jump your brain is quietly looking away.

@char A19 cabin_fireplace | Tiny gaps | Stitched over
Add up all those tiny moments, and every day your brain spends a noticeable amount of time partly blind during eye movements. And you never miss a thing, or rather, you never notice what you miss, because the brain stitches the gaps over so neatly.

@chapter A12 cafe_sunset | A hole in every eye
And there's one more feature of your eye that no camera maker would ever accept.

@char A13 attic_rain | The blind spot | A hole in the picture
In each eye there is a spot with no light catching cells at all. It's where the nerve fibres from the retina gather together and leave the eye, on their way to the brain. Light that lands there is simply not seen. Every one of us has a small hole in the vision of each eye.

@study A03 living_room_day | Edme Mariotte | France, 1660s | Noticed that a small mark vanished when its image fell on the spot where the optic nerve leaves the eye
This was discovered in the 1660s by the French scientist Edme Mariotte. He was studying the anatomy of the eye and worked out where this nerve exit point must be. Then he tested it. He placed small marks on a wall, closed one eye, looked at one mark, and found that the other one disappeared completely at a certain distance.

#ai lf30_b02_02.jpg | Editorial still-life of a plain white card with two small black dots drawn on it, propped against a stack of old books on a wooden desk, soft warm lamplight, calm cosy mood, no people, no text, no logos

@photo lf30_b02_02.jpg | Two dots, one disappears
You can try a version of this at home sometime. Draw a small dot and a small cross on a card, a few centimetres apart. Close your left eye, look at the cross with your right eye, and slowly move the card closer. At a certain distance, the dot vanishes. It's not hidden. It's simply falling into the hole.

@char A04 bedroom_night | So why no hole? | Not a dark patch
But here's the strange part. When you look around normally, even with one eye closed, you don't see a hole. There's no black patch floating in your view. Instead, the blind spot is filled with whatever surrounds it. If it falls on a patterned carpet, you see carpet. If it falls on a blue sky, you see blue.

@char A18 cabin_fireplace | Filling in | Painting the gap
Your brain fills in the gap, using the colours and patterns around it. It doesn't leave a hole and it doesn't tell you it's guessing. It just paints a reasonable continuation of the scene into the space where no information arrives. It's a small, everyday example of your brain showing you what is probably there, rather than what your eye actually sends.

@list A12 cafe_sunset | Your eye is not a camera | Sharp only in a tiny patch ;; Jumps about three times a second ;; Hides its own movements ;; Has a hole that gets filled in
So let's gather it up. Your eye is sharp only in a tiny central patch. It jumps around a few times a second. It hides its own movements from you. And it has a hole in it that your brain quietly paints over. The calm, sharp, complete room you see is not what your eye records. It's what your brain makes of it.

@char A05 autumn_overlook | A deeper question | When does it happen?
That leads us gently to the next belief, and it's one of the strangest of the night. If your brain has to collect, stitch and fill in all of this, that takes time. And if it takes time, then the world you see right now may not quite be the world as it is right now.
