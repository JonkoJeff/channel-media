@chapter A10 attic_rain | A very old observation
So let's begin a long way back. In 1890, an American psychologist and philosopher named William James published a famous book called The Principles of Psychology. It had a whole chapter on habit. This was long before brain scanners, and long before anyone could measure what happens inside a single cell. James simply watched people, and himself, very closely, and then wrote down what he noticed, in clear and often rather beautiful prose.

@quote A19 rooftop_dusk | Habit is thus the enormous fly-wheel of society. | William James, 1890
That's one of the best-known sentences from that chapter. A fly-wheel is a heavy wheel that keeps a machine turning steadily, even when the push behind it is uneven. And James meant that habit does something similar for us all. It keeps daily life turning smoothly, without anyone having to push it again every single morning. Most of what holds a person's day together, he suggested, is not fresh decision at all. It's the steady turning of something already in motion.

@char A15 cabin_fireplace | What a habit can feel like | Strong, and also gentle
Habits also have a very particular feeling, if you pay attention. They tend to be strong, but they rarely announce themselves. You don't feel a loud push toward the tin. You feel nothing much at all, and then you're standing there with the lid in your hand. That's part of why they can seem so mysterious. There's no moment of decision to point at afterward, and so we're left wondering who exactly was in charge. We'll take that question slowly, and with a little humor, because it deserves it.

@char A13 cabin_fireplace | The quiet helper | Why we have habits at all
James also noticed something kind about all this. If every small action needed careful thought, there would be very little room left for anything else. Think about how much you do in a morning without thinking about it. Where you put your keys. Which hand holds the toothbrush. Which way you turn at the end of the street. Habit takes these small jobs off our hands, and that leaves our attention free for the things that truly matter to us.

@list A15 cafe_sunset | Things we do without deciding | Turning on a light ;; Making tea or coffee ;; Locking the door ;; Taking a familiar route ;; Reaching for the phone
It's a good idea to look around, now and then, for just how many of those there are. Turning on a light. Making your tea or coffee in your own particular way. Locking the door behind you. Taking a familiar route to a familiar place. Reaching for your phone at certain moments. None of these were ever really chosen today. They were chosen a long time ago, or perhaps never chosen at all, and then they simply stayed.

@char A12 autumn_overlook | The other side | When a helper becomes a nuisance
But there's another side to this, and James knew it too. The same machinery that takes the effort out of making tea also takes the effort out of scrolling at midnight. A habit doesn't know whether it's good for us. It simply repeats what has been repeated before. So the helper that quietly carries our mornings can also quietly carry the late nights, and the biscuit tin at four o'clock, without ever asking our opinion.
