@chapter N27 rooftop_dusk | Two clocks and one gap
Let's start with what a body actually needs in order to fall asleep. It turns out there isn't one switch, but two, and they don't always agree.

@char N20 living_room_day | A tale of two processes | An idea from 1982
In 1982, a Swiss sleep researcher named Alexander Borbély published a model that is still the backbone of how scientists think about sleep. It's usually called the two-process model. It says that your sleep is governed by two separate systems working together, one that counts how long you've been awake, and one that tracks the time of day.

@char N10 cafe_sunset | Process one | Sleep pressure
The first system is called sleep pressure, or the sleep homeostat. Think of it like a bucket that fills up slowly while you're awake. The longer you stay up, the fuller it gets, and the stronger the pull toward sleep becomes. During sleep, the bucket drains again, and by morning it should be nearly empty.

@char N02 living_room_day | A familiar feeling | The heavy-eyed afternoon
You know the feeling when the bucket is nearly full. After a long day, or after a night of too little sleep, your eyelids feel heavy, your thoughts slow down, and you could fall asleep sitting on a bus. That is sleep pressure at work, and on its own it would make falling asleep easy.

@char N13 bedroom_night | Process two | The body clock
But there's a second system, and it doesn't care how long you've been awake. It's your circadian clock, a roughly twenty-four-hour rhythm driven by a tiny cluster of cells deep in the brain. It raises your alertness at certain times of day and lowers it at others, almost like a tide, regardless of how tired you happen to be.

@char N09 cabin_fireplace | When they disagree | The gap
Now imagine the two systems pulling in opposite directions. Sleep pressure is high, but the body clock is saying it's the middle of the day. That's what happens when you try to nap at four in the afternoon, or when you cross several time zones and your body clock is still on yesterday's schedule. You're tired, and you can't sleep.

@char N23 rooftop_dusk | The quiet evening zone | An alert window
Researchers have even described a stretch in the late evening, a few hours before your usual bedtime, when the body clock pushes alertness up to its daily peak, just as sleep pressure is also high. It's sometimes called the wake maintenance zone, and some scientists think it exists to keep us from falling asleep too early in the evening, and then waking far too soon.
