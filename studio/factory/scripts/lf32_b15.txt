@chapter A05 autumn_overlook | Flow, and meaning
Have you ever looked up from something and realised hours have gone by? You were drawing, or cooking, or fixing something, or playing music, and time just folded away. No one had to push you. You didn't need a reward. The doing was enough.

@char A15 cafe_sunset | A different kind of motivation | When effort feels easy
That state is one of the most pleasant things a human being can feel. And in the 1970s, one psychologist decided to study it seriously, by asking the people who seemed to feel it most.

@study A12 living_room_day | Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi | Beyond Boredom and Anxiety, 1975 | Interviewed rock climbers, chess players, dancers, surgeons and others about moments of deep absorption in what they did
His name was Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi, a Hungarian-American psychologist. He interviewed rock climbers, chess players, dancers, composers and surgeons, people who spent long hours on hard things, often with little outside reward. He asked them what it felt like when it was going well.

@char A11 rooftop_dusk | The same words | Like being carried
Again and again, very different people described the same thing. A feeling of being carried along, like a current. Several of them used the same word for it. Flow. And so that became the name.

@list A12 cafe_sunset | What flow tends to need | Clear goals ;; Quick feedback ;; A challenge that matches your skill ;; Full attention
Csikszentmihalyi found that flow tends to show up under certain conditions. A clear goal, so you know what you're trying to do. Quick feedback, so you can tell how it's going. And a challenge that roughly matches your skill. Not so easy that you're bored. Not so hard that you're anxious.

@char A03 living_room_day | The narrow channel | Between boredom and worry
He pictured it as a channel. On one side, boredom, when your skill is far greater than the task. On the other side, anxiety, when the task is far beyond your skill. Flow lives in the narrow space between them. And as your skill grows, the challenge has to grow too, or you drift back into boredom.

@char A01 autumn_overlook | What it feels like | Time bends
Inside that channel, people described losing track of time, forgetting themselves for a while, and feeling that the activity was worth doing just for its own sake. It's the purest example of intrinsic motivation, the kind we met earlier tonight with the puzzles and the drawings.

@char A13 living_room_day | A word for it | Autotelic
Csikszentmihalyi had a word for activities like this, and for people who seemed to find them easily. Autotelic. It comes from two Greek words, auto, meaning self, and telos, meaning goal. An autotelic activity is its own goal. You do it for the doing.

@char A15 cafe_sunset | Inviting it in | You can't force flow
You can't force flow to happen. But you can make it more likely. Give a task a clear aim. Find a way to see how it's going. Nudge the difficulty up when you're bored, or down when you're anxious. And put away the things that pull your attention elsewhere, at least for a while.

@char A04 attic_rain | A puzzle | Where does it happen most?
You might expect flow to happen mostly in free time. On holiday. In hobbies. But when researchers actually checked, the answer was surprising.

@study A13 rooftop_dusk | Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi & Judith LeFevre | Published in 1989 | Paged working adults at random moments and found flow was reported more often at work than in leisure, yet people still wished to be elsewhere more often at work
In 1989, Csikszentmihalyi and Judith LeFevre gave working adults pagers that beeped at random moments through the week. Each time it beeped, people wrote down what they were doing and how they felt. It's a method called experience sampling, and it catches life as it happens, rather than as we remember it.

@char A06 street_day | The result | The paradox of work
People reported flow-like moments more often at work than in their free time. In their leisure, they were often watching television, feeling rather flat. And yet, at work, they more often said they wished they were somewhere else. The researchers called it the paradox of work.

@char A19 cabin_fireplace | What it suggests | Labels can hide feelings
It suggests something quietly important. The label we put on an activity, work or play, can hide how it actually feels while we're doing it. Some of our best moments may come from the very things we say we'd rather avoid.

@char A10 living_room_day | Beyond flow | Why does it matter?
Flow is about how an activity feels. But there's a deeper question, about whether it means anything. And for that, we have to go somewhere much darker, and then come back into the light.

@study A13 cabin_fireplace | Viktor Frankl | Man's Search for Meaning, 1946 | A psychiatrist who survived Nazi concentration camps and argued that the search for meaning is a central human motive
Viktor Frankl was a psychiatrist from Vienna. During the Second World War, he was imprisoned in Nazi concentration camps. After he survived, he wrote a short book about what he saw there. He argued that a sense of meaning, a reason to go on, was one of the most powerful forces in a human life.

@quote A10 cabin_fireplace | He who has a why to live for can bear almost any how. | Friedrich Nietzsche, quoted by Viktor Frankl
In the book, he quotes the philosopher Friedrich Nietzsche. He who has a why to live for can bear almost any how. Frankl's book is a personal account, not a controlled study. But many later researchers have taken his idea seriously.

@char A15 living_room_day | Meaning at work | The same job, three ways
One of them looked at something quite ordinary. Not survival, but daily work. And the question was simple. Do people doing the very same job experience it in the same way?

@study A05 autumn_overlook | Amy Wrzesniewski and colleagues | Published in 1997 | Found that people saw their work as a job, a career, or a calling, and that this view was not simply set by the type of work
In 1997, the organisational psychologist Amy Wrzesniewski and her colleagues asked people how they saw their work. Some saw it as a job, something you do to get by. Some saw it as a career, a ladder to climb. And some saw it as a calling, something worth doing in itself.

@char A01 cafe_sunset | The result | It wasn't about the title
Here's the interesting part. Even among people with the same kind of job, the researchers found all three views. And the people who saw their work as a calling tended to report more satisfaction with their work, and with their lives. The meaning wasn't printed on the job. People brought it with them.

@char A04 attic_rain | And when meaning is taken away? | A small experiment
Now, if meaning helps, what happens when it's taken away, on purpose? A clever little experiment with toy building kits tried to find out.

#ai lf32_b15_01.jpg | Editorial still-life of small colourful plastic building bricks scattered beside a half-built toy figure on a wooden desk, soft lamp light, calm quiet mood, no people, no text, no logos

@photo lf32_b15_01.jpg | Small figures, built one by one
In 2008, Dan Ariely, Emir Kamenica and Dražen Prelec asked volunteers to build small toy figures from a building kit, one after another, for a small reward that shrank with each new figure. People could stop whenever they liked.

@study A11 rooftop_dusk | Dan Ariely, Emir Kamenica & Dražen Prelec | Published in 2008 | Found that people built fewer toy figures when each finished figure was taken apart in front of them
In one condition, each finished figure was set aside on the desk, adding to a little row. In the other, the experimenter took each figure apart in front of the builder, while they started the next one, and handed the same pieces back. The researchers called that the Sisyphus condition, after the figure in Greek myth who pushes a rock uphill forever.

@char A08 bedroom_night | The result | Undone work drains us
The task was the same. The effort was the same. But people whose figures were taken apart built noticeably fewer before they stopped. Seeing the work undone, right in front of them, drained the motivation out of it.

@char A04 attic_rain | A second twist | Love wasn't enough
There was a second twist. Some volunteers simply loved building things like this, and in the condition where their figures were kept, those people built more. But in the condition where every figure was taken apart, their love for the task seemed to stop mattering. Even the keenest builders gave up sooner.

@char A20 cafe_sunset | The lesson | Seeing that it counts
It's a small study, but the message feels true. People want to see that what they do adds up to something. Even a tiny row of toy figures on a desk can be enough. And that's something you can give yourself, by keeping your finished work where you can see it.
