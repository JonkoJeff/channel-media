@chapter A03 autumn_overlook | A word for the feeling
Now let's try the second small idea. Earlier, we met a study where simply choosing a word for an emotion, angry or scared, quietened the brain's alarm a little. We can borrow that idea, softly, for the night.

@char A10 cabin_fireplace | Checking in | What's here?
Take a moment to notice how you feel right now. Not why you feel it, and not whether you should. Just what's here. It might be tiredness. It might be a little worry about tomorrow. It might be calm. It might be a mixture of things, which is perfectly normal.

@char A13 living_room_day | One word | Keep it simple
Now see if you can find one word for it. Just one. Tired. Restless. Worried. Calm. Sad. Content. The word doesn't need to be perfect. It's only a label, like the name on a jar, so you know what's inside.

@char A19 bedroom_night | Say it gently | No judgement
Let your inner voice say that word once, gently. Not as a complaint, and not as a judgement. Just as a fact. This is tiredness. This is worry. This is restlessness. As if you were a kind friend noticing what someone else was feeling, and saying it out loud so they felt seen.

@quote A12 cafe_sunset | When you put feelings into words, you seem to be hitting the brakes on your emotional responses. | Matthew Lieberman, UCLA, 2007
Remember Lieberman's image of the brakes. Naming a feeling doesn't make it vanish, and that's not the goal. It just seems to slow it a little, the way a gentle touch on the brakes slows a car on a long hill. The feeling is still here. It's just a little less in charge.

@chapter A12 cabin_fireplace | Names, not stories
There's one small trick to this, and it's the difference between a name and a story.

@char A18 attic_rain | A story | The voice runs on
A story sounds like this. I'm worried, because tomorrow is going to be awful, and I always mess these things up, and then everyone will think. That's not a label anymore. That's the inner voice getting back on its old track, going round and round.

@char A10 cabin_fireplace | A name | Then stop
A name sounds like this. Worry. And then nothing else. You let the word sit there, and you don't add anything to it. If a story starts, you can come back to the name. Worry. Just that. Like putting a lid back on a pot, gently, without slamming it.

@list A03 living_room_day | Names, not stories | Notice the feeling ;; Give it one word ;; Let the word sit ;; If a story starts, return to the word
So the steps are simple. Notice the feeling. Give it one word. Let the word sit, without adding to it. And if a story starts, return to the word. You can do this as many times as you need. There's no limit, and there's no failing.

@chapter A05 autumn_overlook | Feelings change
There's something else worth noticing, once you've named a feeling. Feelings don't stay still.

@char A12 cafe_sunset | Weather, not climate | It moves on
A feeling at night can seem as though it will last for ever. But feelings are more like weather than climate. They come in, they change, and they move on. The worry that feels huge at midnight often looks smaller with morning light. You don't have to make it move. It will move on its own.

@char A13 cabin_fireplace | Check again | What's here now?
Let's check in again. What's here now? Maybe the same feeling, maybe a slightly different one. Find one word for it. Heavy. Drowsy. Quiet. Still worried, perhaps, and that's fine too. Just the name, and nothing more.

@char A19 bedroom_night | Softer each time | Lighter words
You might notice that the words get softer as you go. Restless becomes settled. Worried becomes tired. Tired becomes sleepy. You're not forcing anything. You're just noticing what's already happening, as your body slowly gets ready for sleep.

@chapter A12 cabin_fireplace | The body has feelings too
Feelings don't only live in the mind. They live in the body too. So let's spend a little time there.

@char A18 attic_rain | Where is it? | A gentle search
If there's a feeling that's still strong, worry or restlessness, see if you can notice where it lives in your body. Perhaps a tightness in the chest. Perhaps a flutter in the stomach. Perhaps tension in the jaw or shoulders. You don't need to change it. Just find it, the way you'd find a light switch in the dark.

@char A10 cabin_fireplace | Name the place | Chest, shoulders, jaw
Now name that place too. Chest. Or, shoulders. Or, jaw. And then, if you like, add the feeling's word. Tight. Warm. Fluttery. Chest, tight. Shoulders, heavy. Just two words, side by side, like a note left for yourself.

@char A13 bedroom_night | Breathe toward it | Room to soften
Let your next breath travel, in your imagination, toward that place. Not to push the feeling away, but to give it a little room. As you breathe out, imagine the area softening, just slightly, the way ice softens at the edges when the sun touches it.

@list A03 living_room_day | A two-word check | Place ;; Feeling ;; Breath ;; Room
That's all there is to it. Find the place. Name the feeling. Send a breath toward it. Give it a little room. You can come back to this whenever a feeling feels too big, tonight or any night.
