@chapter A15 street_day | Men, women and the weight of culture
The couple with the umbrella pass Hugo with a friendly nod. He nods back, and walks on past a lock, where the water drops a level with a low, steady rushing sound. His father never cried, as far as Hugo knows. Not once, not even at his own mother's funeral. His mother cried at the news, at weddings and at sad songs on the radio. Was that just how men and women were made?

@char A03 cafe_sunset | The belief | Women cry more because of biology
Our eighth belief is that women cry more than men, and that the reason is simply biology. Men are built to hold back, the story goes, and women are built to let go. It's a belief that's been used both to excuse men's silence and to dismiss women's tears. Let's look at the two halves separately. First, do women really cry more? And second, is it really all biology?

@study N28 rooftop_dusk | William Frey | Reported in the 1980s | How often men and women cry
The first half has been studied for decades. In the 1980s, William Frey reported that women cried on average about five point three times a month, and men about one point three times a month. Crying was counted generously, from moist eyes to full sobbing. Newer research, including Lauren Bylsma's diary work, suggests these averages are about the same today.

@list N20 living_room_day | Average crying episodes per month, as reported by Frey | Women: about 5.3 ;; Men: about 1.3
Put side by side, those figures mean that women, on average, cried roughly four times as often as men. Vingerhoets has also described women as crying about four times more than men. So the first half of the belief holds up well. On average, women do cry more often. The more interesting question is why.

@study N07 street_day | Dianne van Hemert, Fons van de Vijver and Ad Vingerhoets | Cross-Cultural Research, published 2011 | Crying in 37 countries
To find out how universal the difference is, Dianne van Hemert, Fons van de Vijver and Ad Vingerhoets analysed data from thirty-seven countries across Africa, Europe, Asia, Oceania and the Americas. Five thousand seven hundred and fifteen people took part, aged sixteen to seventy-four. They were asked how easily they tend to cry, and how long ago they last cried.

@stat A09 attic_rain | Within-country comparisons showing women crying more | 71 of 74 | and the remaining three pointed the same way
Women reported a greater tendency to cry, and had cried more recently, than men. Each country contributed two comparisons, seventy-four in total, and seventy-one of them showed a statistically clear difference in the same direction. The other three still leaned the same way. So women crying more than men appears in a remarkably wide range of cultures.

@char A13 cabin_fireplace | But the gap varies | And that's revealing
Here's the twist. While the difference appeared almost everywhere, its size varied a great deal. According to the American Psychological Association's magazine, the gap between men and women was larger in countries with greater freedom of expression, such as Chile, Sweden and the United States. In countries such as Ghana, Nigeria and Nepal, women reported only slightly more crying than men.

@char A03 autumn_overlook | What that suggests | Culture shapes the gap
If the difference between men and women were purely biological, we might expect it to be roughly the same size everywhere. Instead it grows and shrinks from one country to another. Van Hemert has suggested that in some countries people may cry more simply because their cultures permit it. Where expressing emotion is more accepted, women in particular seem to take up that freedom.

A canal lock at night, its heavy wooden gates closed and water spilling gently over the top, lit by a single lamp. A lock controls how much water flows and when. Culture may work a little like that with tears. The feeling may be there in everyone, but the gates are opened and closed by what each society allows.

@list A20 cafe_sunset | Tears through history, as Vingerhoets describes | Medieval Europe: public weeping was common ;; Age of sentimentalism: famous writers wept openly ;; Victorian era: tears seen as womanly weakness ;; Today: athletes often cry in public
History tells the same story. Vingerhoets has pointed out that in medieval Europe, public weeping was normal among ordinary people. During the age of sentimentalism, writers such as Voltaire and Rousseau cried openly. The Victorian era, by contrast, came to see crying as a womanly weakness. And today, athletes commonly cry in public after winning or losing.

@char A08 bedroom_night | Biology still plays a part | Hormones and genes
None of that means biology plays no part. Researchers have suggested that the hormone testosterone may inhibit crying, and that prolactin, which tends to be higher in women, may promote it. These are possibilities, not settled facts. Vingerhoets has also described twin studies suggesting that a person's general proneness to cry is more strongly shaped by genes than how often they actually cry, which depends heavily on their circumstances.

@char A10 living_room_day | Different outlets | Tears or swearing
Vingerhoets has noticed another pattern. Women often cry when frustrated or angry in situations where they feel they can't express that anger openly. Men, in the same kinds of situations, are more likely to swear. It's a small observation, but a telling one. The feeling might be similar. What differs is the outlet that each person has learned to use.

@list A04 attic_rain | Attachment and tears, from a 2012 Tilburg study | Dismissive attachment: cried less ;; Preoccupied attachment: cried more ;; Women cried more in every style
Personal history matters too. A 2012 study from Tilburg University looked at attachment styles, the patterns of closeness we form with others. People with a dismissive style, who keep others at arm's length, cried less and tried harder to hold back tears. People with a preoccupied style, who worry about closeness, cried more. And within every style, women still cried more than men.

@char A05 rooftop_dusk | The verdict | Half true
So what's the verdict on the eighth belief? Half true. Women do cry more often than men, on average, almost everywhere that's been studied. But the size of the gap depends heavily on culture, history and upbringing, and biology seems to be only part of the story. Men's tears haven't vanished. In many places, they've simply been taught to stay behind the gates.

@char A09 cabin_fireplace | Hugo and his father | A man who never cried
Hugo thinks about his father again, that silent man at the graveside. Perhaps he felt everything and had simply learned, very young, to keep the lock gates shut. Hugo feels a sudden tenderness for him. And he realises that his own tears in the hall weren't a sign of becoming softer with age. Perhaps the gates were simply opening, at last.

@char A13 autumn_overlook | Tears without sadness | The next stop
But there's still the strangest part of his evening to explain. Hugo wasn't sad in that hall. He was proud and moved and full of something warm. So why tears? Ahead, the canal path passes an old community hall with its door open and music drifting out, a rehearsal of some kind. It seems a good place to think about tears that come from joy.
