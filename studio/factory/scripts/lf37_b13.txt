@chapter A18 rooftop_dusk | Set like plaster?
There's a sentence, written more than a century ago, that many people still half believe. It comes from William James, one of the founders of modern psychology. In his great book, The Principles of Psychology, published in 1890, he wrote about how our habits and character settle over time. And he put it in a way that's hard to forget.

@quote A19 cabin_fireplace | In most of us, by the age of thirty, the character has set like plaster, and will never soften again. | William James, 1890
In most of us, by the age of thirty, the character has set like plaster, and will never soften again. It's a beautiful sentence, and a slightly sad one. It suggests that our twenties are the last chance. That somewhere around thirty, the wet plaster hardens, and whoever we are by then is who we'll stay. Forever, more or less.

@char A13 living_room_day | Myth number 9 | Fixed by thirty
So here's Myth number nine, stated plainly. By thirty, your personality is set, and it won't really change again. Many people carry a quiet version of this belief. You might hear it as, people don't change, or, that's just who I am. It can feel comforting, in a way. But it can also feel like a door quietly closing behind you.

@char A05 cafe_sunset | Nora counts | Am I already set?
Nora reads the quote and does a quick sum in her head, the way almost everyone does. How close is she to thirty? Is the plaster already setting around the planner, the worrier, the friend who asks the second question? It's a strange thing to wonder at one in the morning. But it's a very human thing to wonder, at any age.

@char A10 attic_rain | What the research actually says | Following people for years
So what does the research actually say? To test an idea like this, you can't just measure people once. You need to measure the same people again and again, years apart, and see how much their traits hold steady. Fortunately, many studies have done exactly that, following people from childhood all the way into older age. Their answer is quietly hopeful.

@study A12 cafe_sunset | Brent Roberts and Wendy DelVecchio | Psychological Bulletin, a meta-analysis | published in 2000
In 2000, Brent Roberts and Wendy DelVecchio brought together a large number of these long-term studies in one analysis, published in the Psychological Bulletin. They focused on something called rank-order consistency. It sounds technical, but the idea is simple, and rather lovely once you see it. Let me explain it with a simple picture, the kind you can hold in your mind.

@char A15 autumn_overlook | Rank order | A line of people
Imagine a line of people standing in order, from the most talkative to the least. Years later, you line them up again. Rank-order consistency asks how much that order has stayed the same. Does the most talkative person still stand near the front? Has the quietest person stayed near the back? Or has everyone shuffled around? That's the question, asked over and over, across many years.

@char A18 living_room_day | Growing steadier | With age
What Roberts and DelVecchio found was a gentle, gradual pattern. In childhood, the order shuffles around quite a lot. Through young adulthood, it settles more and more. And it keeps growing steadier with age, levelling off later in life. So James was partly right. Personality does become more stable as we grow older. But that isn't the whole story.

@char A03 cabin_fireplace | But never complete | The plaster stays soft
But here's the important part. The consistency never became complete. Not at thirty, and not later either. The line of people stayed similar, but never perfectly the same. People kept on shuffling, just a little. Which means the plaster never fully sets. It thickens, it holds its shape more and more, but it always stays a little soft. There's always room.

@char A20 rooftop_dusk | It settles late | Long after thirty
And notice when the steadiness levels off. Not at thirty, as James suggested, but considerably later in life. Thirty isn't the end of the story. It's somewhere in the middle of a long, slow settling. A great deal can still happen after it, and for many people, a great deal does. A thirtieth birthday is not a deadline. It's just another evening.
