@chapter A03 living_room_day | Goals: aiming the arrow
Most of us, at some point, have sat down and set a goal. Get fit. Learn a language. Finish the thing. And most of us have watched some of those goals quietly fade. So what does the research say about goals that work, and goals that don't?

@char A13 cafe_sunset | Two kinds of goal | Do your best, or something specific
Here's a simple question. If you want someone to do well, should you tell them to do their best? It sounds kind and sensible. But for decades, two researchers collected evidence that it's often not the most helpful thing to say.

@study A12 autumn_overlook | Edwin Locke & Gary Latham | Summarised in 2002 after about 35 years of research | Found that specific, difficult goals usually lead to better performance than vague goals like do your best
Edwin Locke and Gary Latham spent around thirty-five years studying goal setting, in labs and in workplaces. In 2002 they summarised what they had found. Across many studies, specific and challenging goals tended to lead to better performance than easy goals, or than simply being told to do your best.

@list A10 living_room_day | Why specific goals help | They point attention ;; They call up effort ;; They keep you going ;; They push you to find a strategy
Their explanation was practical. A clear goal points your attention at what matters. It calls up effort to match the difficulty. It helps you keep going for longer. And it nudges you to look for better ways of doing the task.

@char A19 rooftop_dusk | The conditions | Not any goal, anywhere
But they also listed the conditions. Goals worked best when people were committed to them, when they got feedback on how they were doing, and when they had the skills to reach them. A hard goal on a task you don't yet understand can lead to frantic effort in the wrong direction.

@char A15 cafe_sunset | A glimpse of yourself | Read more, or twenty pages
Think of the difference between I want to read more, and I'll read twenty pages before bed. The first is a wish. The second tells you exactly what to do tonight, and lets you know, without doubt, whether you did it.

@chapter A04 attic_rain | When goals go wild
Of course, anything powerful can be misused. In 2009, a group of researchers wrote a warning about goal setting, with a memorable title.

@study A18 rooftop_dusk | Lisa Ordonez, Maurice Schweitzer, Adam Galinsky & Max Bazerman | Published in 2009 | Argued that goal setting has side effects that are often overlooked
The paper was called Goals Gone Wild, by Lisa Ordonez, Maurice Schweitzer, Adam Galinsky and Max Bazerman. They argued that goal setting had been prescribed too freely, without warnings about its side effects, the way a strong medicine might be.

@list A04 neon_alley_rain | Side effects they described | Tunnel vision on the goal ;; Other important things neglected ;; More risky choices ;; Less concern about rules
They described several side effects. Narrow goals can create tunnel vision, so that people focus on the number and neglect everything that isn't measured. Goals can encourage riskier choices. And they can make people more willing to bend the rules to hit a target.

@char A13 living_room_day | The debate | A useful caution
Locke and Latham replied that these problems came from badly designed goals, not from goals as such. The debate is still a useful caution. A goal is a spotlight. It makes some things bright, and leaves other things in the dark.

@chapter A10 cabin_fireplace | The trouble with dreaming
Now for one of the most surprising findings in this whole area. It concerns something we're often told to do. Picture your success.

@study A05 autumn_overlook | Gabriele Oettingen and colleagues | Research over more than two decades | Studied how positive fantasies about the future relate to effort and achievement
The psychologist Gabriele Oettingen has spent many years studying what happens when people imagine their goals coming true. Across a number of studies, she and her colleagues found something unexpected. The people who indulged in the most glowing fantasies about the future often did not put in more effort, and sometimes did less.

@study A12 rooftop_dusk | Heather Kappes & Gabriele Oettingen | Published in 2011 | Measured signs of energy before and after people imagined an idealised future
In 2011, Heather Kappes and Gabriele Oettingen looked at this more directly. They asked people to imagine an idealised future, and measured signs of physical energy, such as blood pressure. After a positive fantasy, people showed signs of lower energy, and in some studies, they went on to accomplish less.

@char A19 attic_rain | Why would that happen? | Feeling like you've arrived
One possible explanation is that a vivid, happy fantasy lets you taste the reward early. Your mind gets a little of the feeling of having arrived, without the journey. And when it already feels as if you're there, the urge to set off can quietly fade.

@photo lf32_b08_01.jpg | A plan on paper
Picture an open notebook on a desk, a pencil resting on the page, and a cup of tea still warm beside it. Not a dream board. Just a quiet place to think about what will actually happen tomorrow.

@char A15 cafe_sunset | The better way | Dream, then look at the obstacle
But Oettingen didn't conclude that dreaming is bad. She found that it works much better when it's paired with something else. You imagine the wish, and you imagine the good outcome. Then you look, honestly, at what inside you is most likely to get in the way.

@list A03 living_room_day | Four small steps | Wish ;; Outcome ;; Obstacle ;; Plan
She called the method mental contrasting, and later gave a simple version of it a name that's easy to remember. WOOP. Wish, Outcome, Obstacle, Plan. You name what you want, picture the best outcome, find the main obstacle, and decide what you'll do when it shows up.

@char A10 living_room_day | What an obstacle looks like | Usually, it's inside
Oettingen suggests looking for the obstacle inside you, rather than in the world. Not the weather, or a busy week, but the tiredness that arrives after dinner, the habit of reaching for the phone, the worry that you'll do it badly. Once you can name it, you can plan for the moment it appears.

@chapter A20 living_room_day | If this, then that
That last step, the plan, has a body of research of its own. It's one of the simplest tools in psychology, and one of the best studied.

@study A01 street_day | Peter Gollwitzer | Published in American Psychologist, 1999 | Described implementation intentions: simple if-then plans that link a situation to an action
In 1999, the psychologist Peter Gollwitzer described what he called implementation intentions. Instead of only deciding what you want to do, you decide exactly when and where you'll do it, in an if-then form. If it's seven o'clock after dinner, then I'll go for a walk around the block.

@char A13 cabin_fireplace | An early test | A report over the holidays
One early example came from Gollwitzer's own work with Veronika Brandstatter. Students were asked to write a short report about how they spent Christmas Eve, and to send it in within two days. Some of them were also asked to decide exactly when and where they would write it. Far more of the students who had made that plan actually sent the report in on time.

@char A16 street_day | Why it works | Handing the decision to the moment
The idea is that the plan links a situation to an action in advance. When the situation arrives, you don't have to decide again. The moment itself becomes the cue. Part of the work of starting is handed over to the situation, so it relies a little less on willpower in the moment.

@study A05 cafe_sunset | Peter Gollwitzer & Paschal Sheeran | Published in 2006 | Combined 94 tests of implementation intentions
In 2006, Peter Gollwitzer and Paschal Sheeran combined the results of 94 tests of if-then plans. They found a medium to large effect on reaching goals, across areas as different as health, studying, and the environment. Not magic, and not for every goal, but a solid and repeated effect.

@char A02 autumn_overlook | A glimpse of yourself | Your own if-then
You might try one tonight, if you like. Pick something small you keep meaning to do. Then attach it to a moment you know will happen. If I put the kettle on in the morning, then I'll stretch while it boils. Small, specific, and tied to a cue.

@char A19 rooftop_dusk | The next question | The pull of the unfinished
So, goals point our effort, dreams work better with a look at the obstacles, and plans can hand the starting over to the moment. But once you've started, something else begins to pull on you. The feeling of progress, and the strange grip of the unfinished. That's where we're going next.
