@chapter A13 bedroom_night | The night shift
Here's a thought that might be rather comforting. While you lie there, perhaps nearly asleep, doing nothing at all, there is a quiet team at work in your head. They aren't dreaming up anything dramatic. They're tidying, sorting, and filing the day.

@char A10 living_room_day | An old suspicion | Sleep and memory
People have suspected for a very long time that sleep helps memory. But suspecting something and showing it are different things. The first careful experiment on the question was done a hundred years ago, in a laboratory at Cornell University.

@study A19 cabin_fireplace | John Jenkins & Karl Dallenbach | Published in 1924 | Tested memory for nonsense syllables after hours spent asleep or awake
In 1924, John Jenkins and Karl Dallenbach had two volunteers learn lists of nonsense syllables. Then they tested them after one, two, four or eight hours. Sometimes the volunteers slept through the interval, and sometimes they stayed awake and went about their day. Memory was clearly better after sleep.

@char A03 rooftop_dusk | The surprise | Not simply tiredness
The researchers drew a thoughtful conclusion. Forgetting, they wrote, seems to be less a matter of old impressions simply fading with the clock, and more a matter of new experience getting in the way. A sleeping mind meets less new experience, and that may be a gentler way to spend the hours.

@chapter A12 autumn_overlook | A replay in the dark
So what is happening in there? To answer that, scientists had to listen to individual brain cells.

@study A02 cafe_sunset | Matthew Wilson & Bruce McNaughton | Published in Science, 1994 | Recorded from many hippocampal cells in rats while they ran along a track, and again while they slept
In 1994, Matthew Wilson and Bruce McNaughton recorded the activity of many individual hippocampal cells in rats. While a rat ran around a track, certain cells fired when it was in certain places, so the pattern of cells traced the journey. Then the rat slept. And the cells that had fired together during the run tended to fire together again in sleep.

@char A15 living_room_day | Rehearsing the route | Faster than real life
Later work showed that the replay can happen at a faster pace than the original experience, as if the day were being fast-forwarded. In 2007, Daoyun Ji and Matthew Wilson showed that this replay was coordinated between the hippocampus and the visual cortex, which hints at a conversation between the quick learner and the slower storage areas.

@list A05 cabin_fireplace | What the night shift seems to do | Replays the day's patterns ;; Strengthens the important links ;; Moves the memory toward the cortex ;; Clears some room for tomorrow
A common view, set out by Susanne Diekelmann and Jan Born in 2010, is that sleep supports consolidation. Patterns from the day are replayed. The links that matter are strengthened. The memory leans toward the cortex. And in one popular idea, called synaptic homeostasis, sleep also turns down the noise, making room for tomorrow.

@char A03 bedroom_night | Gentle caution | Still being argued
That last idea has supporters and critics, and the details are still being worked out. And while the broad picture, that sleep helps memory, has a lot of support, scientists continue to argue about exactly which stage of sleep does which job. It's a lively research area, which is a good reason to be modest about it.

@chapter A20 rooftop_dusk | A scent in the night
Here's one of the strangest experiments in this field, and it comes with a lovely smell.

@study A01 living_room_day | Björn Rasch, Christian Büchel, Steffen Gais & Jan Born | Published in Science, 2007 | Used a rose scent while people learned, and again during deep sleep
In 2007, Björn Rasch and colleagues asked volunteers to learn where cards were hidden on a grid, while a faint rose scent was in the room. Then, during deep sleep, some of the volunteers were given the same scent again. When they woke, those who had smelled the rose in their sleep remembered the card positions better than those who had not.

@char A12 cafe_sunset | A cue, not a lesson | What it does not show
It's important to be clear about what this does and doesn't show. The scent didn't teach anyone anything new. They had learned the cards while awake. It seems that the scent reminded the sleeping brain of that learning, and nudged the replay along. You can't learn French by playing it while you sleep.

@chapter A19 cabin_fireplace | When the shift doesn't happen
What about the opposite? What if the night shift is short?

@study A10 rooftop_dusk | Seung-Schik Yoo and colleagues | Published in Nature Neuroscience, 2007 | Kept people awake for a night, then scanned their brains while they tried to memorise pictures
In 2007, Seung-Schik Yoo and colleagues kept volunteers awake all night, and the next day scanned their brains as they tried to memorise pictures. Compared with rested volunteers, the sleepless group remembered fewer of the pictures, and the hippocampus was less active as they tried. A tired brain seems to find it harder to take new things in.

@char A05 living_room_day | Not only after, but before | Sleep prepares the ground
So sleep seems to help twice. It helps to prepare the brain to take in new things, and it helps to settle what's already there. It's why a student's all-nighter is rather a poor bargain. They've swapped a night of consolidation for a few more hours with the book, and a tired brain that finds it harder to make new memories.

@char A02 autumn_overlook | A word of reassurance | Please don't worry
Now, if you're someone who sometimes struggles to sleep, please don't take this as one more thing to worry about. One bad night will not undo your memory, and worrying about sleep tends to make sleep harder. These are averages, over groups of people, and your brain is more forgiving than the headlines make it sound.

@char A03 cafe_sunset | Naps count too | A small bonus
Some studies suggest that even a short nap can help a memory settle. The evidence there is a bit more mixed, and the effects tend to be smaller than for a full night. But it's a gentle reminder that rest isn't a waste of time. In a way, it's part of the work.

@chapter A12 cafe_sunset | Skills in the dark
Sleep seems to help more than facts. It also seems to polish skills, and this is where the evidence is perhaps strongest.

@study A02 rooftop_dusk | Matthew Walker and colleagues | Published in Neuron, 2002 | Taught people a finger-tapping sequence, then tested them after a night's sleep or after a day awake
In 2002, Matthew Walker and colleagues taught people a short sequence to tap with their fingers on a keyboard, as quickly and accurately as possible. When they were tested twelve hours later, those who had slept in between had improved by about a fifth, while those who had stayed awake had not. The practice had improved overnight.

@char A15 autumn_overlook | Practice makes perfect | With sleep
It fits a pattern many musicians and athletes report. You struggle with a passage on Monday, and on Tuesday it's easier, as if your fingers had been practising in the night. In a sense, they had. The motor memory seems to have been refined while you slept.

@study A01 cafe_sunset | Sara Mednick, Ken Nakayama & Robert Stickgold | Published in Nature Neuroscience, 2003 | Compared a nap of an hour or so with a full night, on a visual learning task
In 2003, Sara Mednick, Ken Nakayama and Robert Stickgold tested a visual learning task, where people got faster at spotting a pattern. A nap of an hour or so, containing deep sleep, produced gains in the afternoon that were similar to the gains seen after a whole night. It suggests that a short rest can sometimes do part of the work.

@char A05 living_room_day | Not a prescription | Just a picture
None of this means you have to do anything special. It's simply a gentle reminder that the hours of rest we sometimes feel guilty about are hours in which the brain is quietly practising. And that you can trust it to carry on.

@chapter A15 cafe_sunset | Sleep and insight
Sleep may help with more than keeping things. It may help us see something new in them.

@study A12 rooftop_dusk | Ullrich Wagner, Steffen Gais, Hilde Haider, Rolf Verleger & Jan Born | Published in Nature, 2004 | Gave people a number puzzle with a hidden shortcut, and tested whether they found it after a night's sleep
In 2004, Ullrich Wagner and colleagues gave volunteers a long, tedious number task which had a hidden shortcut that made it much quicker, if you spotted it. After a first session, they were left for eight hours, either awake or asleep. After a night of sleep, people were more than twice as likely to find the shortcut as those who stayed awake.

@char A02 autumn_overlook | Sleeping on it | A real saying
It lends some scientific weight to the old advice to sleep on a problem. The idea is that, while you rest, the brain reorganises what it has learned, and sometimes it finds a connection that your tired, busy daytime mind could not see. It doesn't happen every time. But it is a lovely thought.

@list A15 rooftop_dusk | The third stop | New memories need time and quiet to settle ;; The sleeping brain replays the day ;; Stages of sleep seem to do different jobs ;; Rest before learning helps too
So that's the third stop. New memories need time and quiet to settle. The sleeping brain replays the day. Different stages of sleep seem to do different jobs. And rest before learning helps too.

@char A13 bedroom_night | A question for later | Where is the memory now?
So, after a night of this kind of tidying, where exactly is your Tuesday dinner? Where does a memory actually live? For decades, scientists went looking for it, and what they found is both disappointing and wonderful.
