So here's a possible answer to Hanna's question, one that many researchers find persuasive. As we get older, our lives often settle into more stable routines, and we experience fewer firsts. Fewer new places, fewer new beginnings, fewer brand new situations. Each year may leave fewer distinct memories behind. And when we look back, a year with fewer markers can seem to have passed more quickly.

Here's one way to put it. It is not the years that shrink. It is what they leave behind. That's a hopeful idea in a way, because it suggests that the speed of the years isn't entirely fixed by age. If newness and variety help a year feel fuller, then some of that may be within our reach, at any age. We'll come back to that near the end.

But a word of caution, too. This memory explanation fits a lot of what people describe, and it fits the holiday paradox nicely. So, where have we got to? Time doesn't really speed up. Age has a smaller effect than most people expect. The ratio idea is elegant, but incomplete. Fun can make time fly in the moment, while newness makes it feel long in memory. And routine seems to leave a lighter trace behind. Together, these ideas start to sketch a picture. But there's more to the story.

@chapter A10 cabin_fireplace | Novelty and surprise
Because it isn't only over months and years that newness changes our sense of time. Something similar seems to happen over just a fraction of a second. In the laboratory, scientists have found that new or unexpected things can seem to last longer than familiar ones, even when they're on screen for exactly the same amount of time. This gives us a closer view of how the brain handles novelty.

@char A20 street_day | A stream of pictures | And one odd one out
Here's the kind of experiment researchers use. Imagine sitting in front of a screen while a stream of pictures flashes past. The same image, over and over. Then, suddenly, a different image appears, an odd one out. Each picture is shown for the same brief moment. But when people are asked how long each one lasted, the odd one tends to feel longer than the others.

@study A13 living_room_day | Peter Tse and colleagues | Published in 2004 | An unexpected odd image seemed to last longer than the repeated ones
This is sometimes called the oddball effect. One well known study of it was published in 2004 by Peter Tse and his colleagues. They found that a single unexpected item in a stream of repeated ones seemed to last longer than it really did. The surprise of something new seemed to stretch it out, at least in how people judged its duration.

@char A03 attic_rain | Repetition and the brain | Less effort, less time?
Other researchers have explored the reverse side of the same idea. In 2007, Vani Pariyadath and David Eagleman published a study suggesting that repeated, predictable things can seem shorter than new ones. One explanation is that when the brain sees something familiar, it doesn't need to work as hard to process it. And that reduced effort might show up as a slightly shorter sense of time.

@list A13 cafe_sunset | Small moments, big pattern | New and surprising: seems longer ;; Repeated and expected: seems shorter
So in these tiny laboratory moments, we see a pattern that echoes what we found for whole years. New and surprising things tend to seem longer. Repeated and expected things tend to seem shorter. Of course, a fraction of a second on a screen is very different from a year of life. But it's intriguing that the same theme of newness appears at both scales.

@char A12 living_room_day | Not the same thing | But a family resemblance
We should be careful not to stretch this too far. The oddball effect is about judging the length of a brief moment, right after it happens. Hanna's question is about looking back over a whole year. These involve different processes in the brain, and researchers study them separately. But there's a family resemblance between them, and it points again toward novelty as something that makes time feel bigger.

@char A15 rooftop_dusk | A child's world | Full of oddballs
Think about what that might mean for a child. To a young child, the world is full of oddballs. A new word. A strange insect. The first frost on a window. The way the sea pulls at your feet. So much of what a child sees is still unfamiliar. Perhaps that's part of why childhood often seems so long and spacious when we look back.

@quote A19 cabin_fireplace | Surprise seems to stretch time. | The oddball lesson
So here's the lesson from the oddball studies. Surprise seems to stretch time. Familiarity seems to compress it. That doesn't mean we need constant novelty to live well. Familiar things are often the most comforting things in our lives. But it does help explain why the new and the unexpected leave such a strong mark on our sense of how long things lasted.
