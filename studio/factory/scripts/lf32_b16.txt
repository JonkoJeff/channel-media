@chapter A15 cabin_fireplace | Starting again
Every plan to change something eventually meets a bad day. You skip the walk. You eat the thing you said you wouldn't. You stay up too late, again. And what happens next, in the hours after a slip, matters more than the slip itself.

@char A03 living_room_day | The morning after | Why some starts feel easier
But first, a question. Why do some days feel like a better time to begin than others? A Monday. The first of the month. New Year's Day. Your birthday. Nothing about the world really changes at midnight. And yet something in us does.

@study A12 cafe_sunset | Hengchen Dai, Katherine Milkman & Jason Riis | Published in 2014 | Found that searches for 'diet' and visits to a university gym rose at the start of weeks, months and years, and after birthdays
In 2014, Hengchen Dai, Katherine Milkman and Jason Riis looked at several kinds of data. Internet searches for the word diet. Daily visits to a university gym. And commitments people made online to reach a personal goal.

@char A06 street_day | The result | The fresh start effect
All of them rose at the beginnings of things. The start of a week. The start of a month. The start of a new year. And, at the gym, just after a person's birthday. The researchers called it the fresh start effect.

@char A19 cabin_fireplace | Why it works | A clean page
Their explanation was gentle. Those landmarks seem to draw a line between an old self and a new one. The mistakes feel like they belong to the person you were last week. On the new page, you feel a little more like someone who could do it.

@study A05 autumn_overlook | Hengchen Dai, Katherine Milkman & Jason Riis | Published in 2015 | Found that describing a date as the start of a new period made people more eager to begin a goal on it
A year later, the same team tested whether you could create that feeling with words alone. They described the very same day in different ways. For some people, a date in March was simply the third Thursday of the month. For others, it was the first day of spring.

@char A06 street_day | The result | A new beginning on paper
When the day was framed as a beginning, the first day of spring, people were more eager to start a goal on it. Nothing about the day had changed, only the way it was described. A fresh start, it seems, is partly something we tell ourselves.

@char A15 living_room_day | Use it kindly | You can make your own landmarks
And the good news is, you don't have to wait for January. Any meaningful marker can work. A new season. The first day after a trip. Even the start of tomorrow, if you decide to see it that way.

@char A04 attic_rain | The other side | When a slip becomes a fall
But there's a darker pattern, too, and almost everyone knows it. You break a rule a little, and then, because it's already broken, you break it a lot. Researchers gave this a wonderfully blunt name.

@study A13 rooftop_dusk | C. Peter Herman & Deborah Mack | Published in 1975 | Found that dieters who had already drunk a milkshake went on to eat more ice cream, not less
In 1975, C. Peter Herman and Deborah Mack ran what was described as a taste test. Some volunteers first drank a milkshake. Others didn't. Then everyone was given tubs of ice cream to taste and rate, and could eat as much as they liked.

@char A17 neon_alley_rain | The result | Already broken
People who weren't dieting ate less ice cream after the milkshake, which makes sense. They were fuller. But people who were trying hard to restrict their eating often ate more ice cream after the milkshake. The diet was already broken for the day, so why hold back now? Janet Polivy and Peter Herman later wrote about this pattern, and it became known as the what-the-hell effect.

@char A08 bedroom_night | Why it happens | All or nothing
It seems to come from all-or-nothing thinking. A rule is either kept perfectly, or it's broken. There's no middle. And once it's broken, the rule stops protecting you. It's not just about food. You may know the same feeling with sleep, or screens, or studying.

@char A13 rooftop_dusk | A name from another field | The abstinence violation effect
Researchers who study relapse saw the same thing. The psychologists Alan Marlatt and Judith Gordon described what they called the abstinence violation effect. After a first slip, people often see themselves as having failed completely, and that very feeling of failure makes the next slip more likely. It isn't the slip that does the damage. It's the story told about it.

@char A20 cafe_sunset | The way out | One slip is one slip
The way out is the same kindness we met earlier tonight, in the study about self-forgiveness and procrastination. One slip is just one slip. It's information, not a verdict. The very next choice is still free, and it still counts.

@char A10 living_room_day | And rest | Motivation needs fuel
There's one more thing that helps people start again, and it's the least exciting idea of the night. Rest. We often treat tiredness as a lack of motivation. But very often, it's simply tiredness.

@study A13 cabin_fireplace | K. Anders Ericsson, Ralf Krampe & Clemens Tesch-Römer | Published in 1993 | Studied violin students in Berlin and found the best ones practised in focused sessions, and also slept and napped more
In 1993, K. Anders Ericsson and his colleagues studied violin students at a music academy in Berlin. They found that the best students practised a great deal, in focused sessions, often in the morning. And they also reported sleeping more, including more afternoon naps, than the less accomplished students.

@char A19 cabin_fireplace | What it suggests | Effort and rest belong together
That study is famous for its message about practice, which later research has debated a lot. But the quieter detail is often forgotten. The people who worked hardest also rested the most. Effort and rest aren't opposites. They're two halves of the same rhythm.
