@chapter N27 rooftop_dusk | Do you really know what comes next?
There's a detail in many accounts of déjà vu that's almost more unsettling than the familiarity itself. People say they feel not only that they've been here before, but that they know what is about to happen. Elena felt sure that the window on the landing would have a blue mug on the sill, before she could see it. Is that just a feeling, or is it a prediction, and can anyone test it?

@char N10 living_room_day | The claim | A sense of foresight
The claim is common. In surveys, many people who have experienced déjà vu say it came with a sense of knowing what would happen next, in the conversation or in the room. Some describe it as a sort of mild foresight. And some take it as evidence of something extraordinary, such as a glimpse of the future, or a dream that came true.

@char N19 cafe_sunset | A fair question | Can it be tested?
It's a fair question, and, as it turns out, it can be put to a direct test. If déjà vu really gave you some knowledge of what was coming, people having it should be able to say what will happen next better than chance. If it only gives the feeling of knowing, they won't. That's the logic behind a neat experiment that Anne Cleary and her student Anthony Claxton published in 2018.

@study N02 cabin_fireplace | Anne Cleary and Anthony Claxton | Psychological Science, 2018 | Déjà vu: an illusion of prediction
The team built virtual scenes, like walking through a series of corridors and rooms. Some of the new scenes had the same layout as an earlier scene that people had visited, but with different objects in them. At each turning point, the participants were asked whether they felt déjà vu, and whether they felt they knew which way the path would turn, left or right.

@char N09 living_room_day | The twist | The earlier turn was known
Here was the clever part. In the earlier scene with the same layout, the path had turned in a known direction. So if the déjà vu feeling were based on a real memory, then people who felt it could, in principle, predict the turn by sensing the old pattern. The researchers could compare what people felt they knew against what they actually guessed.

@char N10 cafe_sunset | What people felt | I know where it goes
People did report more feeling of knowing what was coming, when they had déjà vu. The sense of prediction was real, in the sense that people reported it, and it rose with the experience of déjà vu. This part of the result matches what people say about real episodes. When the feeling arrives, so does the sense of foresight.

@stat N19 cabin_fireplace | Accuracy when predicting the turn | Chance | people with déjà vu did no better than guessing
But the accuracy told a different story. When researchers checked how often people predicted the correct turn, those who felt a strong sense of déjà vu did no better than chance. They felt that they knew, but they didn't. The title of the paper puts it neatly: déjà vu, an illusion of prediction.

@char N09 living_room_day | What it shows | Feeling isn't foresight
So the feeling of foresight appears to be part of the illusion, not evidence that the illusion is true. The brain produces a sense of familiarity, and then draws a natural conclusion: if I know this place, then I should know what happens next. That conclusion comes from the feeling itself. It isn't a separate piece of information that has arrived from anywhere else.

@photo lf52_b07_01.jpg | A fork in a quiet path
A fork in a quiet path. Faced with a fork, most of us have a faint feeling about which way to go, and often we trust it more than we should. The experiment suggests that the sense of knowing and the fact of knowing can separate, in much the same way that confidence and accuracy can separate in memory for events, faces and facts.

@char N02 living_room_day | A cousin of the effect | Hindsight
There's a well-known cousin of this effect in the psychology of judgment. In 1975, the psychologist Baruch Fischhoff published a series of experiments on hindsight bias. People who learned how an event turned out were more likely to say they had known it all along. Once we know the outcome, it feels as though it was predictable, and we misremember our earlier uncertainty.

@char N19 cafe_sunset | Why this matters here | After-the-fact certainty
Hindsight bias is relevant because many stories of predictive déjà vu are told after the fact. A person feels that they know what's coming. Then something similar happens. In memory, the feeling and the outcome merge, and the story becomes, I knew that would happen. The uncertain, unverified moment of the feeling itself is lost, and what's left is a neat tale.

@char N10 cabin_fireplace | A second cousin | The illusion of will
There's another cousin, from the work of Daniel Wegner and Thalia Wheatley in 1999. They showed that people can feel they caused an event when a thought about it came just before it happened, even when they had no control. The brain links thoughts with events that follow closely, and it labels the link as cause. A feeling of knowing, followed by a result, can be linked in the same way.

@char N09 living_room_day | A famous claim | Feeling the future
Sometimes this topic spills into a bigger question: can people sense the future? In 2011, the psychologist Daryl Bem published a paper in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology called Feeling the Future, reporting experiments that seemed to show people responding to events that hadn't happened yet. It caused a sensation, and a great deal of debate.

@char N19 cafe_sunset | What happened next | Failed replications
Other researchers tried to repeat his experiments. In 2012, Stuart Ritchie, Richard Wiseman and Christopher French reported several attempts, in a paper called Failing the Future, and they did not find the effect. The episode became a landmark in the discussion of how psychology tests its own claims, and it left most researchers unconvinced that anyone can feel the future.

@list N27 rooftop_dusk | What we can say about predictive déjà vu | The feeling of prediction is real ;; The accuracy of the prediction is at chance ;; Hindsight can make stories seem more convincing ;; No good evidence that déjà vu foretells anything
Let's put that together. The feeling of prediction is real, and common. The accuracy of the prediction, when tested, is at chance. Hindsight can make stories of fulfilled predictions seem more convincing than they were. And there's no good scientific evidence that déjà vu foretells anything. That's not a defeat for wonder, it's just an honest reading of the data.

@char N02 living_room_day | A kinder way to see it | A sign of a clever machine
There's a kinder way to see all of this. A brain that can produce a feeling of prediction is a brain that is constantly trying to predict. Neuroscientists describe the mind as an anticipating machine, always guessing the next sight, sound and word. Déjà vu may be what it feels like when that guessing machine's sense of confidence fires at the wrong moment.

@char N10 cafe_sunset | Looking back | What we set out to learn
So where do we stand? We've seen that people with déjà vu often feel they know what will happen next. We've seen that Cleary and Claxton found that their predictions were no better than chance, that hindsight can polish such stories, and that Bem's claims about feeling the future did not survive replication attempts.
