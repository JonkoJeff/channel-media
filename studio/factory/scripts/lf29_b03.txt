@char A13 attic_rain | A tricky question | What were you thinking?
Try this for a moment. What were you thinking about ten seconds ago? Not roughly, but exactly. Was there a voice? Was there a picture? A feeling in your chest? It's surprisingly hard to say. By the time you look, the moment is gone, and what you find is a guess about it, coloured by what you expect to find.

@char A10 cabin_fireplace | The problem | Memory rewrites it
That is the problem that faced anyone who wanted to study the inner voice in adults. If you ask people in general, do you talk to yourself a lot, most will say yes, and they'll say it with confidence. But general impressions about our own minds can be surprisingly unreliable. We tend to report what we think should be there.

@chapter A16 rooftop_dusk | The beeper method
The American psychologist Russell Hurlburt, at the University of Nevada, Las Vegas, spent decades working on this problem. His solution was simple and rather clever.

@study A14 street_day | Russell Hurlburt | University of Nevada, Las Vegas | Descriptive Experience Sampling: a random beep, then a careful interview about that exact moment
Hurlburt developed a method he called Descriptive Experience Sampling. Volunteers carry a small beeper through their ordinary day. At random moments it beeps, and when it does, they jot down what was in their experience at the very instant of the beep. Not what they were thinking about in general, but what was actually there, right then. The next day, a researcher interviews them carefully about each beep, trying hard not to put ideas into their heads.

@list A12 cafe_sunset | Why the beep helps | It catches a random moment ;; It's fresh, not remembered ;; It's specific, not general ;; Interviews remove guesswork
The beep solves several problems at once. Because it's random, it catches ordinary moments rather than the ones people remember. Because people write notes immediately, the moment is fresh. And because the interviews focus on single, specific moments, people slowly learn to describe what was really there, rather than what they assume is always there.

@photo lf29_b03_01.jpg | A beep in an ordinary day
Imagine carrying one of these through your day. You're making tea, and it beeps. You're walking to the bus, and it beeps. Each beep is like a camera shutter opening on the inside of your mind for a fraction of a second. And what Hurlburt and his colleagues found, when they looked at those snapshots, surprised them.

@chapter A05 autumn_overlook | What the snapshots showed
In 2008, Hurlburt and his colleague Christopher Heavey published a study in the journal Consciousness and Cognition. They had given beepers to a stratified random sample of thirty students from a large urban university.

@stat A03 living_room_day | Moments with inner speech | 26% | average share of sampled moments (Heavey & Hurlburt, 2008)
Here is the headline number. On average, inner speech showed up in about a quarter of the sampled moments, 26 percent. That's a lot, but it's far less than most people would guess. Many of us are sure that we talk to ourselves almost constantly. When the beeper actually caught people in the act, the inner voice was there in roughly one moment out of four.

@bars A18 cabin_fireplace | Inner speech varied hugely | % of moments | Lowest person:0 ;; Median person:20 ;; Average:26 ;; Highest person:75
And that average hides something even more interesting. People were wildly different. Some students had no inner speech in any of their samples at all. Others had it in as many as three quarters of them. The median person, right in the middle, had it about one fifth of the time. So two people sitting side by side on a bus may have very different kinds of minds.

@list A13 attic_rain | Five common kinds of inner experience | Inner speech ;; Inner seeing ;; Unsymbolized thinking ;; Feelings ;; Sensory awareness
Inner speech was only one of five common kinds of experience they found. The others were inner seeing, which is like pictures in the mind, unsymbolized thinking, which is a clear thought with no words or images attached, feelings, and sensory awareness, such as noticing the colour of a cup or the warmth of the sun. Each of these turned up in roughly a quarter of the samples, often more than one at a time.

@char A19 cabin_fireplace | Thoughts without words | A surprising one
That third one, unsymbolized thinking, is worth pausing on. Hurlburt describes it as knowing exactly what you are thinking, for example, wondering whether your friend will drive or take the train, but with no words and no pictures in the experience. Many people are surprised to learn they do this, because they had always assumed every thought must be made of words.

@chapter A12 cafe_sunset | An old discovery
In a way, this wasn't the first time someone had discovered that minds differ on the inside.

@study A10 living_room_day | Francis Galton | England, 1880 | Asked people to picture their breakfast table, and found that some saw it vividly and some barely at all
In 1880, the English scientist Francis Galton published a study in the journal Mind about mental imagery. He asked people to picture their breakfast table that morning and describe how clear the image was. Some described a scene as bright and detailed as the real thing. Others said they saw almost nothing. Galton was struck by how differently people described the same simple task.

@char A13 attic_rain | The same for words | Different inner worlds
What Galton found for pictures, Hurlburt found for words. Some people live with a near constant inner voice. Some rarely hear one. And because we can't look inside each other's heads, most of us simply assume that everyone else's mind works the way ours does. It's only when someone asks the question carefully that the differences show up.

@chapter A02 street_day | A questionnaire for the inner voice
Beepers are slow and careful. Other researchers wanted a quicker way to ask large numbers of people about their inner speech.

@study A03 autumn_overlook | McCarthy-Jones & Fernyhough | Durham University, 2011 | The Varieties of Inner Speech Questionnaire: four common qualities of everyday inner speech
In 2011, Simon McCarthy-Jones and Charles Fernyhough published a questionnaire called the Varieties of Inner Speech Questionnaire. Instead of just asking whether people talk to themselves, it asks what their inner speech is like.

@list A15 cafe_sunset | Four qualities of inner speech | Dialogic: a back and forth ;; Condensed: fragments, not sentences ;; Other people: voices of others ;; Evaluative: judging and motivating
They described four common qualities. Dialogic inner speech, a back and forth, like a conversation. Condensed inner speech, in fragments and single words, the shorthand we met earlier. Inner speech with other people in it, where you hear the voice of a parent, a friend or a teacher. And evaluative or motivational inner speech, where the voice judges how you're doing or pushes you on.

@char A20 living_room_day | Which is yours? | A quiet check
You might like to ask yourself which of these sound familiar. Do you argue with yourself? Do you think in quick fragments? Do you sometimes hear a particular person's tone, a parent's warning or a coach's encouragement? Do you have a voice that comments on how you're doing? Most people recognise more than one.

@char A18 cabin_fireplace | Voices of others | Where they come from
That third quality fits neatly with Vygotsky's idea. If inner speech grows out of real conversations, it makes sense that the voices of the people who raised us and taught us would still echo in it. When you hear a thought in your grandmother's tone, it may be a small piece of a conversation you had long ago, still running in a new form.

@chapter A05 autumn_overlook | What we've learned so far
So let's gather up what the beepers and questionnaires have taught us.

@list A12 cafe_sunset | The inner voice, measured | Common, but less constant than we think ;; Very different from person to person ;; Sometimes words, sometimes not ;; Often shaped like a conversation
The inner voice is common, but it may not be as constant as we assume. It differs enormously from one person to the next. Some of our thinking happens in words, and some happens without them. And a lot of inner speech is shaped like a conversation, with more than one point of view.

@char A10 bedroom_night | A question left over | What about the zeros?
But that leaves an intriguing question hanging. What about the people at the very bottom of the range, the ones whose beeps found almost no inner speech at all? Do they think differently? Do they do some tasks differently? In 2024, two researchers decided to find out.
