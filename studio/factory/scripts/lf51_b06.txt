@chapter N27 cabin_fireplace | Why time crawls
Let's look at the most noticeable part of boredom, which is the way the clock seems to slow to a crawl. It's such a universal experience that we have a proverb for it, a watched pot never boils.

@char N10 living_room_day | The watched clock | Mira checks again
Mira glances at the clock on the wall, and it has moved by what looks like two minutes. She is sure it should be more. She looks away, counts to a hundred in her head, and looks back, and the minute hand has barely shifted. It's a small kind of torture, and it's one that almost everybody recognises.

@char N19 rooftop_dusk | A strange fact | Time isn't a sense
Here's something that sounds odd. We don't have a dedicated sense for time, the way we have eyes for light or ears for sound. Our brains build a feeling of duration from other things, including attention, memory and emotion. Which means that the feeling can stretch and shrink, depending on what the mind is doing.

@char N09 cabin_fireplace | Two kinds of timing | In the moment and in hindsight
Psychologists separate two kinds of time judgement. Prospective timing is how long something feels while it is happening, as in, how long have I been sitting here? Retrospective timing is how long it seems afterwards, as in, how long was that appointment? These two behave differently, and boredom pulls them in opposite directions.

@char N02 living_room_day | In the moment | Attention to the clock
For prospective timing, a leading idea is that the more attention you pay to the passing of time, the longer it feels. Researchers Dan Zakay and Richard Block described this in an influential model, often called the attentional gate model, in the 1990s. In it, a kind of mental gate lets pulses through to be counted, and attention to time opens the gate wider.

@char N10 cafe_sunset | The gate | More pulses, longer time
When nothing else is holding your attention, there's little else to attend to but time, so the gate opens wide and more pulses are counted. When you're absorbed in something engrossing, attention goes to the task and fewer pulses are counted, and time seems to fly. That is the whole of the saying, time flies when you're having fun, put in a sentence.

@char N19 living_room_day | A study | Danckert and Allman, 2005
In 2005, James Danckert and Ava-Ann Allman published a paper in the journal Brain and Cognition, with the lovely title, Time flies when you're having fun. They looked at how people's boredom related to their sense of time. In their work, people who were more prone to boredom tended to feel that time passed more slowly, and to be less accurate in their time estimates.

@char N09 rooftop_dusk | More recently | Waiting in a lab
A later study, led by Marta Witowska with Stefan Schmidt and Marc Wittmann, published in 2020 in Acta Psychologica, looked at real waiting. Participants were asked to wait in a room for a period, and the researchers asked how bored they felt and how long it seemed. Greater boredom was linked with a stronger feeling that time was dragging.

@char N10 cabin_fireplace | A loop | Boredom and slow time
You can see how this makes a loop. Boredom turns attention to the clock. Attention on the clock makes time feel longer. And longer time feels more boring, so we check the clock again. It's a small trap that the mind builds for itself, and understanding it can take some of the sting out.

@list N11 cafe_sunset | Why waiting feels so long | Nothing else to hold attention ;; Not knowing how long it will last ;; No explanation for the delay ;; Waiting alone ;; Worry about what comes next
Back in 1985, the management thinker David Maister wrote a famous essay, The Psychology of Waiting Lines, that gathered some of the things that make waits feel long. They included empty time, uncertainty about how long it will last, a delay that isn't explained, waiting alone, and anxiety. These were observations from service settings, rather than controlled experiments, but they ring true.

@char N23 autumn_overlook | A useful lesson | Occupied time passes faster
The most useful of these is the first. Maister's observation, that occupied time feels shorter than unoccupied time, is why waiting rooms have magazines and televisions, and why some lifts have mirrors. It matches what the attention research suggests. If you give the mind something to hold, the clock loses its grip.

@char N02 living_room_day | The uncertainty | Not knowing is harder
The second item, uncertainty, may be the most powerful. A wait with a known end is easier to bear than a wait with no end in sight. Knowing that the appointment will begin in ten minutes lets the mind relax a little. Not knowing leaves it on constant, low-level alert, and that is exhausting in its own right.

@char N09 cabin_fireplace | A twist | The paradox of boredom
Now comes a twist. After a boring stretch has ended, we often look back at it very differently. A long, uneventful afternoon can seem oddly short in memory. That's because retrospective time depends on how many distinct memories were laid down, and monotony produces very few.

@char N19 rooftop_dusk | A familiar example | Dull days vanish
You may notice this when you think about a month of identical days at work, compared with a week of travel. The week of travel seems long in memory, packed with new sights and surprises. The month of sameness seems to evaporate, even though it felt endless while you were living it.

@char N10 living_room_day | The double bind | Slow now, quick later
So boredom stretches the present and shrinks the past. Each boring minute drags, but the whole stretch leaves almost no trace. Researchers sometimes call this the paradox of boredom, and it explains why a dull period of life can feel both interminable and strangely lost.

@char N02 cafe_sunset | A thought | Time and novelty
It also hints at something encouraging. If novelty is what stretches our memory of time, then small, new experiences might be one way to make our days feel fuller. A different route to work, a new recipe or a new corner of town gives the mind something new to record.

@char N27 rooftop_dusk | A caution | No magic formula
That said, it's wise to be careful with grand claims. The size of these effects varies from person to person, and from setting to setting. There's no formula that guarantees a slower or quicker life. But the basic pattern of attention, time and memory is well supported.

@char N09 living_room_day | Back to Mira | A small trick
Mira, still in her plastic chair, tries a little experiment. She decides not to look at the clock for what feels like ten minutes. She notices the pattern on the floor tiles, the muffled sound of someone typing, and the way the light shifts across the wall. When she looks again, to her surprise, more time has passed than she expected.

@char N10 cabin_fireplace | What happened | Attention moved
What happened is exactly what the research predicts. By taking attention off the clock, Mira closed the gate a little. She hasn't escaped the waiting, but she has changed what the waiting is made of, and that is often all that boredom needs.
