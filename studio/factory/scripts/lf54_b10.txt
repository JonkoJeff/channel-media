@chapter N29 cabin_fireplace | The urge and the brake
Oskar has a theory about resisting yawns. The harder he tries, he says, the more the yawn insists. It's like being told not to think of a particular tune. He has no data for this, only years of staff meetings. But the feeling of an urge building up against a brake is one that researchers have taken seriously.

@char A12 rooftop_dusk | Echo actions | Copying without meaning to
Contagious yawning belongs to a wider family called echo actions: automatically imitating what someone else does. Echoing a word is one, echoing a movement is another. Everyone has a mild tendency in this direction. In some neurological conditions it becomes much stronger. Yawning is interesting to researchers partly because it's a safe, everyday echo that almost anyone can experience on cue.

@char A10 autumn_overlook | The motor cortex | The strip that moves you
Part of the brain that matters here is the primary motor cortex, a strip running roughly from ear to ear over the top of the head, which is heavily involved in producing voluntary movements. Neighbouring areas help prepare actions and hold them back. If contagious yawns are echo actions, how excitable this movement system is might make a difference.

@study N09 bedroom_night | A University of Nottingham team led by Stephen Jackson | Current Biology, published 2017 | Resisting the contagious yawn
In a study published in 2017, a team at the University of Nottingham led by the neuroscientist Stephen Jackson asked thirty-six adults to watch clips of other people yawning. Sometimes the volunteers were told to let yawns happen freely. Sometimes they were told to resist them. Their yawns, including stifled ones, were counted, and they reported how strong the urge felt.

@list N10 attic_rain | What the team measured | Yawns while allowed ;; Yawns while resisting ;; The felt urge to yawn ;; Excitability of the motor cortex
The team also used a technique called transcranial magnetic stimulation. A coil held against the head delivers brief magnetic pulses that nudge nerve cells underneath, and by measuring the small muscle responses that follow, researchers can estimate how excitable and how restrained a person's motor cortex is. That gave them a physical measure to set beside each person's yawning.

@stat N20 living_room_day | What predicted contagion | Excitability | and inhibition in each person's motor cortex
The Nottingham team studying contagious yawns reported that each person's level of motor cortex excitability, together with the strength of its braking or inhibition, predicted how prone that person was to contagious yawning. In other words, part of the difference between the yawn catcher and the unmoved passenger may lie in how their movement systems are set, somewhat below the reach of intention.

@char N01 cafe_sunset | Turning up the signal | A change in the urge
Then they went a step further. When they used electrical stimulation to increase excitability in the motor cortex, people's urge to yawn went up. That matters because it moves beyond a simple link. Changing something in the brain was followed by a change in the experience. It suggests that this system isn't merely along for the ride.

@photo lf54_b10_01.jpg | A soft model of a brain on a desk
A simple model of a brain on a quiet desk, the kind that might sit in a university office. It's easy to talk about brain areas as though each one did a single job. In truth, a yawn involves a network, from the brainstem that organises the basic movement to the regions that perceive other people. The motor cortex is one important voice among several.

@char N27 cabin_fireplace | About resisting | Willpower has limits
What about Oskar's theory that trying not to yawn makes it worse? The Nottingham findings suggest that willpower has only a limited grip here. Told to resist, people could alter how the yawns came out, often stifling them, but they didn't simply become immune. The tendency each person showed seemed to travel with them across the instructions.

@char A03 rooftop_dusk | Stifled yawns count | Half hidden, still there
That distinction between a full yawn and a stifled one matters. If you only count wide open mouths, resisting looks very successful. Count the swallowed, flared-nostril versions too and the picture changes. The program can be squashed into a smaller shape while still running. Anyone who has fought a yawn during a solemn speech already knows this from the inside.

@char N29 autumn_overlook | Mirror systems | A popular idea, treated carefully
You may have heard that contagious yawning is explained by mirror neurons, nerve cells that become active both when an action is performed and when it's seen. Some brain imaging studies have reported activity, during yawn viewing, in regions often associated with this kind of matching. Other studies have found different patterns. The idea is plausible in outline and not yet a complete explanation.

@list A10 bedroom_night | Why brain pictures need care | Small groups ;; Different tasks ;; Yawning moves the head, which disturbs scans ;; Activity during viewing is not proof of cause
Imaging this behaviour is awkward. The groups tend to be small, the tasks differ, and a real yawn moves the head, which is exactly what a brain scanner dislikes. And an area lighting up while someone watches a yawn doesn't show that it causes the response. These are reasons for patience, and the field is still collecting pieces.

@char N09 attic_rain | A picture so far | Perceive, prepare, release
Here's a cautious sketch that fits what we've heard. You perceive or imagine a yawn. That activates, to some degree, your own representation of the movement. A movement system with its own level of excitability and braking decides how far this goes. If it crosses a threshold, the old, built-in yawning pattern is released, and then it runs to completion.

@curve A12 living_room_day | A sketch of the idea | Time after seeing a yawn | Urge | 0.6 | builds, then releases
Think of the urge as something that builds and then discharges. This line is only an illustration of the experience people describe: a quiet start, a rising pressure, a peak where the yawn breaks through or is smothered, and then relief. It's there to help you picture it, and it doesn't plot measurements from any particular study.

@char N10 cafe_sunset | Relief afterwards | The scratch for the itch
That relief afterwards explains why the struggle feels so lopsided. Holding back asks for continuous effort. Giving in ends the pressure in six seconds. It's rather like an itch. You can leave it unscratched with some determination, but the whole time part of your mind stays busy with it, and busy minds aren't very good at listening to a head teacher.

@char N20 cabin_fireplace | Oskar's compromise | The quiet yawn
Oskar's own compromise, perfected over many years, is to yawn through his nose with his lips closed while nodding thoughtfully. He believes it's invisible. His colleagues, who can see his eyes water, have never had the heart to tell him. And given what we heard about covered mouths, they're probably catching it anyway.

@list N01 rooftop_dusk | Where we are | Yawn contagion is a kind of echo action ;; Motor cortex excitability and braking predicted it ;; Boosting excitability raised the urge ;; Resisting doesn't make you immune
To gather it up: contagious yawning can be seen as an echo action. In the Nottingham study, the excitability and restraint of each person's motor cortex predicted how readily they caught yawns, and raising excitability raised the urge. Trying to resist could shrink or disguise a yawn without removing the pull toward it.

@char N27 autumn_overlook | Next stop | When does it begin?
If catching yawns depends on a brain that perceives other people and a movement system that responds, then you might expect it to take some growing into. It does. Newborns yawn on their first day, but a contagious yawn can leave a toddler entirely unmoved. At the next stop, we'll find out when that changes.
