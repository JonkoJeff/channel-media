@chapter A10 cabin_fireplace | The man who couldn't forget
Imagine, for a moment, never forgetting anything. Every conversation, every face, every number, kept for good. It sounds like a superpower. But there's a man whose story suggests it might be something more complicated.

@study A19 rooftop_dusk | Alexander Luria | The Mind of a Mnemonist, 1968 | Described a Moscow newspaper reporter, known as S., whose memory seemed to have no limit
In the 1920s, a young Moscow newspaper reporter named Solomon Shereshevsky was sent to see the Russian psychologist Alexander Luria. His editor had noticed that he never took notes. Luria tested him, with long tables of numbers and lists of words, and could find no limit. He wrote about him in his 1968 book, The Mind of a Mnemonist, and followed him for roughly three decades.

@char A03 living_room_day | How he did it | Colours, shapes, pictures
Shereshevsky's memory seemed to depend on a rich blend of the senses. Words and numbers came with colours, tastes and shapes, a condition now called synaesthesia. He would also place what he wanted to remember along a familiar street in his mind, and walk along it later. And years after a test, he could repeat a long table exactly.

@char A13 autumn_overlook | The cost | A mind too full
But Luria also wrote about the price. Shereshevsky found it hard to grasp the general meaning of what he read, because every word summoned a flood of images that got in the way. He sometimes failed to recognise people, because faces changed so much from moment to moment. He was swamped by detail.

@char A19 cafe_sunset | Trying to forget | A strange effort
Luria describes how S. struggled to forget things that no longer mattered. He reportedly tried writing numbers on paper and burning them, hoping that the act would help them to go. His life shows that a perfect memory is not the same as a useful one. A good memory is selective.

@chapter A12 rooftop_dusk | A modern case
In recent decades, researchers have met people with an unusual gift for remembering the events of their own lives.

@study A02 living_room_day | Elizabeth Parker, Larry Cahill & James McGaugh | Published in Neurocase, 2006 | Described a woman, known as AJ, who could recall what she had done on almost any date from her teenage years
In 2006, Elizabeth Parker, Larry Cahill and James McGaugh reported the case of a woman they called AJ. Given almost any date since her early teens, she could tell them what the day was, what she did, and often what was in the news. It was later called highly superior autobiographical memory. Other people with the same ability were found and studied afterwards.

@char A15 autumn_overlook | Not a perfect recorder | Something specific
But these people were not good at everything. In tests of memory for lists of words, they did not do much better than others. What they had was a remarkable way of recalling their own life stories, rather than a camera. And their memories were still reconstructed in the way ours are.

@study A05 cafe_sunset | Lawrence Patihis and colleagues | Published in PNAS, 2013 | Tested people with highly superior autobiographical memory for false memories
In 2013, Lawrence Patihis and colleagues tested people with this ability for false memories. They gave them word lists designed to trigger false recall, along with other tests of false memory. The group was as likely as other people to produce false memories. Even an exceptional memory is a rebuilding, not a recording.

@chapter A03 bedroom_night | Forgetting at work
So perhaps forgetting is not a defect. Let's look at what it might be doing for us, starting with a clever experiment.

@study A01 rooftop_dusk | Michael Anderson, Robert Bjork & Elizabeth Bjork | Published in 1994 | Had people practise some items from a category, then tested the items they didn't practise
In 1994, Michael Anderson and Robert and Elizabeth Bjork asked people to learn lists of pairs, such as fruit and orange, or fruit and banana. Then they practised some of them, say fruit and orange, several times. Later, the items that had not been practised, like fruit and banana, were harder to recall than items from categories that had not been practised at all.

@char A20 living_room_day | Remembering one thing, forgetting another | Retrieval-induced forgetting
The act of remembering orange seemed to push banana down a little. This is called retrieval-induced forgetting. One idea is that your brain has to quieten competing memories in order to bring up the one you want. It's still debated whether the quietening is active, but the effect has been shown many times.

@photo lf31_b11_01.jpg | Letting go, quietly
There is something peaceful about it. A pond doesn't hold every leaf that falls. It lets them drift by, and the surface stays clear enough to reflect the sky. Forgetting, in a way, keeps the surface clear enough for you to use.

@study A12 autumn_overlook | Blake Richards & Paul Frankland | Published in Neuron, 2017 | Argued that forgetting helps the brain make better decisions in a changing world
In 2017, Blake Richards and Paul Frankland made this argument in a paper in the journal Neuron. They suggested that the goal of memory isn't to keep everything. It's to help you make good decisions. Letting go of outdated or unimportant details helps you to generalise and to adapt when the world changes.

@char A02 cafe_sunset | The car park | An everyday example
There is a simple example that psychologists like. Where did you park your car? You need to remember where you parked it today, but not where you parked it on any other day. If yesterday's spot kept coming to mind, you'd be lost. Forgetting yesterday makes room for today.

@list A05 living_room_day | Seven ways memory lets us down | Transience: fading over time ;; Absent-mindedness: never really stored ;; Blocking: tip of the tongue ;; Misattribution: the wrong source ;; Suggestibility: ideas planted from outside ;; Bias: today colouring yesterday ;; Persistence: what won't leave
Daniel Schacter, in 1999, listed what he called the seven sins of memory. Transience, absent-mindedness, blocking, misattribution, suggestibility, bias and persistence. We've already met several of them tonight. His point, though, was not that memory is bad. It was that each sin is the other side of something useful.

@char A19 cabin_fireplace | The last sin | When memories won't leave
Persistence deserves a gentle word. Some memories we wish we could set down, and can't. It can feel unfair that the mind keeps hold of the painful ones so firmly. If that's something you carry, it is worth speaking to a professional who can help, and not trying to manage it alone. Research on this is active, and there is real help.

@char A13 rooftop_dusk | A kinder view | Forgetting as care
For most of us, most of the time, though, forgetting is a quiet kindness. It lets an old embarrassment lose its sting. It lets last year's worries fade. It leaves room for new things. And it makes the memories that stay stand out all the more.

@chapter A12 living_room_day | Can you choose to forget?
That raises a question many people wonder about. Can you decide to forget something? And is it good to try?

@study A15 rooftop_dusk | Michael Anderson & Collin Green | Published in Nature, 2001 | Taught people word pairs, then asked them not to think of some of the answers when they saw the cues
In 2001, Michael Anderson and Collin Green taught volunteers pairs of words. Then they showed one word from some of the pairs, and asked people not to think of the word that went with it. They were asked to keep the other word out of mind, repeatedly. Later, those words were harder to recall than words they had not tried to suppress.

@char A20 autumn_overlook | A mild effect | And a debated one
It suggests that holding a memory out of mind can make it a little less available. But it's an effect of degree, not a delete button. Researchers still debate how large and how reliable it is, and these were neutral word pairs, not painful life events. It's a reminder that real memories are far stickier than lab words.

@char A19 cabin_fireplace | A gentler path | Not fighting the thought
In everyday life, pushing a memory away tends to work less well than people hope. For many, it's the gentler paths, like talking, time, rest and support, that help a hard memory lose its grip. That is not a medical claim, just a reminder that being kind to yourself is not a bad strategy for a memory that hurts.

@list A03 living_room_day | The eighth stop | Perfect memory is not the same as useful memory ;; Even exceptional memories are rebuilt ;; Remembering one thing can quieten a rival ;; Forgetting helps us adapt
So that's the eighth stop. A perfect memory is not the same as a useful one. Even exceptional memories are rebuilt. Remembering one thing can quieten a rival. And forgetting helps us adapt to a changing world.

@char A10 bedroom_night | Now, the rebuilding | The memory that changes
And that word, rebuilt, is the one I've been holding back. Because the strangest part of the story isn't how memories fade. It's how they change. And it begins with a very old story about ghosts.
