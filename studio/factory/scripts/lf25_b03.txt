@chapter A12 living_room_day | Us and them
Some conversations are hard because we know the other person well. Others are hard because they belong to a group that we've decided to distrust. Politics, religion, generations, ways of living. In these cases, we often arrive with a picture of the other side already in our heads. A growing body of research asks how accurate that picture is.

@char A04 attic_rain | Naive realism | I see it as it is
Social psychologists, notably Lee Ross at Stanford, described a common stance that they called naive realism. It has three parts. We believe that we see the world objectively, we believe that reasonable people will agree with us once they see the facts, and so we conclude that anyone who disagrees must be uninformed, irrational or biased.

@list A03 living_room_day | The three beliefs of naive realism | I see things as they really are ;; Reasonable people see them the same way ;; Those who don't must be misinformed or biased
It's a stance that feels like plain common sense, which is why it's so hard to catch. Notice that each side in a disagreement holds it at the same time. Both think that they're the realist and the other is distorted by something.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Pronin, Lin & Ross | Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin, 2002 | The bias blind spot: perceptions of bias in self versus others
Emily Pronin and her colleagues asked people how susceptible they were to a range of biases, such as the tendency to take credit for success and blame others for failure, compared with the average person. Most people said that they were less biased than the average person, which can't be true for most. And people didn't change their view even after learning about the bias.

@char A06 cafe_sunset | An uncomfortable finding | The bias we can't see in ourselves
The bias blind spot is a gentle name for a serious problem. It helps to explain why a conversation can go wrong even when both people have good intentions. Each person is quietly confident that the other one is the biased one, and so each sees little reason to adjust.

@chapter A13 rooftop_dusk | How different are we, really?
If naive realism makes us assume that the other side sees things in a distorted way, how big do we think the gap is? And how big is it really? A line of research has tried to measure the difference between what people believe about the other side, and what the other side believes.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Ahler & Sood | Journal of Politics, 2018 | The parties in our heads: misperceptions about party composition and their consequences
Douglas Ahler and Gaurav Sood surveyed Americans about the makeup of the two main parties. They found that people consistently overestimated how many members of the opposing party belonged to the groups that seemed most unlike them. People believed that the other side was more extreme and more stereotypical than it actually was, and when told the real numbers, their feelings towards the other party softened a little.

@char A12 living_room_day | The perception gap | Imagined distance
Other researchers have found a similar pattern. In several countries, people overestimate how extreme the other side's views are, and underestimate how much they share. A large survey project called the Perception Gap, led by the group More in Common, found that those who followed politics most closely were often the most mistaken about their opponents, though the details differ between surveys.

@list A12 living_room_day | What the perception gap research suggests | We imagine the other side as more extreme than it is ;; Those who are most engaged are not always most accurate ;; Correcting the picture can reduce hostility ;; The gap is bigger in our heads than in reality
This is not a claim that there are no real differences. There are many, and some of them are deep. It's a claim that the distance is often smaller than it feels, and that part of the heat in a conversation comes from arguing with a version of the other person that doesn't quite exist.

@chapter A06 cafe_sunset | Where do the stereotypes come from?
Why would we hold such an exaggerated picture? One answer is that we get our impressions of the other side from the loudest and most extreme examples. Moderate people are quiet. They don't go viral. So the people who represent a group in the news and on social media are often not typical of it at all.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Bail | Breaking the Social Media Prism, 2021 | How to make our platforms less polarizing
The sociologist Chris Bail, whom we met in an earlier video, argued that social media creates a distorted mirror. Extreme voices post more and get more attention, while moderate people stay silent. The result is that we see the other side through its most outspoken members, and conclude that this is what they are like. It is a theory with support, though it's still being tested.

@char A04 attic_rain | A practical idea | Meet a typical person
It follows that one of the best ways to reduce the distortion is to talk to ordinary people from the other side, face to face, about ordinary things. Not the person who shouts the loudest, but the one in the next office, the neighbour, the cousin. Such conversations tend to replace the cartoon with a person, and people are harder to dislike than cartoons.

@chapter A12 living_room_day | The curiosity gap
There's another surprising finding. People often assume that the other side would not be interested in talking, or would be unpleasant if they did. But experiments show that such conversations are generally more pleasant than expected. In a series of studies, strangers who talked to each other, including across divides, enjoyed it more than they predicted.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Epley & Schroeder | Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, 2014 | Mistakenly seeking solitude
Nicholas Epley and Juliana Schroeder asked commuters in Chicago to either talk to a stranger on the train or bus, sit in silence, or do what they normally did. Those asked to talk reported a more positive commute, and productivity wasn't harmed. Yet before the task, most predicted that they would prefer solitude. The same pattern, expecting a conversation to be awkward and finding it enjoyable, has been repeated in many places.

@char A01 cafe_sunset | The underestimated pleasure | Conversations go better than expected
Later work by Epley and others found that people also underestimate how much others enjoy being asked questions, being thanked, and being contacted out of the blue. The fear of an awkward conversation is usually larger than the reality. That doesn't mean every talk goes well. But it suggests that our first reaction, to avoid, is not the best guide.

@list A03 living_room_day | Words to keep from this chapter | Naive realism ;; Bias blind spot ;; Perception gap ;; Stereotype
Four terms from this chapter. Naive realism is the belief that we see the world as it is. The bias blind spot is seeing bias in others and not in ourselves. The perception gap is the difference between what we think the other side believes and what it believes. And a stereotype is a simplified picture of a group.

@list A12 living_room_day | A small experiment | Think of someone you disagree with ;; Write down what you think they believe ;; Ask them what they actually believe ;; Compare the two
Here's a small experiment you can try this week. Think of one person whose views you find difficult. Write down, in a few sentences, what you think they believe and why. Then, if it's safe and possible, ask them. Don't argue, only ask, and listen to the answer. Many people find that their picture was partly right and partly a caricature. The distance between the two is the size of your own perception gap, and it's a good thing to know.

@list A19 living_room_day | Signs you may be arguing with a caricature | You can describe their view only in insulting terms ;; You've never heard them state it themselves ;; You assume they hold every view of their group ;; You'd be surprised if they agreed with you on anything
Here are four signs that you may be arguing with a picture of the person, and not the person. First, if you can only describe their view in insulting words, it's probably not how they would describe it. Second, if you've never heard them state their position in their own voice, you may be relying on someone else's summary. Third, if you assume they hold every view that people in their group are supposed to hold, you're missing the individual. And fourth, if you'd be shocked to learn that you agree on anything at all, the odds are that you do, and haven't yet discovered it. None of this means they're right. It means that the argument you're having should be with them, not with a cartoon that is easier to beat.

@char A15 cabin_fireplace | A gentle thought | Real people are harder to hate
There's a kindness in this research. It says that the person you're arguing with is probably less extreme, more complicated and more like you than the version in your head. That doesn't make their views right. But it means that you can disagree with a person, and not with a caricature, which is a far better place to begin.

@char A08 cabin_fireplace | Looking back | What the divide taught us
So what did we learn? That each side thinks it sees the world as it is. That most people think they're less biased than average. That we imagine the other side as more extreme than it is. And that talking to a real person usually goes better than expected.
