@char A04 bedroom_night | The tip of the tongue | A famous feeling
There's a particular sensation Marta has been living with since the party, and almost everyone knows it. You're certain you know the word. You can feel its shape. You can almost taste it. You can tell it has two syllables, perhaps, or that it starts with a certain sound. And yet it won't come. Psychologists call this the tip-of-the-tongue state, and it's one of the most studied feelings in all of memory research.

@char A13 cabin_fireplace | Not a new discovery | William James
It was noticed long before laboratories existed. In 1890, in his great book The Principles of Psychology, William James described the experience beautifully, the way a missing word is present in our minds in a sort of gap, a gap with a shape. He didn't give it a name, but he captured exactly what it feels like, and he saw that it was a puzzle worth thinking about.

@study A10 living_room_day | Brown & McNeill, 1966 | The tip of the tongue phenomenon | Researchers read out definitions of rare words and asked people to name them. When people were in a tip-of-the-tongue state, they could often say the first letter and the number of syllables better than chance.
Then, in 1966, two Harvard psychologists, Roger Brown and David McNeill, decided to study it properly. They read out definitions of rare words and asked volunteers to name the word. When someone was stuck in a tip-of-the-tongue state, the researchers asked what they could tell about the missing word. And people could say much more than you might expect. They did better than chance at the first letter, the number of syllables, and even words that sounded similar.

@list A19 attic_rain | What people knew about a word they couldn't say | The first letter ;; The number of syllables ;; Words that sound alike ;; Words that mean the same ;; The pattern of its syllables
So, stuck on a word, people still knew a surprising amount about it. The first letter. How many syllables it had. Words that sounded a bit like it. Words with a similar meaning. And the pattern of its syllables. The word wasn't missing at all. It was partly there, with its outline visible and only the middle missing.

@char A12 street_day | What this tells us | Partly there
That's a fascinating clue about what's going on. It suggests that a tip-of-the-tongue state isn't an empty drawer. It's a half-open one. Some of the information about the word is available, and some isn't, and the part that's missing is the sound itself. That's exactly Marta's experience. She could feel the name's shape, its length, perhaps a vowel, and she couldn't make it into a sound.

@quote A18 rooftop_dusk | The meaning arrives. The sound does not. | A way to picture it
One way to put it is this. The meaning arrives. The sound does not. Marta has the man, the whole man, with all his meaning. What's missing is the final step of turning that meaning into a spoken word. It's like having the complete recipe in your head, and being unable to find the last ingredient, though you can see the jar.

@study A03 autumn_overlook | Harley & Bown, 1998 | The transmission-deficit idea | One explanation holds that a word's meaning and its sounds are stored and retrieved separately. In a tip-of-the-tongue state, activation reaches the meaning but does not pass on to the sounds.
Researchers have a model for this, sometimes called the transmission-deficit idea, with a version published by Harley and Bown in 1998. It says that the meaning of a word and its sounds are stored and retrieved separately. In a tip-of-the-tongue state, activation reaches the meaning, but doesn't quite pass on to the sounds. The signal travels most of the way and then fades before the last step.

@char A04 neon_alley_rain | Does this fit Marta? | Almost perfectly
It fits Marta's night rather well. For a person's name, the meaning is the person. Everything she knows about the man, the whole network of him, is lit up. But the sounds of his name sit at the end of a faint connection, used only now and then. The signal goes through the thick roads of meaning, and fades on the thin road of sound.

@photo lf45_b10_01.jpg | A ripple where the fish just turned
Think of a quiet pond at dusk. A fish has just turned beneath the surface, and you can see the ripples spreading out. You know something's down there. You can see exactly where it moved. But you can't see the fish itself. That's what it's like to be on the tip of the tongue. The ripple is the meaning, the first letter, the number of syllables. The fish is the word, still just under the surface.

@study A15 cafe_sunset | Schwartz, 1999 | Tip-of-the-tongue idioms around the world | A survey of 51 languages found that 45 have an idiom for the experience that refers to the tongue, mouth, or throat. American Sign Language users describe a tip-of-the-fingers experience.
And the experience seems to be universal. In a 1999 survey of 51 languages, the researcher Bennett Schwartz found that 45 of them have an idiom for it, using the tongue, the mouth or the throat. American Sign Language was one of the languages without such an idiom, and yet its users report a tip-of-the-fingers state, with the word almost on their hands.

@char A10 living_room_day | Everywhere, always | A shared human thing
That's a lovely detail. Across forty-five languages, people reached for the same picture, a word that's nearly out of the mouth but not quite. It suggests that Marta's feeling at two in the morning is one that's been felt in every country and every century. Nobody had to be told what it meant. The sensation has always been just that familiar.

@char A19 cabin_fireplace | And the name itself | Proper names are the usual victims
There's a pattern in what people get stuck on. Proper names are among the most common words on the tip of the tongue. In diary studies, where people logged every episode over weeks, most of the words that got stuck were proper nouns, often names of people they hadn't seen or spoken to for a while. Which is exactly what Marta's neighbour is. A name she uses rarely, from someone she doesn't talk to often.

@char A19 cabin_fireplace | Same for everyone | Bilingual speakers
One more finding is worth knowing, because it shows how special proper names are. Researchers have compared bilingual and monolingual speakers, who differ in how often they get stuck on ordinary words. Bilingual speakers have more tip-of-the-tongue states for most words, particularly in their less used language. But for proper names, a 2005 study by Gollan and colleagues found that bilingual and monolingual speakers got stuck equally often.

@char A10 living_room_day | Why that's interesting | Names are different
That's a lovely clue. For common words, having two languages to choose from makes retrieval harder, because the words compete. But proper names don't have a second-language competitor, and they get stuck at the same rate as for everyone else. It suggests that the trouble with names is a feature of names themselves, with their thin connections, and not of how many words a person has.

@char A12 street_day | Another pattern | Partial and alternates
Researchers have noticed another pattern. When younger adults get stuck, they more often report partial sound information about the target word, like its first sound or its length, and sometimes an alternative word that keeps coming to mind in its place, like exotic when the word you want is eccentric. Older adults report less of that. In general, the stuck state is one of partial information, with a few misleading substitutes.

@char A04 bedroom_night | Marta's substitutes | Wrong names
Marta has had that too. All evening, a few wrong names have kept coming forward, Adrian, Anders, Andrew, each one sounding a bit like it might be right, and none of them feeling quite correct. That's the typical feature of the tip of the tongue, the intruder words that sit exactly where the right word should be. They're a sign of how close, and how far, the mind is.

@char A12 autumn_overlook | A final point | Not a rare event
So the tip of the tongue isn't a strange breakdown. It's a regular visitor in healthy minds, a sign that the system is working in layers, with meaning and sound living in different places. When the layers connect, you speak without effort. When they don't, you feel the gap. Both are normal ways for a mind to behave.
