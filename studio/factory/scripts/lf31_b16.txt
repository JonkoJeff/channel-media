@chapter A01 autumn_overlook | Can a memory be trained?
We've spent the evening seeing how memory works. It's natural to wonder whether we can make it work better. The honest answer is a gentle yes, in some ways, and a gentle no, in others. And the difference is rather interesting.

@char A15 cafe_sunset | An old story | A poet and a collapsing roof
The oldest memory technique we know of comes with a legend. The Roman writer Cicero tells the story, in a book from about 55 BC, of a Greek poet called Simonides. He had stepped outside during a banquet, and while he was out, the roof fell in, and the guests were crushed beyond recognition.

@char A20 living_room_day | The poet remembers | By their places
According to the tale, Simonides was able to name every guest, because he remembered where each of them had been sitting. From that, the story goes, he realised that places could hold memories. Whether or not it happened, the lesson is real. Ancient orators used it to deliver long speeches without notes, and it's now called the method of loci, or the memory palace.

@list A12 rooftop_dusk | How the memory palace works | Picture a place you know very well ;; Walk through it in a fixed order ;; Put one vivid image at each spot ;; To recall, walk through again, and look
The method is simple. You picture a place you know very well, like your own home. You walk through it in a fixed order. At each spot, you place a vivid, slightly odd image of the thing to remember. Later, to recall, you take the same walk in your mind, and look at what's at each place.

@study A05 autumn_overlook | Eleanor Maguire, Elizabeth Valentine, John Wilding & Narinder Kapur | Published in Nature Neuroscience, 2003 | Scanned the brains of people ranked among the best memorisers in the world, and compared them with other volunteers
In 2003, Eleanor Maguire and colleagues studied people who compete in memory championships. Their brains did not look structurally different from other people's. But when they memorised, they used regions linked to visual memory and navigation. Nearly all of them said they used the method of loci. Their gift, in other words, seemed to be a strategy.

@study A02 cafe_sunset | Martin Dresler and colleagues | Published in Neuron, 2017 | Trained ordinary volunteers in the method of loci for about six weeks
In 2017, Martin Dresler and colleagues took it a step further. They trained ordinary volunteers in the method for about six weeks, half an hour a day. The number of words the trained volunteers could remember more than doubled, and the pattern of connections in their brains moved towards that of the memory champions.

@char A19 cabin_fireplace | What it is good for | And what it isn't
It's worth being clear about what this technique is good for. It's wonderful for lists, sequences and things that need to be recalled in order. It is less useful for understanding, which depends on meaning. The memory palace stores an item. It doesn't tell you why the item matters.

Many people find that the method works best with a place they know every corner of. A childhood home, a street to school. It's a nice thing that the places we remember so easily can also be used to hold whatever we need to keep.

@chapter A03 living_room_day | Do brain games help?
What about the games and apps that promise to train your brain? That's a question that was tested on an enormous scale.

@study A13 rooftop_dusk | Adrian Owen and colleagues | Published in Nature, 2010 | Asked more than eleven thousand people to practise online brain-training tasks several times a week for six weeks
In 2010, Adrian Owen and colleagues recruited 11,430 people to take part in an online study of brain training. For six weeks, they practised a set of tasks about memory, reasoning and attention, for at least ten minutes a day, three days a week. The people got much better at the tasks they practised. That was no surprise.

@char A02 autumn_overlook | The key question | Does it spread?
The key question was whether the improvement spread to other tasks that had not been practised, such as a general test of memory or reasoning. And the answer, in this study, was no. The gains stayed with the tasks themselves. Practice improved the game, not the player.

@study A12 cafe_sunset | Daniel Simons and colleagues | Published in Psychological Science in the Public Interest, 2016 | Reviewed the evidence offered by brain-training companies
In 2016, a team led by Daniel Simons reviewed the evidence put forward for commercial brain-training programs. They concluded that there was good evidence that practice improves the practised tasks, but little convincing evidence that it improves everyday cognition more broadly. Many studies, they noted, had flaws in their design.

@char A15 living_room_day | Where that leaves us | Be kind to your wallet
That doesn't mean that learning is pointless. Learning a language, an instrument, or a new city does change the brain. It just seems to be the learning itself that counts, in a rich and varied way, not a score on a puzzle. If you enjoy the puzzles, enjoy them. Just don't expect them to fix a bad night's sleep.

@chapter A19 cabin_fireplace | The simplest gift
Then there's something you may have heard a hundred times, and it turns out to have rather good evidence behind it.

@study A01 rooftop_dusk | Kirk Erickson and colleagues | Published in PNAS, 2011 | Compared a year of brisk walking with a year of gentle stretching in older adults
In 2011, Kirk Erickson and colleagues asked 120 older adults to take part in a year-long study. Half walked briskly for about forty minutes, three days a week. The other half did gentle stretching and toning. At the end of the year, the walkers' hippocampus was about two percent larger than at the start. In the stretching group, it had shrunk by about one and a half percent.

@char A20 living_room_day | And memory followed | A modest benefit
The walkers also did better on a test of spatial memory. The effects were modest, and the study was relatively small, so it's best seen as a promising sign, not a promise. But it fits a larger picture, in which moving your body seems to look after your brain, including the part that holds your memories.

@chapter A05 cafe_sunset | Pens and keyboards
One more everyday question. If you are trying to learn from a lecture, is it better to take notes by hand, or on a laptop?

@study A15 autumn_overlook | Pam Mueller & Daniel Oppenheimer | Published in Psychological Science, 2014 | Compared students who took notes by hand and on laptops, and tested them on the lecture
In 2014, Pam Mueller and Daniel Oppenheimer found that students who took notes by hand did better on questions about ideas than students who typed. They suggested that typists copy the lecture almost word for word, without thinking, while writers have to listen, choose and rephrase, which is deeper processing.

@char A12 rooftop_dusk | A fair ending | The effect shrank
But later studies tried to repeat it, and generally found a much smaller difference, or none. A fair summary is that how you take notes matters less than whether you think while doing it. If typing makes you copy, slow down and put it in your own words. If writing helps you do that, write.

@chapter A12 cafe_sunset | Say it out loud
Here's a tiny tip with some evidence behind it, and it costs nothing at all.

@study A02 autumn_overlook | Colin MacLeod and colleagues | Published in 2010 | Had people read words either silently or aloud, and tested which they remembered better
In 2010, Colin MacLeod and colleagues asked people to read lists of words, some silently and some aloud. Later, the words they had said aloud were remembered better than the ones they had read silently. It's called the production effect. The act of speaking seems to add something distinctive to the memory, a trace of having said it.

@char A20 living_room_day | A little distinctiveness | Your own voice
That fits the theme of tonight. A memory is strengthened by anything that makes it more yours, and more different from its neighbours. Saying it aloud, drawing it, acting it out, all add extra hooks. So if you have something important to remember, it doesn't hurt to mutter it to yourself.

@list A03 living_room_day | What actually seems to help | Spacing your practice ;; Testing yourself ;; Thinking about meaning ;; Using places and pictures for lists ;; Moving your body and sleeping well
So, a short list of what actually seems to help. Spacing your practice. Testing yourself. Thinking about meaning. Using places and pictures for lists. And looking after your body, with movement and sleep. It's not as exciting as a pill, but it works, and it's available tonight.

@char A13 bedroom_night | Almost time | To let it rest
And now we've come to the last part of the evening. All this talk of remembering, and training, and rebuilding. But the single most useful thing your memory can have tonight is the thing it's been waiting for all along. A bit of quiet.
