@chapter A13 cabin_fireplace | Myth: It's just a feeling, it means nothing
Let's take that idea seriously. It's just a feeling, and it means nothing. Plenty of people say it, kindly, to themselves and to each other. A feeling comes, a feeling goes, and there's no use reading too much into it. And in a way, that attitude can be steadying. But some psychologists have asked a bolder question. What if the feeling is not noise at all, but a message?

@char A19 attic_rain | A message in the body | Think of hunger
Think about hunger for a moment. When your stomach growls, you don't think, how strange, a meaningless sensation. You understand it. It's a signal that your body needs food, and it nudges you, quite firmly, to go and find some, even if you were busy with something else. Thirst works the same way. Neither one is pleasant.

@quote A18 cafe_sunset | Comfort wouldn't get us moving. | A thought on signals
And neither one is pointless. They're alarms that exist because the thing they point to matters for staying alive.

@char A12 living_room_day | A social species | Made for each other
Now consider that humans are, above almost everything else, a social species. For most of our history, a person on their own was in real danger. Groups shared food, kept watch, raised children and looked after the sick. Being connected wasn't a luxury. It was part of what kept people alive. Think about how long that lasted, across thousands of years, through winters and journeys and long nights around a fire.

@char A15 autumn_overlook | An old alarm | Built for company
A species like that might well have evolved a signal for when connection runs low.

@quote A18 cafe_sunset | Hunger and thirst protect our physical body while loneliness protects our social body. | Stephanie Cacioppo
That's exactly the idea put forward by John Cacioppo, the late psychologist who spent his career studying loneliness, and by his wife, the neuroscientist Stephanie Cacioppo. As she has put it, hunger and thirst protect our physical body, while loneliness protects our social body. On this view, loneliness isn't a malfunction. It's an alarm that our ancestors may have needed, a nudge to turn back toward other people.

@char A15 street_day | A book on the subject | Cacioppo and Patrick
John Cacioppo and William Patrick set the idea out for a general audience in a book published in 2008, called Loneliness: Human Nature and the Need for Social Connection. Its main message is gentle. The sting of loneliness may be the very thing that encourages us to reach out. In other words, the discomfort has a job. It's asking something of us, and it's asking quietly.

@char A09 bedroom_night | What it asks | Reconnect
So what does the signal ask for? Not much, really. It doesn't ask us to become more charming or more outgoing. It asks us to turn toward others again, and to be turned toward. It's the same as hunger asking for food, or thirst asking for water. It points at a need, and then it waits for us to notice. That is far from meaningless. It's one of the most human messages there is.

@list A10 rooftop_dusk | Two sides of one signal | Hunger says: find food ;; Thirst says: find water ;; Loneliness says: find your people
Here's the comparison side by side. Hunger says, find food. Thirst says, find water. And on this view, loneliness says, find your people. Every one of these feels uncomfortable on purpose, because comfort wouldn't get us moving. It's not that the feeling is the problem. The feeling is the messenger, and the need behind it is the thing to listen to.

@char A03 attic_rain | Tomas hears it | Tonight's message
Back on the sofa, Tomas thinks about this. His uneasy Sunday evening starts to look a little different. It isn't a mood to be embarrassed about, or a weakness to hide. It's his own system telling him, quite sensibly, that he misses real contact, and that it would like some soon. Nobody needs to rush.

@char A12 living_room_day | Hold it gently | Say hello to it
He doesn't have to obey it fiercely. He can simply say, all right, I hear you.

@chapter A19 living_room_day | Can it be tested? | A fair question
Of course, a beautiful idea isn't a proven one. A careful listener might ask whether anyone has actually checked that loneliness behaves like hunger. Does the brain treat missing company the way it treats missing food? It's a hard thing to test, because you have to create a little loneliness on purpose, ethically, and then look inside the mind. But a team of researchers found a way.

@char A12 cafe_sunset | The setup | Ten hours, two days
The researchers, led by Livia Tomova, invited forty healthy young adults to take part. On one day, each person spent ten hours in complete social isolation. On another day, they spent ten hours fasting, with no food.

@char A13 attic_rain | In the scanner | Pictures of people, pictures of food
After each day, the participants lay in a brain scanner, and were shown pictures of people enjoying each other's company, and pictures of tempting food.

@study A18 rooftop_dusk | Tomova and colleagues | Nature Neuroscience, published 2020 | After 10 hours of isolation, a midbrain region responded to social cues much as it did to food cues after fasting
Here is what they found. After ten hours of isolation, a small region in the midbrain, rich in cells that produce dopamine and linked to wanting and craving, responded to pictures of people being together. And after ten hours without food, the same region responded to pictures of food. The two responses were similar. In a very real sense, a brain missing company seemed to crave it, a little like a hungry brain craves a meal.

@char A15 autumn_overlook | Reading it carefully | Similar, not identical
It's worth reading this with some care. The responses were similar in that one midbrain region, but other parts of the brain responded differently to food craving and to social craving, so this isn't a claim that loneliness is hunger. And the people were healthy young adults, isolated for just one day. Even so, there's something real here.

@char A10 cabin_fireplace | What people said | Missing company
After isolation the participants reported feeling more social craving, more loneliness and more discomfort, and the brain's response was stronger in those who wanted contact most.

@char A10 bedroom_night | What this means | A real need
If the study points where the researchers think it does, then loneliness isn't weakness. It's closer to a need that has gone unmet for a while, and which the brain responds to in an ancient way. That matters, because it changes the tone of the whole conversation. That is a gentle thing to sit with, and a good thing to remember on a quiet evening.

@char A09 bedroom_night | A fair comparison | Nobody scolds a hungry person
We would never tell a hungry person that their appetite is meaningless, or that they ought to be above it.

@char A13 living_room_day | Not a command | Information
But it's also worth remembering what a signal is. A signal is information, and not an instruction to panic. Hunger doesn't mean you're starving, and a flash of loneliness doesn't mean something is badly wrong. It means a need is near the surface. Tomas can hold it the way he'd hold a rumbling stomach on a busy afternoon, with calm, and a plan for something nourishing a little later.

@list A12 cafe_sunset | What the second myth tells us | Loneliness may work like a signal ;; Hunger and thirst are signals too ;; A brain missing company can show craving ;; The signal is information, not a flaw
Let's gather the second myth. Loneliness may be less like a random mood, and more like a signal. Hunger and thirst are signals that protect the body, and loneliness may protect the social side of us. In one experiment, a brain deprived of company showed craving in a region that also responds to food. And the signal is information, rather than a flaw.

@char A18 rooftop_dusk | A kind of relief | You're built this way
There's a quiet relief in this, and it's worth letting it settle. If you've ever felt lonely and thought, what's wrong with me, the answer may be, nothing at all. You're a social creature, and a social creature feels the lack of company the way it feels the lack of food. The feeling is what it looks like when your body takes connection seriously.

@quote A09 attic_rain | It feels less like a thought, and more like something in the body. | Tomas, tonight
But signals can be uncomfortable, and loneliness can truly sting. That's part of how they get our attention. Tomas can feel it in his chest tonight, a dull heaviness, a tightness he doesn't have a good word for. And that raises the next question. If loneliness is a signal, why does it hurt in that particular way? Why does it feel less like a thought, and more like something in the body?

@char A19 cabin_fireplace | A word we all use | Hurt feelings
Notice how we talk. We say hurt feelings. We say a broken heart. We say that a remark stung, or that someone was cold, or that being left out was painful. We use the language of bodily pain for social situations without a second thought. And that might not be a coincidence, because the brain seems to treat the two more alike than you'd think.
