@chapter N27 rooftop_dusk | Larks and owls
Scientists have a word for the time of day your body prefers, and it turns out the differences between us are real, measurable, and mostly not a choice.

@char N19 cafe_sunset | Chronotype | A word for your natural rhythm
The word is chronotype. It describes your natural preference for sleeping and waking, from very early to very late, and it varies smoothly across the population, with most people somewhere in the middle. Genes seem to account for a good share of it, and so does age. Children tend to be early, teenagers drift later, and most adults slowly move earlier again after their twenties.

@study N11 cafe_sunset | Till Roenneberg and colleagues | Current Biology, 2004 | Surveys of more than twenty thousand people in Central Europe showed that sleep timing moves later through the teenage years, peaks around age twenty, and then gradually moves earlier
In 2004, the German chronobiologist Till Roenneberg reported that the timing of sleep shifts across a lifetime in a very regular way. Using thousands of questionnaires from people of all ages, he found that sleep timing gets later through adolescence, peaks at around the age of twenty, and then slowly moves earlier. He even suggested that the peak is a handy marker of the end of adolescence.

@char N09 bedroom_night | A very common mismatch | Social jetlag
The trouble, Roenneberg pointed out, is that our society runs on one schedule, the alarm clock, while our bodies run on many. A late chronotype who has to be at work by eight is, in a very real sense, living in a different time zone from their own biology. His team gave this a name that has stuck, social jetlag, and it was described in a 2006 paper with Anna Wittmann.

@char N02 living_room_day | How it shows up | Weekend sleep-ins
You can spot it in people who sleep long on weekends. If your body clock wanted to run an hour or two later all along, then on free days you let it, and you wake up much later than during the week. The difference between those two schedules is a rough measure of social jetlag, and across many surveys, it's common, especially among the young.

@char N13 attic_rain | A careful note | A lark isn't a better person
It's worth adding that researchers don't see larks as better or owls as worse. There's some research linking late chronotypes with poorer outcomes on average, but much of that may come from the mismatch with early schedules, not from the late timing itself. Given a free schedule, many owls do perfectly well. It's often the world that is tuned for the lark.

@char N10 autumn_overlook | Practical hope | A clock that can shift
The good news is that the clock is not fixed. We saw earlier that campers moved about two hours earlier in a week without light from lamps. Researchers have also shown that more morning light, regular meal times and consistent wake times can nudge it. It takes patience, because the clock moves slowly, but it does move.

@chapter N20 cabin_fireplace | Is eight hours a rule?
Which brings us to the question that Nora keeps asking herself at three in the morning. How much sleep does she actually need, and how badly is she failing?

@char N10 bedroom_night | A familiar number | Eight hours
Almost everyone has heard it. Eight hours a night. It's printed on posters, repeated by doctors, and quoted in newspapers. And so, when Nora checks the clock and works out that she'll get only five hours if she falls asleep right now, a small wave of panic arrives, which, as we'll see, may be the least helpful thing of all.

@char N09 cafe_sunset | Where eight came from | A convenient average
Sleep scientists say there is no magic in the number eight. It sits near the middle of the range that typically leaves most adults well rested. In 2015, expert panels from the American Academy of Sleep Medicine and the Sleep Research Society reviewed hundreds of studies and concluded that adults should regularly sleep seven or more hours, while noting that individuals vary.

@study N07 cabin_fireplace | Daniel Kripke and colleagues | Archives of General Psychiatry, 2002 | A survey of more than one million American adults followed for six years found that those who reported sleeping about six and a half to seven and a half hours had the lowest death rates
A famous study from 2002, led by Daniel Kripke, followed more than a million adults for six years. It found that people who reported sleeping around six and a half to seven and a half hours had the lowest death rate, and that people who reported sleeping eight hours or more, or less than about six, had a higher one. It's a big, well-known result, but it has important limits.

@char N23 living_room_day | The limits | Association, not instruction
The study relied on people reporting their own sleep, and it observed associations. It doesn't show that sleeping eight hours causes harm, or that cutting back would help. People who are ill often sleep longer, which could explain part of the pattern. The study's real message is that the healthiest range seems to lie around the middle, and that extremes deserve attention.

@char N10 rooftop_dusk | Sleeping the old way | A study in three groups
Another set of clues comes from people who live without electric light. In 2015, Gandhi Yetish and colleagues recorded sleep in three groups living traditional lives, the Hadza of Tanzania, the San of Namibia, and the Tsimane of Bolivia, using wrist devices over many days. These communities didn't sleep the amount that we'd assume was natural.

@stat N11 autumn_overlook | Sleep in three traditional societies | About 6 to 7 hours | average nightly sleep recorded by wrist devices, in communities with no electric light
Their average sleep was around six to seven hours a night, shorter than the usual eight-hour advice, and they reported almost no trouble with insomnia. They also took few naps. The researchers' point was that natural sleep, in the full dark and the full daylight, doesn't seem to be much longer than the modern recommendation, and that sleeping a bit less than eight hours is not necessarily a sign of something wrong.

@char N19 autumn_overlook | A rare group | The short sleepers
At the far end of the range are a rare handful of people who sleep for short periods and feel fine. In 2009, Ying-Hui Fu and Louis Ptáček reported a gene variant in one family that was linked to this natural short sleep, and later studies found more such variants. They're extremely rare, and the lesson isn't that anyone can train themselves to sleep four hours. It's that need varies, and some of that variation is inherited.

@char N02 living_room_day | A better yardstick | How you feel in the day
So how do you know whether you're getting enough? Sleep experts often suggest looking at the day instead of the number. Do you wake up reasonably refreshed? Can you stay alert through a quiet afternoon without a lot of caffeine? Do you fall asleep within a few minutes of sitting down in a warm room? If the answers are mostly yes, your own number may be fine, even if it's a bit below the textbook.

@char N09 cabin_fireplace | Age changes it | Not the same at every age
The recommended range also changes across life. Teenagers need more, around eight to ten hours according to the guidelines of the American Academy of Sleep Medicine, while older adults are often advised seven to eight. Many older adults find that their sleep gets lighter and more broken, which is a normal part of ageing, and not always a disorder, although it's worth mentioning to a doctor if it troubles you.

@char N10 bedroom_night | Back to Nora | The hidden panic
So, what's the lesson for Nora? That the right amount of sleep is a range, not a number, and that her own need probably sits somewhere in it. And that adding fear about the total to an already restless night may be one of the surest ways of keeping herself awake, which is the next thing we'll look into.
