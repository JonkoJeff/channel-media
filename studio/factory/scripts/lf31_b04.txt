@chapter A05 living_room_day | What decides what stays
Here's a question that sounds simple. If a thing gets through the gate, and sits in the workspace for a few seconds, what makes it stay for years? For a long time, people assumed the answer was repetition. Say it enough times, and it sticks. It turns out that something else matters even more.

@char A15 cafe_sunset | Not how often | How deeply
The answer, in a word, is depth. Not how many times you went over something, but what your mind did with it while it was there. Did you just let it wash past? Or did you think about what it meant, connect it to something you knew, and make it matter to you?

@study A02 rooftop_dusk | Fergus Craik & Robert Lockhart | Published in 1972 | Proposed that how well something is remembered depends on how deeply it is processed, from shallow features to meaning
In 1972, Fergus Craik and Robert Lockhart proposed what they called levels of processing. Shallow processing is paying attention to the surface, like what the letters look like. Deeper processing is paying attention to the sounds, and deepest of all is paying attention to what something means. The deeper you go, they suggested, the stronger the trace.

@study A12 autumn_overlook | Fergus Craik & Endel Tulving | Published in 1975 | Asked people different kinds of questions about words, then tested memory
Three years later, Craik and Endel Tulving put it to a test. They showed people words, one at a time, and asked a different question about each. Is it written in capital letters? Does it rhyme with a certain word? Does it fit into a given sentence? Afterwards, they tested how many of the words people could pick out.

@char A20 living_room_day | The result | Meaning wins
The words that had been thought about for their meaning, the ones that were asked about in a sentence, were remembered far better than those where people had only looked at the letters. Nobody was told to memorise anything. The only difference was the kind of thinking each word had received.

@char A01 cafe_sunset | Why rereading disappoints | A comfortable trap
This explains a very common experience. You read a page three times, and it feels familiar, but a week later you couldn't tell anyone what it said. Rereading is mostly shallow. The words slide past, and the sense of familiarity tricks you into thinking you've learned them.

@chapter A19 cabin_fireplace | The coin in your pocket
Now here is a gentler way to see the same idea, with an object you might have seen a thousand times.

@study A13 rooftop_dusk | Raymond Nickerson & Marilyn Adams | Published in 1979 | Asked Americans to recall and recognise the design of a penny
In 1979, Raymond Nickerson and Marilyn Adams asked people in the United States to draw a one-cent coin from memory, the penny. They had handled it for years. Yet most drawings got details wrong, or misplaced them, and when people were shown versions of the coin, many couldn't pick the real one from the imitations.

@char A05 living_room_day | Seen, but never looked at | No reason to remember
The explanation isn't that people have bad memories. It's that you never need the details of a penny. You only need to know it's a penny. So your mind, quite sensibly, files it at the shallowest possible level, and never bothers with the rest.

@study A15 autumn_overlook | Adam Blake, Meenely Nazarian & Alan Castel | Published in 2015 | Asked students to draw the logo of a brand they see every day
In 2015, Adam Blake, Meenely Nazarian and Alan Castel ran a modern version of the same test. They asked 85 students to draw a very famous logo, an apple with a bite taken out. Only one of the 85 drew it correctly. Many students put the leaf in the wrong place, or forgot it. Even people who owned the brand's products did not do much better.

It's a gentle reminder that familiarity and memory are different things. You can see something every day for years, and still not hold the picture in your mind. What you've stored is the label, not the picture.

@chapter A12 cafe_sunset | Making it about you
There's another route to depth, and it's one most of us use without noticing. We remember what feels personal.

@study A02 rooftop_dusk | Timothy Rogers, Nicholas Kuiper & William Kirker | Published in 1977 | Asked people to judge words about themselves, about other things, and then tested memory
In 1977, Timothy Rogers and colleagues showed volunteers adjectives, and asked different questions. Does this word describe you? Does it mean the same as another word? Does it rhyme? The words that people had considered in relation to themselves were remembered best of all. This became known as the self-reference effect.

@char A20 cabin_fireplace | Why it works | A hook in your own story
One idea is that you already have a very rich web of knowledge about yourself, and linking something to it gives the new thing many hooks to hang from. A fact about the world is a loose thread. A fact about you is a thread already woven into the cloth.

@study A01 living_room_day | Norman Slamecka & Peter Graf | Published in 1978 | Compared words people read with words they produced themselves
There's a close cousin of this. In 1978, Norman Slamecka and Peter Graf asked volunteers either to read a word, or to produce it themselves from a clue, such as a rhyme. Words that people generated were later remembered better than words they had simply read. This is the generation effect. Effort of the right kind seems to help.

@chapter A19 cabin_fireplace | When your heart is involved
And then there's the most powerful booster of all, one that no one needs to be taught.

@study A10 rooftop_dusk | Larry Cahill, Bruce Prins, Michael Weber & James McGaugh | Published in Nature, 1994 | Gave people a drug that blunts the body's stress response, then tested memory for an emotional story
In 1994, Larry Cahill and James McGaugh and colleagues told volunteers a slide story with a dramatic middle and a neutral version. A drug that blunts the stress hormone's effect, propranolol, made people less able to remember the emotional version. For the neutral version, the drug made no difference. Emotion, it seemed, was giving the memory a push.

@char A05 autumn_overlook | A push, not a guarantee | Be careful here
It's worth being careful about what this means. Emotion tends to strengthen the central gist of an event, the feeling of it, what mattered. It doesn't promise that every detail is correct. We'll see in a while that a memory can be intense and still wrong in places.

@chapter A05 cafe_sunset | The camera in your pocket
Here's a very modern question about what makes it into memory. When you take a photo of something, do you remember it better, or worse?

@study A15 rooftop_dusk | Linda Henkel | Published in Psychological Science, 2014 | Took students on a museum tour, asking them to photograph some objects and just observe others
In 2014, Linda Henkel took students on a tour of a university museum. She asked them to look at some objects, and to photograph others. The next day, the students remembered fewer of the objects they had photographed, and fewer details about them, than the ones they had simply looked at.

@char A01 living_room_day | The photo effect | Handing it over
The idea is that taking a photo can feel like handing the job of remembering to the camera. Your attention relaxes, because the picture will do the remembering. Interestingly, when students zoomed in on a part of the object with the camera, their memory for that part held up, because zooming took attention.

@study A12 autumn_overlook | Betsy Sparrow, Jenny Liu & Daniel Wegner | Published in Science, 2011 | Asked people to type facts into a computer, and told some that the facts would be saved
In 2011, Betsy Sparrow and colleagues told volunteers to type trivia facts into a computer. Some were told the computer would save them, and some that it would be erased. Those who thought the facts were saved remembered fewer of them. It was called the Google effect on memory.

@char A20 cafe_sunset | A fair warning | Not all findings last
In the interest of honesty, you should know that this result did not hold up well. In 2018, a large team that tried to repeat 21 social science experiments published in Nature and Science found that the effect did not repeat for this one. The photo effect has fared better, but the lesson is that it's wise to wait for repeats.

@chapter A12 rooftop_dusk | The one that stands out
Attention has one more habit that you can use. It loves what is different.

@study A02 autumn_overlook | Hedwig von Restorff | Published in 1933 | Showed people lists of items in which one item looked different from the rest
In 1933, the German psychologist Hedwig von Restorff gave people lists of items, and made one of them stand out, by printing it in a different colour, or placing a number among words. People remembered the odd one out much better than the others. It's known as the isolation effect, or the von Restorff effect.

@char A20 cafe_sunset | Why novelty sticks | The odd one out
Our minds seem to be tuned to notice what is unexpected, because the unexpected is usually the thing worth knowing. A red apple among green ones. A single wrong note in a familiar tune. This is why a funny, odd or unusual image is easier to remember than a plain one, and why the memory palace we will meet later uses strange pictures.

@list A15 living_room_day | Ways to go deeper | Ask what it means ;; Link it to something you know ;; Link it to your own life ;; Say it in your own words ;; Let it matter to you
So, if you ever wanted a short recipe from this stop, it might look like this. Ask what something means. Link it to what you already know. Link it to your own life. Say it in your own words. And let yourself care about it, if you can.
