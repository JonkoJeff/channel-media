@chapter N20 living_room_day | Why you can't tickle yourself
Nadia stops at a pedestrian crossing and waits for the little green figure. Out of curiosity, she runs a fingertip lightly along the inside of her own wrist, a place that used to make her squirm when her father did it. Nothing. Not even a twitch. Her own touch is just touch. The lights change, and she crosses, wondering why.

@char N14 attic_rain | An old puzzle | Noticed for centuries
People have wondered about this for a very long time. Aristotle, more than two thousand years ago, is often said to have asked why nobody can tickle themselves. Charles Darwin discussed tickling in his 1872 book on the expression of emotions. But it took modern brain science to offer a convincing answer.

@study N01 rooftop_dusk | Sarah-Jayne Blakemore, Daniel Wolpert and Chris Frith | Nature Neuroscience, 1998 | Central cancellation of self-produced tickle sensation
In 1998, a team in London led by Sarah-Jayne Blakemore studied this directly. Volunteers lay in a brain scanner while a soft piece of foam brushed across their palm. Sometimes they moved the foam themselves, using a little handle. Sometimes the experimenter moved it. Same foam, same palm, same movement, but a different person in charge.

@char A03 autumn_overlook | Same touch, different brain | The brain turns it down
The brain responded differently. When the touch was self-made, areas that process touch were less active than when the same touch came from someone else. And a part of the brain at the back of the head, called the cerebellum, seemed to be involved in predicting the sensation. Your brain, in effect, saw your own touch coming and turned the volume down.

@char A10 living_room_day | Why the brain does this | Predicting your own movements
Why would it do that? One leading idea is that the brain is constantly predicting the results of our own movements. Every time you move, your brain makes a quiet forecast of what you'll feel. If the feeling matches the forecast, it's treated as less important and dimmed. That leaves more attention for surprises, the touches that come from outside.

@list A19 attic_rain | Why this prediction is useful | We'd notice every brush of our own clothes ;; Our own movements would feel strange ;; Outside touches would be harder to spot
Think how useful that is. Without it, you'd be constantly startled by your own hands, your own clothes, your own hair brushing your face. Your movements would feel noisy and strange. And it would be much harder to notice the touch that really matters: a bug landing on your arm, or a hand on your shoulder from behind.

@study A05 cafe_sunset | Sarah-Jayne Blakemore, Chris Frith and Daniel Wolpert | Journal of Cognitive Neuroscience, 1999 | Spatio-temporal prediction modulates the perception of self-produced stimuli
In a clever follow-up study published in 1999, the team made the prediction harder. Volunteers moved a handle that controlled a robot, which then brushed their palm. The researchers could add a short delay, or change the direction of the robot's movement, so that the touch no longer matched exactly what the person had done.

@char N27 cabin_fireplace | A small delay | And the tickle came back
The results were neat. The bigger the mismatch, the more ticklish the self-made touch felt. Even a delay of a fraction of a second, a fifth of a second or so, made people's own touch feel noticeably more ticklish. When the brain's prediction was thrown off, the touch felt more like it came from someone else.

It's a fitting place for this puzzle. Every step she takes, her brain is predicting the feel of her own feet on the ground, and quietly turning it down.

@char N09 living_room_day | Tickling needs surprise | And another person
So tickling seems to need a degree of surprise, something the brain can't perfectly forecast. That's why tickling from another person works, and why the best ticklers, like Nadia's father, are a little unpredictable. You never quite know where the next poke is going to land, and that uncertainty is part of the fun.

@char A04 conspiracy_board | Is tickling really funny? | An old theory
There's another tickle puzzle that's worth a look. In the nineteenth century, Darwin and the German scientist Ewald Hecker both suggested that tickling and humour are closely related, that a good joke tickles the mind the way fingers tickle the ribs. This became known as the Darwin-Hecker hypothesis. If it were true, being in a jolly mood should make you more ticklish.

@study N20 cafe_sunset | Christine Harris and Nicholas Christenfeld | Cognition and Emotion, 1997 | Humour, tickle, and the Darwin-Hecker hypothesis
In 1997, the psychologists Christine Harris and Nicholas Christenfeld tested this. Some volunteers watched comedy clips before being tickled, others didn't. If humour warmed people up for tickling, the comedy group should laugh more when tickled. The researchers then compared how much each group laughed during the tickling.

@char A13 attic_rain | No warm-up effect | Tickle and humour may differ
Watching comedy didn't make people any more ticklish. The jolly mood simply didn't carry over to the ribs. On this evidence, tickle laughter and humour laughter may be more separate than Darwin and Hecker thought, two different routes into the same noisy outburst.

@char N01 cabin_fireplace | Laughing, but enjoying it? | Not always
Harris also pointed out that people who are tickled often laugh even when they don't seem to be enjoying it much. Laughter during tickling may be partly a reflex, a bit like flinching. That fits with what many of us remember. As a child, you might have laughed and begged for it to stop, both at the same time, and meant both.

@list A03 bedroom_night | What tickling teaches us | The brain dims our own touch ;; Tickling needs surprise ;; Tickle laughter may be partly reflex ;; It may differ from laughing at jokes
So tickling teaches us several things. The brain dims touches it can predict, which is why we can't tickle ourselves. Tickling needs a little surprise, and usually another person. The laughter it produces may be partly automatic. And it may not be quite the same thing as laughter at a joke, even if it sounds alike.

@char A12 rooftop_dusk | A family memory | Nadia's father
Nadia smiles at the memory of her father's tickle attacks. She realises he always started with the same warning, here comes the tickle monster, and that the warning itself used to make her laugh before he'd even touched her. Perhaps that was the surprise working in advance. Or perhaps it was just the joy of being chased by someone who loved her.

Every human being laughs for the first time at some point, usually in the first few months of life. What makes a baby laugh, before it understands a single word? That's where we're going next.
