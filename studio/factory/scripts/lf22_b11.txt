@chapter A12 cafe_sunset | Myth eleven: closeness just takes years
We often think of deep closeness as something that can only grow slowly, over many years. Old friends, long marriages, the people who've known us forever. And there's truth in that. Time matters. But researchers have found that the way people spend their time together may matter just as much. And one study suggested that the right kind of conversation can create a surprising sense of closeness in under an hour.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Aron, Melinat, Aron, Vallone & Bator | Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin, 1997 | The experimental generation of interpersonal closeness: a procedure and some preliminary findings
In 1997, Arthur Aron, Elaine Aron and their colleagues paired up students who were strangers. For about forty-five minutes, the pairs took turns asking each other a set of thirty-six questions, given to them in three sets. The questions started light and gradually became more personal.

@list A12 living_room_day | How the questions build | Early: given the choice of anyone in the world, whom would you want as a dinner guest? ;; Middle: what do you value most in a friendship? ;; Later: what is your most treasured memory? ;; Last: share a personal problem and ask your partner's advice
Early questions were gentle, like, given the choice of anyone in the world, whom would you want as a dinner guest? Later ones went deeper, like, what is your most treasured memory? And near the end, people were asked to share a personal problem and ask for the other person's advice on how they might handle it.

@char A03 cafe_sunset | The result | Closer than small talk
Other pairs spent the same amount of time making small talk, with questions like, what was the best gift you ever received and why? Afterwards, the pairs who had answered the gradually deepening questions felt noticeably closer to each other than those who had made small talk. The questions became famous years later, often called the thirty-six questions to fall in love.

@char A13 rooftop_dusk | Not a love spell | Closeness, not romance
It's worth saying clearly that the study was about closeness, not love. Most pairs didn't fall in love. But they did feel genuinely closer, more quickly than you might expect from a single conversation with a stranger. The key wasn't the specific questions. It was the gradual, mutual opening up.

@chapter A15 cabin_fireplace | Opening up, together
That idea of mutual opening up has a long history in psychology, and a well-known review put numbers on it.

@study A04 attic_rain | Collins & Miller | Psychological Bulletin, 1994 | Self-disclosure and liking: a meta-analytic review
In 1994, Nancy Collins and Lynn Carol Miller combined many studies on self-disclosure, sharing personal information about ourselves. They found three linked patterns. People who disclose more tend to be liked more. People tend to disclose more to those they already like. And people tend to like others more after disclosing to them.

@char A12 living_room_day | A gentle spiral | Sharing and liking
So sharing and liking feed each other, in a gentle upward spiral. You share a little. The other person responds warmly and shares something back. You both feel a little closer, and so you share a little more. That's how many close relationships grow. Not in one leap, but in small steps, taken together.

@char A10 cabin_fireplace | Feeling understood | Responsiveness matters most
Researchers like Harry Reis and Phillip Shaver have argued that the most important part of this process isn't the sharing itself. It's how the other person responds. When you share something and feel understood, valued and cared for, closeness grows. When you share and feel ignored or judged, it doesn't. Reis calls this perceived partner responsiveness.

@chapter A13 rooftop_dusk | The power of a question
And there's a simple way to show responsiveness that most of us don't use nearly enough. Asking questions.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Huang, Yeomans, Brooks, Minson & Gino | Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 2017 | It doesn't hurt to ask: question-asking increases liking
In 2017, Karen Huang and her colleagues at Harvard studied conversations between strangers, including chats online and real speed dates. People who asked more questions, especially follow-up questions that built on what the other person had just said, were liked more by their conversation partners.

@char A03 cafe_sunset | Follow-up questions | I'm listening
Follow-up questions seem to be especially powerful because they show that you were really listening. Oh, you grew up by the sea, what was that like? It's a small thing. But it tells the other person that their words landed, and that you want to know more about them.

@chapter A15 cafe_sunset | We underestimate deep talk
So deeper conversation brings people closer. Why don't we do it more often? A study published in 2022 offered an answer.

@study A04 attic_rain | Kardas, Kumar & Epley | Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 2022 | Overly shallow? Miscalibrated expectations create a barrier to deeper conversation
Michael Kardas, Amit Kumar and Nicholas Epley asked strangers to have either shallow or deep conversations with each other. Before talking, people predicted how awkward the conversations would be and how much they'd enjoy them. Afterwards, they reported how it had actually gone.

@char A12 living_room_day | Better than expected | Less awkward, more connecting
People consistently expected deep conversations to be more awkward and less enjoyable than they turned out to be. They also underestimated how interested their partners would be in hearing about their thoughts and feelings. In reality, the deep conversations left people feeling more connected, and they weren't nearly as uncomfortable as feared.

@char A10 attic_rain | A quiet barrier | Fear keeps us shallow
So one reason we stick to small talk is a simple misjudgement. We think others don't want to go deeper, and they think the same about us. Everyone stays politely on the surface, when many of us would actually welcome something more meaningful.

@chapter A12 cafe_sunset | How many hours?
Still, depth isn't everything. Time does matter. And one researcher tried to work out roughly how much of it friendship takes.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Jeffrey Hall | Journal of Social and Personal Relationships, 2019 | How many hours does it take to make a friend?
In 2019, Jeffrey Hall at the University of Kansas surveyed adults who had recently moved to a new city, and students starting university. He asked them about the new people they had met, how much time they had spent together, and how close they had become.

@bars A12 living_room_day | Rough hours spent together | hours | Casual friend:50 ;; Friend:90 ;; Close friend:200
His estimates suggested that it took roughly fifty hours together to go from acquaintance to casual friend, around ninety hours to become a friend, and more than two hundred hours to become a close friend. And it mattered how the time was spent. Time spent joking, catching up and talking about meaningful things helped more than time spent just working side by side.

@photo lf22_b11_01.jpg | Time spent together
So closeness takes both. Time, yes, often a lot of it. But also the right kind of time. Conversations that go a little deeper, questions that show we're listening, and the willingness to share something real and respond warmly when someone shares with us.

@list A13 rooftop_dusk | How closeness grows | Gradually deepening conversation ;; Taking turns to open up ;; Responding with warmth and understanding ;; Asking follow-up questions ;; Spending real time together
Gradually deepening conversation. Taking turns to open up. Responding with warmth. Asking follow-up questions. And spending real time together. None of these are secrets. But knowing that they work, and that most people would welcome them more than we expect, might give us a little courage to try.
