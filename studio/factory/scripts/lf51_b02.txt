@chapter N10 living_room_day | An old, old feeling
Let's wander back through history for a while, because boredom turns out to be a much older companion than the waiting room, the smartphone or the office.

@char N19 autumn_overlook | A restless monk | Fourth-century Egypt
Around the fourth century, in the deserts of Egypt, there lived monks who had chosen a life of prayer, silence and very little else. One of them, a thinker named Evagrius Ponticus, wrote about a particular trouble that visited the monks in the middle of the day. He called it acedia, and later writers nicknamed it the noonday demon.

@char N09 cabin_fireplace | The noonday demon | Restless in the afternoon
As Evagrius described it, the sun seemed to stand still, the day seemed fifty hours long, and the monk kept looking out of his cell to see whether anyone was coming. He felt dislike for the place, for the work, and even for his own life. Many modern scholars read this as an early portrait of something very like boredom, although acedia also carried religious meanings, and historians still debate how closely the two match.

@char N02 living_room_day | A careful note | Not every word means the same
It's worth being careful here. Old words and modern words rarely line up perfectly. Acedia was part of a moral and spiritual vocabulary, and it covered sorrow, weariness and neglect of duty as well. So when we say the monks were bored, we mean that something recognisable in their descriptions has a family resemblance to what we feel today, not that the two are identical.

@char N23 rooftop_dusk | A Roman complaint | Seneca on sameness
The same family resemblance appears in ancient Rome. The philosopher Seneca, writing in the first century, described a kind of weariness with life that he linked to repetition. In one of his letters he paraphrases the thought of a person asking, how long will it be the same things? I will wake, I will sleep, I will eat, I will be cold, I will be hot. Nothing ever ends.

@char N10 cabin_fireplace | Sameness | The heart of it
That complaint, how long will it be the same things, is remarkably close to what researchers now say about boredom. When experience stops changing, when nothing new arrives, the mind begins to protest. Seneca had no scanner and no questionnaire, but he had noticed that sameness itself can feel like a weight.

@quote N27 rooftop_dusk | Boredom is the root of all evil. | Søren Kierkegaard, 1843
Fast forward to 1843, and the Danish philosopher Søren Kierkegaard wrote a line that has been quoted ever since, that boredom is the root of all evil. He wasn't giving a scientific verdict, of course, he was a philosopher writing in a very dramatic voice. But it shows how seriously thinkers took the feeling long before psychology existed.

@char N19 cafe_sunset | A new word | Dickens and the Victorians
And then there's the word itself. The English word boredom is often said to have first appeared in print in the early 1850s, and Charles Dickens's novel Bleak House, published in 1852 and 1853, is frequently credited with introducing it. Dictionaries and scholars disagree on the details, so it's safest to say that the word is surprisingly young, even if the feeling is not.

@char N09 living_room_day | A young word | A feeling gets a name
Why would a word arrive so late? One idea, defended by historians such as Patricia Meyer Spacks, in her 1995 book Boredom: The Literary History of a State of Mind, is that as society changed, the experience became more noticeable and more worth naming. People had more free time, more choices, and more expectation that life should be interesting, and a feeling that now had a place to appear began to need a word.

@char N10 living_room_day | Not everyone agrees | A lively debate
Not every scholar agrees that boredom is a modern invention. The classicist Peter Toohey, in his 2011 book Boredom: A Lively History, argued that boredom has been with humans much longer, and that ancient writers described its symptoms clearly, even if they used other words. Between them, the two views make a useful pair. The feeling may be ancient, while our way of talking about it is newer.

@char N02 cabin_fireplace | A pattern | Sameness plus expectation
If we put the stories together, a pattern appears. Boredom seems to arrive whenever two things meet. On one side is monotony, the sameness of days, the repetition of work or the emptiness of waiting. On the other side is expectation, the sense that our time ought to be filled with something worthwhile.

@char N23 autumn_overlook | A modern edge | Expectation has grown
That second ingredient may be growing. We now carry devices that can fill almost any gap in a few seconds, so an empty moment feels stranger than it used to. The monk looked out of his window to see if anyone was coming. We look at a screen to see if anything has changed. The reach is the same, and only the window is different.

@char N19 living_room_day | A cautious thought | Is it really worse today?
Whether people today are more bored than people in the past is a surprisingly hard question to test. We can't give a questionnaire to the fourth century, and surveys over the last few decades measure different things in different ways. So it's wise to be cautious about any headline that tells us we live in an unprecedented age of boredom.

@char N10 rooftop_dusk | What does seem clear | A lasting human thing
What does seem clear is that the feeling is widely recognisable across times and places. In the desert, in a Roman villa, in a Danish study, in a Victorian drawing room and in a modern waiting room, people have described the same restless emptiness. That suggests it may be part of how human minds are built, rather than a recent glitch in the way we live.

@char N09 cafe_sunset | Back to Mira | A very old chair
So when Mira stares at the poster for the fourth time, she is joining a very long line. The monk who looked at the sun, the Roman who counted the sameness of his days and the writer who wished for something to happen are all, in a sense, sitting in that same plastic chair.

@char N02 street_day | A good question | Why would it exist?
And that raises a good question, which will keep returning tonight. If boredom is so old, so widespread and so unpleasant, why haven't we lost it? Evolution is not sentimental about useless feelings. A feeling that hurts, and that has lasted this long, may well be doing a job.
