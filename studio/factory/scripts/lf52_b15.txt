@chapter N10 cafe_sunset | What to do when it happens
Let's bring all of this down to earth. You're in a new place, or in the middle of a conversation, and the feeling arrives: this has happened before. What can you do with that knowledge? Nothing dramatic. But a few small habits can turn a puzzling flicker into something you can actually enjoy, and learn from, and they're based on what we've seen over the last fourteen chapters.

@char N19 living_room_day | The first habit | Name it
The first habit is simply to name it. Say to yourself, that's déjà vu, a false alarm of familiarity. Putting a label on a feeling often takes some of the sting out of it. Psychologists who study emotion have found that naming an experience can calm the brain's reaction to it. For something as harmless as déjà vu, a label is often all you need.

@study N02 cabin_fireplace | Matthew Lieberman and colleagues | Psychological Science, 2007 | Putting feelings into words
The relevant research comes from a 2007 study by Matthew Lieberman and colleagues, who showed that putting an emotion into words reduced activity in the amygdala, a region involved in strong feelings. That was a study of emotional faces, not of déjà vu, so we should be careful. But the broad idea, that naming helps, is a sensible one to borrow for an eerie but harmless feeling.

@char N09 living_room_day | The second habit | Ask where it might come from
The second habit is to get curious about the source. If the layout theory is right, then the feeling often vanishes once the source is found. So try it. Ask yourself, does this room remind me of anywhere? A house from childhood, a building in a film, a street from a holiday. Sometimes the answer comes, and the feeling dissolves. Sometimes it doesn't, and that's fine too.

@char N10 cafe_sunset | Why this helps | The feeling needs a missing source
Remember what we learned in chapter six. The feeling of déjà vu arises when familiarity has no source. When you find one, the puzzle is solved, and the feeling fades. In a sense, you're doing what the checking system does, but on purpose, and with a little more time. It's a small piece of mental detective work, and a pleasing one.

@char N19 living_room_day | The third habit | Test the prediction
The third habit is to test the sense of prediction. The next time you feel that you know what's coming, say it to yourself, or write it down on your phone, before it happens. The window will have a mug. The next person will say a certain word. Then see what comes. If the study by Cleary and Claxton is right, you won't do better than chance, and noticing that is quietly illuminating.

@photo lf52_b15_01.jpg | A notebook and a pencil by a window
A notebook and a pencil by a window. Keeping a short note is the simplest research tool there is. Not a diary of everything, only a line or two when it happens: where you were, how tired you felt, what you were doing, how long it lasted, and whether your prediction came true. After a few months, you might see a pattern that is entirely your own.

@list N27 rooftop_dusk | What to jot down | Where you were ;; How tired or stressed you felt ;; What you were doing ;; How long it lasted ;; Whether the prediction came true
If you'd like to try it, here's a short list of what to jot down. Where you were. How tired or stressed you felt. What you were doing. How long it lasted. And whether the feeling of prediction came true. It takes less than a minute, and it can turn a passing oddity into a small personal experiment.

@char N02 cabin_fireplace | The fourth habit | Stay relaxed
The fourth habit is to stay relaxed. People sometimes try to hold on to a déjà vu, to extend it, or to work out what it means, and the harder they chase it, the faster it fades. Brown's review notes that the feeling usually lasts only ten to thirty seconds. It's like a soap bubble, beautiful until you try to catch it. Let it come, and let it go.

@char N09 living_room_day | A small experiment | The word experiment
Here's a gentle experiment for another day. Take a very common word, like door, or table, and write it twenty or thirty times, quickly. Watch what happens to the word. Does it start to look wrong? That's jamais vu, a safe and harmless way to feel the familiarity system wobbling the other way, and to see for yourself that the feeling of the known is something the mind manufactures.

@char N10 cafe_sunset | Another experiment | A new place, a close look
Another experiment is to visit somewhere new, and, when you arrive, see whether anything about the layout seems to ring a bell, even before you can say why. Notice whether you can find a source. You may be surprised by how often a place that feels vaguely familiar turns out to share its shape with somewhere you know well. The mind is a pattern-matcher, and it's fun to catch it at work.

@char N19 living_room_day | What not to do | Don't over-interpret
There are also a few things not to do. Don't take it as a sign or an omen, because the best evidence says that it carries no information about the future. Don't be alarmed by it, since for nearly everyone it's harmless. And don't dismiss yourself if it ever becomes frequent, or comes with other symptoms, because then a conversation with a doctor is wise.

@char N02 cabin_fireplace | A reminder | This isn't medical advice
A reminder, as always. This video is for learning and is not medical advice. If you're ever concerned about a pattern of episodes, particularly if they repeat, come with strange smells or tastes, fear, a blank spell, or gaps in memory, please see a doctor. An honest description of what happens is the most useful thing you can bring.

@char N09 living_room_day | For parents | A gentle explanation for children
If a child tells you that they've felt as if something had happened before, you can offer a gentle explanation. The mind has a part that says, I know this, and a part that says, hold on, do I? Sometimes the first part speaks a little early. It's a sign that the mind is working, and nothing to be afraid of. Children often feel relieved to hear that it's normal, and shared by almost everyone.

@char N10 cafe_sunset | The social side | Sharing the feeling
There's also a social side. Many people keep their déjà vu to themselves, afraid that it sounds odd. But as Elena found, saying it aloud usually brings a smile of recognition. The feeling is shared by most adults, and talking about it makes it less mysterious and more interesting. Next time it happens, you might mention it to a friend and see what they say.

@char N19 living_room_day | A broader habit | Hold feelings lightly
Beyond déjà vu, there's a broader habit to take away. Treat your feelings of knowing as information, not as verdicts. The sense that you know something, that you've met someone before, or that a claim is true, is a signal from a system that is fast and usually reliable but sometimes wrong. A small pause, a question, a check, and you've used what the research teaches.

@char N02 cabin_fireplace | Looking back | What we set out to learn
So where do we stand? We've seen four small habits: name the feeling, look for its source, test the prediction, and stay relaxed. We've seen some gentle experiments, a few things not to do, and a reminder that frequent or unusual episodes are a matter for a doctor. All of it rests on what the research says.
