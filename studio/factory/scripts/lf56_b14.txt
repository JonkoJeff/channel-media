@chapter A19 cafe_sunset | The laughter epidemic
In January 1962, at a girls' boarding school in the village of Kashasha, near Lake Victoria in what was then Tanganyika and is now Tanzania, a few pupils began to laugh and couldn't stop. Over the following weeks, the laughter spread through the school. It's become one of the most famous strange stories in the history of laughter, and it's often told wrongly.

@study N20 rooftop_dusk | A. M. Rankin and P. J. Philip | Central African Journal of Medicine, 1963 | An epidemic of laughing in the Bukoba district of Tanganyika
The main source is a report published in 1963 by two doctors, A. M. Rankin and P. J. Philip. They described what happened at the school and in the surrounding area. According to their report, the attacks of laughing, often mixed with crying and restlessness, affected many of the pupils, and lasted from a few hours to many days in each person.

@stat A10 cabin_fireplace | Pupils affected at the school | 95 of 159 | according to Rankin and Philip, 1963
Their report gives the numbers. Ninety-five of the school's 159 pupils were affected, and the school had to close in March of that year. When the pupils went home, the episodes appeared in some of their villages too, and other schools in the region were affected. Over the following months, the outbreaks gradually faded away.

@char N01 autumn_overlook | Not a funny story | It wasn't joyful laughter
It's important to say clearly what this was not. It wasn't an epidemic of fun. The people affected weren't happily amused. The reports describe laughter mixed with crying, fear and distress, and people who were frightened by what was happening to them. Calling it a laughter epidemic can make it sound comic. For those who lived through it, it was not.

@char N09 cabin_fireplace | What was it, then? | A stress response that spread
Doctors found no infection or poison to explain it. Most researchers who have studied the episode since describe it as a case of what's called mass psychogenic illness: real physical symptoms that spread through a group under stress, without any infectious cause. The symptoms are real, not imagined. They simply come from the mind and body under pressure, rather than from a germ.

@char N14 attic_rain | A time of change | Stress and pressure
Some researchers have pointed out that this happened at a time of great change. Tanganyika had just become independent, at the end of 1961, and many young people were facing new pressures and expectations, at school and at home. That kind of stress, some have suggested, may have helped set the stage. But it's hard to know for sure, decades later.

@char A03 living_room_day | What it tells us | About contagion
What does it tell us about laughter? It's an extreme, unusual example of something we've seen all evening: that laughter and other emotional behaviour can spread between people. Most of the time, that contagion is gentle and pleasant, like the couple at the next table. In rare situations of intense stress, it can become something frightening and hard to stop.

@char A13 cafe_sunset | A careful word | Rare and complex
It's worth being careful not to make too much of it, though. Episodes like this are rare, and they're complicated, involving culture, stress, beliefs and group dynamics as well as laughter. They aren't a sign that ordinary contagious laughter is dangerous. They're a reminder that human beings are deeply connected, in our feelings and our bodies, sometimes more than we realise.

So here's what we can say. It began at a girls' boarding school in 1962. The laughter was mixed with crying, restlessness and fear. Doctors found no infection or poison. Most researchers see it as mass psychogenic illness, a stress response that spread through a group. And it's rare, complex, and nothing to joke about.

@char A04 conspiracy_board | Why we laugh | Pulling it together
So, why do we laugh? We've gathered a lot of pieces. Laughter seems to have begun as a play signal, millions of years ago. It's mostly social, tied far more to people than to jokes. It spreads. It helps bind friends and couples. It can soothe the body for a while. And it can come from nerves and stress as well as from joy.

If there's one idea that ties it all together, it might be this. A laugh is a way of saying, we're together, and it's all right. That's what a young chimpanzee's panting laugh seems to say during a game of chase. It's what a baby's giggle says during peekaboo.
