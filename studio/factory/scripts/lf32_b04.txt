@chapter A05 autumn_overlook | Rewards and schedules
Let's talk about rewards now. Not the rewards inside the brain, but the ones out in the world, and the surprising ways their timing shapes what we do. Our story starts in New York, in the 1890s, with a young scientist and a group of hungry cats.

@study A12 rooftop_dusk | Edward Thorndike | Columbia University, 1898 | Placed cats in puzzle boxes that opened when they pulled a loop or pressed a lever, and timed how quickly they escaped over many trials
In 1898, Edward Thorndike, then a graduate student at Columbia University, built a set of wooden puzzle boxes. A cat placed inside could get out, and reach a little food, by pulling a loop of string, or pressing a lever, or stepping on a small platform. Thorndike timed each escape, over and over.

@char A04 attic_rain | No sudden insight | Just slow improvement
What he found was not a flash of understanding. The cats didn't seem to suddenly realise how the box worked. Instead, they scrambled about, hit the right move by chance, and escaped. And on later tries, they got there a little faster, then a little faster again. The useless movements slowly dropped away.

@study A13 cabin_fireplace | Edward Thorndike | The law of effect | Actions followed by a satisfying result become more likely; actions followed by discomfort become less likely
Thorndike summed it up in what he called the law of effect. Actions that are followed by a satisfying result tend to be repeated. Actions followed by discomfort tend to fade. It sounds almost too obvious to say. But it became one of the foundations of the science of learning.

@study A12 autumn_overlook | B. F. Skinner | Harvard and elsewhere, 1930s to 1950s | Built a small chamber where an animal could press a lever or peck a key for food, and recorded every response over time
Decades later, the American psychologist B. F. Skinner took that idea and made it precise. He built a small chamber where a rat could press a lever, or a pigeon could peck a little key, and food would sometimes drop into a tray. A pen recorded every single response, minute by minute.

@char A16 street_day | Not every time | The interesting part
And here's where it gets interesting. Skinner didn't have to give food after every press. He could give it after every tenth press. Or after the first press once a minute had passed. Or at random, sometimes after two presses, sometimes after twenty. These patterns are called schedules of reinforcement.

@study A05 rooftop_dusk | Charles Ferster & B. F. Skinner | Schedules of Reinforcement, 1957 | Showed in great detail that different reward schedules produce very different, very reliable patterns of responding
In 1957, Charles Ferster and Skinner published a huge book, Schedules of Reinforcement, full of records from pigeons. It showed that each kind of schedule produces its own reliable pattern of behaviour. Change the schedule, and you change the pattern, almost like changing the rhythm of a song.

@list A03 living_room_day | A few patterns | Every time: steady, but stops quickly ;; At fixed times: a pause, then a rush ;; After an unpredictable number: fast and steady ;; Unpredictable rewards last longest
Here are a few of those patterns, simplified. A reward every time gives steady behaviour, which stops quickly when the rewards end. Rewards at fixed times give a pause after each one, then a rush as the next one nears. Rewards after an unpredictable number of tries give fast, steady responding. And unpredictable rewards tend to keep behaviour going longest after they stop.

@char A04 neon_alley_rain | Maybe this time | The pull of not knowing
Think about why that might be. If a reward always comes after one press, you notice straight away when it stops. But if rewards have always been unpredictable, a long dry spell doesn't feel like the end. The next try might be the one. Maybe this time.

@char A10 bedroom_night | Familiar? | Checking, and checking again
Many researchers have pointed out how much this resembles some modern habits. Pulling down to refresh a feed. Checking for messages. Most of the time there's nothing new. Every so often, there's something lovely. It's a pattern that can keep the hand moving, even when the eyes are tired.

@char A13 cabin_fireplace | A word of care | People are not pigeons
A word of care, though. People are not pigeons in a box. We have plans, memories, values and other people around us, and we can notice what we're doing. The schedules are a powerful influence on behaviour, not a complete explanation of it. Still, knowing the pattern can make it a little easier to see.

@study A12 rooftop_dusk | B. F. Skinner | Published in 1948 | Gave hungry pigeons food at regular intervals no matter what they did, and many developed odd repeated rituals
In 1948, Skinner tried something curious. He gave hungry pigeons a little food at regular intervals, no matter what they were doing. Nothing they did made any difference. And yet many of the birds developed strange rituals. One turned in circles. Another kept thrusting its head into an upper corner of the cage.

@char A04 attic_rain | Superstition | Learning from coincidence
Skinner called this superstition. Whatever the bird happened to be doing when the food arrived seemed to get stamped in, and repeated, and so it was more likely to be doing it when the next food came along. A coincidence turned into a habit. It's easy to think of human lucky rituals that might work in a similar way.

@chapter A19 cabin_fireplace | Closer and faster
There's another old finding about rewards that's worth meeting, because it shows up in rats, and also, rather charmingly, in coffee shops.

@study A12 rooftop_dusk | Clark Hull | Published in 1932 | Proposed the goal gradient: animals speed up as they get closer to a reward, with rats running faster near the end of a runway
In 1932, Clark Hull, whom we met earlier, described what he called the goal gradient. The idea was that the closer an animal is to a reward, the harder it works. Rats running down a straight alley towards food tended to run faster as they neared the end.

@char A16 street_day | The final stretch | You've felt it too
You've probably felt this yourself. The last few pages of a good book go faster than the first few. The final stretch of a long walk home has its own extra energy. When the end is in sight, something seems to switch on.

@study A05 autumn_overlook | Ran Kivetz, Oleg Urminsky & Yuhuang Zheng | Columbia University, 2006 | Studied café reward cards and found that people visited more often as they got closer to a free coffee
In 2006, Ran Kivetz, Oleg Urminsky and Yuhuang Zheng, at Columbia University, looked for the goal gradient in people. They studied a café reward card, where each coffee earned a stamp, and a full card earned a free coffee. And they found that people came back more often, with shorter gaps between visits, as their card filled up.

@char A20 cafe_sunset | A clever twist | A head start
Then they tried a clever twist. Some customers received a card that needed ten stamps. Others received a card that needed twelve stamps, but already had two stamped as a bonus. Both groups had exactly ten more coffees to go. The only difference was how the card looked.

@char A11 street_day | It mattered | Already on the way
And yet the head-start card was filled faster, on average. Feeling that you've already begun, even when the start was given to you, seemed to pull people along. Progress, or even the look of progress, is motivating in itself.

@char A13 rooftop_dusk | Why it works | The gap feels smaller
One way to understand this is that the remaining distance feels smaller when you think of yourself as partway there. Ten steps out of ten feels like a beginning. Ten steps out of twelve feels like you're well on your way. We'll come back to this idea of progress later tonight.

Picture a coffee cup on a small café table, and a little card beside it. A few marks already on it. That small sense of being partway there is enough to change what people do.

@char A15 cafe_sunset | Rewards are powerful | And that's the point
So rewards shape behaviour powerfully. Their timing, their predictability, and how close they seem all matter. It might seem, then, that the simplest way to motivate anyone is just to add more rewards. More stamps. More prizes. More praise.

@char A02 living_room_day | Small rewards at home | A cup of tea
Many people use this quite naturally. A cup of tea after finishing a chore. A short walk after a long stretch of work. A chapter of a book once the dishes are done. These small rewards, close in time to the effort, follow exactly the kind of pattern Thorndike saw in his cats.

@list A03 autumn_overlook | The third stop | Satisfying results get repeated ;; The schedule shapes the pattern ;; Unpredictable rewards keep us going ;; We speed up near the goal
So that's our third stop. Satisfying results tend to get repeated. The schedule of rewards shapes the pattern of behaviour. Unpredictable rewards keep us going longest. And we speed up as the goal comes into view.
