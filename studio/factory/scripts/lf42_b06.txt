@chapter A10 living_room_day | Habits and places
Now, let's go a little deeper, because that observation holds a very big idea. If a habit is a link between a cue and an action, then the cue has to be somewhere. And a great many cues turn out to live in the world around us, in places, in people, in the usual order of the day. If that's right, then changing the world around us should change which habits show up. Researchers found a lovely natural way to test this. It didn't need a laboratory. It only needed a real change of address.

@study A13 cafe_sunset | Wood, Tam and Guerrero Witt | Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, published 2005 | After transferring universities, habits survived when the context stayed similar
In 2005, Wendy Wood and her colleagues reported a study of one hundred and fifteen students who had just transferred to a new university. Most had also moved to a new community. The researchers asked about three everyday behaviors, exercising, reading the newspaper, and watching television, and about the circumstances in which they did them. Then they followed up, to see which habits made the journey, and which did not.

@list A19 rooftop_dusk | Three everyday behaviors | Exercising ;; Reading the newspaper ;; Watching television
These three were chosen because many people do them regularly, and often without much thought. Exercising might happen at the same gym or on the same path. Reading the paper might be part of a particular morning, with a particular cup of something warm. Television might belong to a certain sofa and a certain hour. Each one is tied up with a setting, and that was exactly what made them a good test of the idea.

@stat A10 cafe_sunset | The students | 115 | transfer students took part
There were one hundred and fifteen transfer students in this study, and they had changed their surroundings to very different degrees. Some had moved to a new community, which changed almost everything around them. Others had switched colleges within the same community, so many parts of daily life stayed where they were. That difference gave the researchers a natural comparison, with no one having to be asked to change anything at all.

@char A18 living_room_day | The pattern | Where it stayed the same, it stayed
This is what they found. Strong habits tended to continue after the transfer when the setting for the behavior stayed similar. If a student kept exercising in the same kind of place, or reading with the same kinds of people around, the habit carried on. But when the setting changed, the habit was disrupted. The location, the people nearby, even what roommates were doing. The habit lost its cues, and the behavior wobbled.

Think of a path you've walked so many times that your feet know every turn. You don't plan the corners. You don't need to. The path itself seems to steer you. Now imagine suddenly being set down in a different place, with different corners. The feet hesitate, the mind has to take over, and what had been automatic becomes something we have to think about again. It can be a little tiring, and a little fresh, both at once.

@chapter A03 attic_rain | Who was affected
There's a detail in this study that's quite gentle. The people most affected by the change in surroundings were the ones who had strong habits. Those with weak habits, or no habit at all, were largely unaffected. That makes sense. If a behavior was never running on cues, then a new place has no automatic link to break. It's only the well-worn paths that feel the difference when the ground shifts. The light, new habits just drift along unnoticed.

@char A15 cabin_fireplace | And intentions? | Following what we mean to do
Once the habit was disrupted, something else seemed to take over. Behavior tended to follow what people intended to do, though this wasn't always statistically significant. In other words, when the old cue is gone, the person starts steering again, at least a little. And for behaviors that weren't habits in the first place, intentions guided what people did, whether or not their surroundings changed. This fits nicely with what we explored a little earlier.

@quote A19 cabin_fireplace | The habit lives where it was learned. | A way to hold the finding
It isn't a quote from the paper, just a simple way to remember the idea. The habit lives where it was learned. Not in the sense that it's trapped there, but that its cues are scattered around that place, like small signposts. Take away the signposts, and the habit has less to go on. Put them back in front of us, and the old behavior may well come walking back, quite politely, as if it had never left. That's worth knowing, because it means a return to an old habit isn't a betrayal. It's just a signpost doing its job.

@char A10 bedroom_night | One careful point | It's not only about intention
It might be tempting to say that people simply changed their minds when they moved, and that's why their habits changed. The researchers looked at that too. They found that the context change did shift people's intentions somewhat. But the pattern of intention change didn't match the pattern of habit disruption. So changed intentions alone couldn't explain why the habits faded. The surroundings themselves seemed to matter. Both things were true together, and it takes a careful study to tell them apart.

@curve A12 rooftop_dusk | Does the habit come along? | How similar the new surroundings are | How likely the habit is to continue | 0.9 | Same cues, same habit
Here's one way to picture the whole story. On the left, the surroundings are very different from before, and the habit is likely to be disrupted. As we move to the right, more and more of the old setting is still there, and the habit is more likely to carry on. It's a gentle slope rather than a cliff. The more of the old cues remain, the more of the old habit comes along.

@char A13 living_room_day | Back to Ines | The coffee and the switch
So Ines starts to look at her new life a little differently. The coffee habit stayed behind because its cues did. The light switch habit followed her because the doorway, the evening and the bag over her shoulder all came along, and the cue was strong enough to reach into a hallway where it no longer belonged. Both are the same process, working in two directions, and there is something rather tidy in that. She finds it oddly comforting that her hand and her coffee are following the same rule.

@list A03 cabin_fireplace | Ordinary cues around us | Where we are ;; Who is nearby ;; What we just did ;; How the room looks and feels
These are ordinary examples rather than findings, but they might sound familiar. Where we are. Who is nearby. What we've just finished doing. The way a room looks and feels at a certain hour. Any of these can quietly carry a habit, and any of them can quietly let it go. We don't have to list them all. We only need to notice that the world is doing more of the work than we thought. And that is a very kind thing to discover, because it means we were never doing it all alone.

@char A15 rooftop_dusk | A hopeful thought | Maybe a move is not only a loss
Ines has been thinking of the move as something that took things away from her. Her friends, her old streets, her familiar routines. But the study offers a slightly different view. When the setting changes, the old cues go quiet, and the habits tied to them loosen. That means there may be a window, a rare moment when the ground is a little softer than usual, and new things might be planted more easily. It's only a hope for now, and we'll look at what the research says about it, but it feels like a good one to carry.

@char A18 autumn_overlook | A rare window | And she is standing in it
It's a gentle thought to fall asleep on. Most of us rarely get a moment when so many of our daily cues change at once. Ines happens to be living in one right now. The coffee is gone, the old route is gone, and the first autumn in a new place is only just beginning. And so the next question is, quite simply, what a person might do with a window like that.
