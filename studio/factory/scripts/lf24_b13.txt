@char A15 cabin_fireplace | The night-time phone | Sleep and the feed
It's late. The lights are low, the day is over, and the phone is still in your hand. For many people, this is when social media is hardest to put down, and when it may do the most damage. Not because of what it shows, but because of what it displaces. In this chapter, we look at sleep, and at a worry that has its own acronym.

@chapter A06 cafe_sunset | Does the phone cost us sleep?
Sleep is one of the most important things we do. It consolidates memory, regulates mood and repairs the body, and a long line of research links poor sleep to worse mood, worse attention and worse health. If social media shortens or disturbs sleep, that could be a bigger problem than anything it does to mood directly.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Levenson, Shensa, Sidani, Colditz & Primack | Preventive Medicine, 2016 | The association between social media use and sleep disturbance among young adults
Jessica Levenson and her colleagues at the University of Pittsburgh surveyed one thousand seven hundred and eighty-eight young adults in the United States, aged nineteen to thirty-two. They found that those who used social media more often during the day, and those who spent more total time on it, were more likely to report sleep disturbance, even after accounting for other factors. The association was stronger for people who checked often.

@stat A14 conspiracy_board | Young adults in the Pittsburgh sleep survey | 1,788 | aged 19 to 32, surveyed about social media use and sleep
Once again, this is a correlation. People who sleep badly might scroll more, because they're awake and bored. But the finding fits with other research, and it points to a plausible chain: use late at night, a mind that is stimulated, a later bedtime, less sleep. Several studies have found that the habit of using a phone in bed is one of the stronger predictors of poor sleep.

@char A04 attic_rain | The light question | Is it the glow?
Many people blame the blue light from screens, which can suppress melatonin, the hormone that helps us feel sleepy. There's a good experiment behind this. In 2015, Anne-Marie Chang and her colleagues at Harvard compared reading on a light-emitting device before bed with reading a printed book. The readers of devices took longer to fall asleep and had lower melatonin.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Chang, Aeschbach, Duffy & Czeisler | Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 2015 | Evening use of light-emitting eReaders negatively affects sleep, circadian timing, and next-morning alertness
Twelve people spent two weeks in a sleep laboratory, reading for four hours before bed each evening, on an e-reader one night and a printed book the next, over several days. When they read on the device, melatonin rose later, the body clock shifted, and they felt less alert the next morning. It's a small study, but a careful one, and the results are consistent with what we know about light and the body clock.

@list A03 living_room_day | What interrupts sleep | Bright, blue-rich light before bed ;; Stimulating or upsetting content ;; Notifications during the night ;; Staying up later because there's no end to the feed
Notice that only the first of these is about light. The others are about what the phone makes us do. Many sleep researchers think that the content and the habit matter at least as much as the glow, though it's hard to separate them cleanly.

@chapter A13 rooftop_dusk | A fear with an acronym
Now to the worry that has its own initials. FOMO, the fear of missing out. It's the uneasy feeling that others are having rewarding experiences without you, and that you should be checking in to keep up. It's an old feeling, but social media gives it a daily feed of evidence.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Przybylski, Murayama, DeHaan & Gladwell | Computers in Human Behavior, 2013 | Motivational, emotional, and behavioral correlates of fear of missing out
Andrew Przybylski and his colleagues developed a questionnaire to measure FOMO, and tested it with over two thousand people. They found that people with higher FOMO were more likely to have unmet needs for competence, autonomy and belonging, lower mood and life satisfaction, and to use social media more heavily, including when driving and shortly after waking.

@char A12 living_room_day | What FOMO tells us | An unmet need, not a gadget problem
The authors interpreted FOMO as a sign of an unmet psychological need. People who feel disconnected or unsure of themselves are more drawn to checking what others are doing, and checking can then deepen the feeling. It's a loop in which the cure and the cause look very similar.

@list A12 living_room_day | The FOMO loop | Feeling left out or unsure ;; Checking the feed for reassurance ;; Seeing others' best moments ;; Feeling more left out
Such loops are common in psychology. And the usual way out isn't to fight the feeling directly but to meet the underlying need, with real connection, in a form that fits your life.

@char A01 cafe_sunset | A cheerful counterpart | JOMO
Some people have started to talk about the joy of missing out, JOMO. It sounds like a slogan, but it points at something real. A person who has chosen what matters, and is at peace with not doing the rest, has less to fear from the highlights of other people's lives. Choosing is the antidote to fear of missing.

@photo lf24_b13_01.jpg | A bedside at night
A bedside table, a small lamp, and a phone charging across the room. It's a small arrangement with a good deal of evidence behind it. Keeping the phone away from the bed puts a little friction between you and the feed at the hour when you most need it.

@char A19 cabin_fireplace | A practical idea | An hour of margin
A common suggestion from sleep researchers is a wind-down hour: dim lights, no feed, something calm. It doesn't need to be perfect. Even a gentle change, such as leaving the phone across the room, gives your brain a chance to shift gears. For many people, it's the single most effective change they can make.

@list A03 living_room_day | Words to keep from this chapter | Melatonin ;; Circadian rhythm ;; FOMO ;; Sleep hygiene
Four terms from this chapter. Melatonin is a hormone that signals night to the body. The circadian rhythm is the body's internal clock. FOMO is the fear of missing out. And sleep hygiene means the habits that support good sleep.

@list A03 living_room_day | A calmer evening routine | Charge the phone outside the bedroom ;; Dim the lights an hour before bed ;; Keep a book or a notebook by the bed ;; Set a fixed time to stop checking
A calmer evening starts with a few small choices. Charging the phone in another room removes the temptation and the night-time alerts. Dimming the lights tells the body clock that the day is ending. And a book or a notebook next to the bed gives your hands something else to do. Sleep scientists stress regularity, a similar bedtime and wake time each day, as the most important rule of all. You don't have to do everything. Pick the one that feels easiest, and let it become a habit before you add another.

@char A15 cabin_fireplace | A gentle thought | Rest is not wasted
If you stay up scrolling because the day didn't feel like yours, that's an understandable instinct. Researchers call it revenge bedtime procrastination, the wish to claim some time for yourself at the cost of sleep. If it's you, it might help to find a small pocket of your day that's really yours, before the night.

@char A08 cabin_fireplace | Looking back | What the night taught us
So what did we learn? That heavier social media use is linked to more sleep problems. That evening light from devices can delay the body clock. That FOMO signals unmet needs, and feeds a loop. And that a small boundary around bedtime goes a long way.
