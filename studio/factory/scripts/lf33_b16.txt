@chapter A19 cabin_fireplace | Tools for choosing
We've spent a long evening with Mara, looking at all the ways a mind can lean, wobble and tell itself stories. It would be easy to come away thinking that good decisions are impossible. But that's not the lesson at all. Researchers have also found some simple, gentle tools that help. Not to make us perfect. Just a little wiser.

@char A13 living_room_day | Advice for a friend | Easier than for ourselves
Here's a curious thing you may have noticed in your own life. When a friend brings you a problem, you often see it clearly. You can see both sides, you can see what they're missing, you can see that it will probably work out. But when the same problem is your own, it all becomes cloudy.

@study A01 cafe_sunset | Igor Grossmann and Ethan Kross | Psychological Science, 2014 | People reasoned more wisely about a friend's problem than their own, until they stepped back
In 2014, Igor Grossmann and Ethan Kross gave this a lovely name. Solomon's paradox, after the biblical king famous for his wise advice, and his rather less wise personal life. In their study, people reasoned more wisely about a friend's relationship troubles than about the same troubles in their own relationship.

@char A10 rooftop_dusk | Stepping back | Using your own name
They considered more points of view, admitted more uncertainty, and saw more possible ways things could turn out. But then came the hopeful part. When people were asked to think about their own problem from a distance, as if it were happening to someone else, even using their own name instead of I, the gap disappeared. They became as wise about themselves as about their friends.

@char A15 autumn_overlook | Mara steps back | What would she tell a friend
So imagine Mara asking herself a new question. If my best friend were sitting here, with a sister by the sea and two columns on a sheet of paper, what would I tell her? Or even, what should Mara do? It sounds a little strange, talking about yourself by name. But the research suggests it helps.

@chapter A05 cabin_fireplace | The premortem
The second tool comes from Gary Klein, the psychologist who studied firefighters. He called it a premortem. Instead of waiting until something has gone wrong to ask why, you imagine, right at the start, that it already has.

@char A12 living_room_day | Imagine it failed | Then explain why
Here's how it works. Imagine it's a year from now, and the decision turned out badly. Then write down every reason you can think of for why it went wrong. It's remarkable how much easier it is to find problems when you imagine they've already happened, rather than asking, what could go wrong?

@study A03 rooftop_dusk | Deborah Mitchell, Edward Russo and Nancy Pennington | Journal of Behavioral Decision Making, 1989 | Imagining an event had already happened helped people find more reasons for it
Klein based the idea partly on a 1989 study by Deborah Mitchell, Edward Russo and Nancy Pennington. They found that when people imagined an event had already happened, rather than that it might happen, they came up with more reasons for it. They called it prospective hindsight. Looking back from an imagined future seems to loosen the imagination.

@char A20 cafe_sunset | A premortem for Mara | Both ways
Mara could do a premortem on both choices. It's a year from now, I moved, and it went badly. Why? Perhaps I was lonely, and didn't make an effort to meet people. And then, it's a year from now, I stayed, and it went badly. Why? Perhaps I spent the year wishing I'd gone. Each story points to something she could watch out for, whichever she chooses.

@chapter A16 autumn_overlook | Consider the opposite
The third tool is one we met briefly earlier, with anchors. It's called considering the opposite. In 1984, Charles Lord, Mark Lepper and Elizabeth Preston found that simply asking people to imagine how they'd judge things if the evidence pointed the other way helped them weigh evidence more fairly.

@char A13 cabin_fireplace | Telling them to be fair | Did not work
Interestingly, simply telling people to be fair and unbiased didn't help much. What helped was a specific mental move. What if the opposite were true? What would I notice then? For Mara, that might mean asking, what if staying is the brave choice, and going is the easy one? It might not be true. But asking can show her what she's been overlooking.

Picture a single coin resting on the sheet of paper, right between the two columns. It looks almost silly there, beside all those careful notes. And yet, as we're about to see, a coin can do something the notes cannot.

@chapter A18 cafe_sunset | The coin
And now, as promised, back to the coin. At the start of the night, we wondered whether Mara could let a coin decide. It sounds like the opposite of everything careful we've talked about. But in 2021, the economist Steven Levitt published a study that took the idea seriously.

@study A01 living_room_day | Steven Levitt | Review of Economic Studies, 2021 | About 20,000 coin flips for real life decisions, and how people felt months later
Levitt set up a website where people who were stuck on a big decision could have a virtual coin decide for them. Heads meant make the change. Tails meant stay as you are. Around twenty thousand coin flips took place. Months later, Levitt followed up with the people who had agreed to take part, asking what they had done and how they felt.

@char A15 rooftop_dusk | People listened | More often than expected
First, people were influenced by the coin. Those who got heads were noticeably more likely to make the change than those who got tails. So the coin really did tip some decisions. And that allowed Levitt to ask the key question. Were the people nudged into change happier, or less happy?

@char A10 cabin_fireplace | Change, and happier | For big decisions
For important decisions, especially things like leaving a job or ending a relationship, people who made a change reported being happier, on average, two months and six months later, than those who stayed as they were. Levitt was careful to say that this doesn't prove change is always better. But it suggested that people who are truly torn may tend to lean too much towards staying.

@char A12 autumn_overlook | The other use | What you feel
And there's a second, gentler way a coin can help. Some researchers and many wise friends have pointed out that when the coin is in the air, you often find yourself hoping for one side. And when it lands, you might feel a flicker of relief, or of disappointment. That flicker tells you something your columns didn't.

@char A19 bedroom_night | Mara's coin | In the air
Imagine Mara flipping her coin. It spins upward, catching the lamplight. And in that half second, before it lands, she finds herself thinking, please be heads. That's not the coin deciding. That's her, finally speaking clearly, through the noise of all her careful reasons.

@list A20 cafe_sunset | Gentle tools | Ask what you'd tell a friend ;; Imagine it went wrong, and why ;; Consider the opposite ;; Notice what you hope for
So here are the gentle tools. Ask what you'd tell a friend in your place. Imagine it went wrong, and explain why. Consider the opposite of what you believe. And notice what you hope for, when the coin is in the air. None of them makes a decision for you. All of them help you see it more clearly.
