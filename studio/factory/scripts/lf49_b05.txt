@chapter N10 living_room_day | Where thoughts go
Anton has been keeping a mental list of where his thoughts went when they left page twelve. A meeting on Thursday, a back door, a half-remembered tune. He wonders whether that's typical, or whether his mind is simply odd.

@char N02 cafe_sunset | A fair question | Are the destinations random?
It's a reasonable question, because a wandering mind feels random. One moment you're reading about mountain paths, and the next you're thinking about a sandwich you had a decade ago. But when researchers collected the contents of wandering thoughts, they found that the content was far from random. It leaned strongly in certain directions.

@char N19 autumn_overlook | Reading the contents | What people actually reported
One way to find out is to do what Killingsworth did, and Kane did, but to go a step further. Instead of asking only whether people were wandering, you ask what they were thinking about. Past, present or future? Pleasant, neutral or unpleasant? About themselves, or about someone else? And then you count.

@study N07 cafe_sunset | Arnaud Stawarczyk and colleagues | Acta Psychologica, 2011 | Volunteers were interrupted at intervals and asked to describe the content of their thoughts
In 2011, the Belgian researcher Arnaud Stawarczyk and his colleagues published one such study, using a new kind of experience sampling method. They found that a large share of mind wandering was about the future, and about personal goals, plans and concerns. Much of the rest concerned the past, and a smaller share was about the present, though not about the task at hand.

@char N23 rooftop_dusk | A striking pattern | A mind that looks ahead
That tilt towards the future shows up in other work too. In 2011, Benjamin Baird, Jonathan Smallwood and Jonathan Schooler published a paper in Consciousness and Cognition with the title Back to the future. They asked people to do a simple task that left room for the mind to roam, and then looked at what they'd been thinking about.

@study N07 living_room_day | Benjamin Baird, Jonathan Smallwood and Jonathan Schooler | Consciousness and Cognition, 2011 | People did an undemanding task and reported on their thoughts, which were often about their own future plans
They found that, when people's minds wandered, the thoughts were more often about the future than about the past. And the researchers suggested something interesting about why. In their study, the future-oriented thoughts tended to be about personal goals. It was as if the mind, given a spare moment, used it to plan.

@char N02 autumn_overlook | An old idea | Autobiographical planning
They called this autobiographical planning, and it has an appealing logic. We are creatures with goals, relationships and responsibilities, and we live in a world that changes. A mind that regularly checks in on what's coming next, even when it's not asked to, may be a mind that keeps us a little better prepared.

@char N10 cabin_fireplace | What Anton recognizes | A meeting and a door
This fits Anton's list nicely. The meeting on Thursday was a plan, a rehearsal of a sentence he wants to say. The back door was a small piece of housekeeping, a check on the future. Only the tune, which came from nowhere, was a gift from the past. Two out of three of his thoughts were, in a sense, working.

@char N09 living_room_day | Where this comes from | Imagining what hasn't happened
All of that depends on a remarkable ability. To think about the future, you have to build a scene in your head that you haven't yet seen. You have to imagine a place, people, words, even how you'd feel. And the mind does this using the same machinery it uses to remember.

@study N07 rooftop_dusk | Daniel Schacter and Donna Addis | Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B, 2007 | Memory and imagining the future rely on overlapping brain systems, because both rebuild scenes from stored pieces
In 2007, the psychologists Daniel Schacter and Donna Addis proposed the constructive episodic simulation hypothesis. The idea is that memory isn't a recording that we play back. It's a rebuilding, which pulls together pieces of past experience. And because the same pieces can be recombined, the same system can be used to imagine things that haven't happened yet.

@char N20 bedroom_night | Supporting evidence | Memory and imagination share a network
Researchers at University College London, including Demis Hassabis and Eleanor Maguire, reached a similar view that same year. In their work, people with damage to the hippocampus, a region important for memory, found it hard to imagine new, detailed scenes. And brain imaging showed that remembering the past and imagining new scenes both engaged overlapping regions, including parts of the default network.

@char N10 living_room_day | Putting it together | One system, two uses
That gives us a useful way to picture mind wandering. It isn't random noise. It's the brain's scene-building system running in a spare moment, drawing on memory and pointing, quite often, towards the future. A wandering mind is, in a sense, a rehearsal stage.

A paper map lies across a wooden table, partly unrolled, with a small compass and a pencil resting on it. A few routes are sketched in light pencil. None of the roads have been walked yet. It's a picture of a future being gently planned.

@char N02 cafe_sunset | An honest limit | Not all of it is planning
It's worth being honest that not all mind wandering is purposeful planning. Plenty of it is simply a memory floating up, a song, an image or a snippet of conversation. And some of it is worry, which is a different thing altogether, and we'll come to that later tonight. The future-leaning pattern is a trend, not a rule.

@char N19 autumn_overlook | Another honest limit | Mostly students and volunteers
There's also the usual caution about who was studied. Much of this work involves university students, and in some countries more than others. Whether the same balance of past and future holds across all ages and cultures is something researchers are still working out.

@char N10 cabin_fireplace | A gentle thought | The mind as a traveler
Still, there's something quietly encouraging about the picture. When your mind wanders off, it may not be running away from your life. It may be walking through it, past and future, checking the doors, trying the routes and trying on the words you'd like to say.
