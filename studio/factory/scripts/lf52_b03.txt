@chapter N09 living_room_day | Old explanations
People didn't wait for neuroscience to puzzle over this feeling. For as long as there has been writing about the mind, there have been attempts to explain why a new moment can seem old. Some of those attempts were poetic, some were medical, and a few were surprisingly close to ideas that researchers still discuss today. Let's take a short walk through them, in the order they appeared.

@char N02 cabin_fireplace | The ancient answer | Remembering a past life
The oldest answer is probably the most natural one. If a place feels familiar, perhaps you've been there before. And if you haven't, in this life, perhaps you have in another. Versions of this idea appear in many traditions of reincarnation. The Greek philosopher Plato, in dialogues such as the Meno, argued that learning is really a kind of recollection of things the soul once knew.

@char N19 living_room_day | A careful note | Plato wasn't describing déjà vu
A careful note here. Plato was making a point about knowledge and the soul, not describing the modern experience of déjà vu. But later writers often borrowed his language, and the idea that the feeling is a memory from another time became a standard way to talk about it. It's a beautiful idea, and it's still the explanation that many people reach for first.

@quote N27 rooftop_dusk | We all have some experience of a feeling, that comes over us occasionally, of what we are saying and doing having been said and done before, in a remote time. | Charles Dickens, David Copperfield, 1850
Writers noticed the feeling long before scientists measured it. In David Copperfield, published in 1850, Charles Dickens gave one of the clearest descriptions in English literature. He called it a feeling of having said and done the same thing before, in a remote time, and he treated it as something every reader would recognise.

@char N10 cafe_sunset | Doctors take notice | Wigan and the two brains
In the nineteenth century, doctors began to look for medical explanations. In 1844, the English physician Arthur Wigan proposed that the two halves of the brain normally work in perfect step, and that déjà vu happens when they fall slightly out of time with each other. One half would perceive the scene a moment before the other, so that the second half experiences it as a repeat.

@char N09 living_room_day | The idea survives | Split perception
That picture of a brain with two out-of-step halves has a long afterlife. It reappears, under different names, in the idea of split perception, and in the suggestion that a scene can reach awareness twice, once as a quick glimpse and then again as a full view. Researchers today no longer accept the two hemispheres version, but the notion of a delay between two streams of processing is still alive.

@char N19 cafe_sunset | A name and a stage | Boirac, again
We met Émile Boirac in the first chapter. In 1876, he wrote to the Revue philosophique to describe the illusion and to give it a name. He thought of it as a kind of false memory, a trick in which the present moment is mistaken for something remembered. That framing, an error of memory rather than a sign of the supernatural, is the one that science has followed ever since.

@study N10 living_room_day | Henri Bergson | Revue philosophique, 1908 | Memory of the present and false recognition
The French philosopher Henri Bergson offered a more striking theory in 1908, in an essay on the memory of the present. He argued that every moment is split in two as it happens, into a perception and a memory of that perception, so that we are always, in a sense, remembering the present. Déjà vu, he suggested, is the rare moment when we become aware of that split.

@char N02 cabin_fireplace | Why Bergson matters | An idea ahead of its time
Bergson's picture sounds odd, but it contains something modern researchers find interesting. It treats déjà vu as a glitch in the timing of ordinary memory, rather than as a message from somewhere else. And it points to the idea that recognition, the feeling that a thing is known, is produced by the mind and is not simply given by the world.

@study N19 cafe_sunset | Sigmund Freud | Letter to Romain Rolland, 1936 | A disturbance of memory on the Acropolis
Sigmund Freud wrote about a related feeling in a famous open letter in 1936, when he was eighty years old. He recalled a trip to Athens in 1904, when he stood on the Acropolis and was struck by a strange sense of unreality, as though what he was seeing couldn't be real. He linked it, in the letter, to guilty feelings about having travelled further in life than his father.

@char N09 living_room_day | Freud and déjà vu | Psychoanalytic ideas
Freud also wrote about déjà vu more directly. In his book The Psychopathology of Everyday Life, he suggested that the feeling might come from a hidden, unconscious fantasy that the present moment happens to echo. Few researchers today find that explanation testable, but it shows how widely the experience was discussed. Almost every major thinker of the era felt they had to account for it.

@photo lf52_b03_01.jpg | A desk with old letters and a lamp
An old desk with letters and a lamp. Much of what we know about early ideas on déjà vu comes from letters, journals and essays, where thoughtful people tried to describe a feeling that no one could measure. They had no brain scans, and no controlled experiments. What they had was careful attention to their own experience, and a willingness to write it down.

@char N10 cabin_fireplace | A scientific shift | From stories to tests
By the early twentieth century, psychologists began trying to turn these stories into testable ideas. Edward Titchener, a leading experimental psychologist of the time, suggested that déjà vu might happen when you glimpse a scene while distracted, and then look at it properly a moment later. The second look feels like a repeat of something you have already seen, but can't quite place.

@char N19 street_day | A modern echo | Distraction and familiarity
It's a neat idea, and a version of it is still debated. Perhaps a brief, half-attended glance really does leave a trace, and when the full scene arrives, that trace creates a feeling of having been here. Researchers have tried to test it in the laboratory, with mixed results. We'll look at the better evidence for that kind of explanation in a later chapter.

@list N02 rooftop_dusk | A summary of the old ideas | A memory from a past life ;; Two halves of the brain out of step ;; A split between perception and memory ;; An unconscious fantasy ;; A glimpse while distracted
Let's gather those ideas in one place. A memory from a past life. Two halves of the brain out of step. A split between perception and memory. An unconscious fantasy. And a glimpse while distracted. Each one tries to explain the same experience, and as we'll see, a couple of them hold a seed of something that modern science has kept.

@char N09 cafe_sunset | The pop culture version | Glitches in the matrix
The old explanations have modern cousins in popular culture. In the 1999 film The Matrix, a character says that déjà vu is usually a glitch, a sign that something in the world has been changed. Millions of viewers took away the idea that the feeling is an error in the system, which, interestingly, is not far from how researchers think of it, though they mean a system of memory, not a machine.

@char N10 living_room_day | What changed | Testing instead of telling
What changed in the twentieth century wasn't a single discovery. It was a change of method. Instead of asking, what could déjà vu mean, researchers started to ask, what can we make happen in the laboratory? That shift from stories to tests is the same shift that has helped to explain many of the mind's odder habits, and it's the shift that the rest of this video follows.

@char N02 cabin_fireplace | Looking back | What we set out to learn
So where do we stand? We've seen that early thinkers explained the feeling as a past life, as two brain halves out of step, as a split between perception and memory, as a hidden fantasy, or as a distracted first glance. We've seen Dickens describe it in 1850, and Bergson and Freud reach for it in different ways.
