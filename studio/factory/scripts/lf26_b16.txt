@chapter A12 living_room_day | Putting it back together
We began this video with a simple idea: that every one of us walks around with a set of quiet assumptions about the world. That it's mostly safe. That it makes sense. That we are worthy. And that when something shatters those assumptions, the shock we feel is not only about the event itself. It's about the loss of the framework we used to understand everything. Let's finish by pulling the threads together.

@char A04 attic_rain | The core idea | The assumptive world
Ronnie Janoff-Bulman showed us that the assumptions of safety, meaning and self-worth are the foundation under our feet. We don't notice them until they crack. Then everything feels unsteady. That's why survivors of very different events, from illness to betrayal, so often say the same thing: I don't recognise my own life.

@list A03 living_room_day | The map of this video | Shattered assumptions: why the shock goes deeper than the event ;; The just world: why we blame and why it hurts ;; Assimilate or accommodate: two ways to rebuild ;; The grief myths: no stages, no timetable ;; Resilience: the most common outcome
Remember where we started. The just-world belief, studied by Melvin Lerner, explained why people are so quick to blame victims. And the distinction between assimilation and accommodation told us that there are two ways to repair a worldview: bend the facts, or bend the beliefs. Healthy recovery usually involves some of each.

@chapter A13 rooftop_dusk | What the evidence says
Then we looked at the evidence on how people really recover. Maciejewski and his colleagues found no neat stages of grief. Stroebe and Schut described the swing between facing a loss and getting on with life. And Bonanno's work showed that resilience, not collapse, is the most common response to hardship.

@list A12 living_room_day | The second half in brief | Meaning can help, but it does not have to make sense ;; Growth is possible, but never a duty ;; Other people are among the strongest protections ;; Writing can help a little, for some people ;; Beliefs are defended harder when they cost us more
Each of these points came with caution. Meaning-making helps when it leads somewhere and harms when it loops. Posttraumatic growth is real for some people and overstated for others. Expressive writing has a small average effect. And Festinger's study of a failed prophecy reminded us that the beliefs we have paid the most for are the ones we hold hardest, which is a lesson in humility.

@char A01 cafe_sunset | The gentle illusions | Hope is part of the design
Shelley Taylor's study of women facing cancer showed that mild, hopeful beliefs about the future are not a sign of weak thinking. They are part of how minds cope. A person doesn't need to be certain to carry on. They need enough hope to take the next small step, and enough company to take it with.

@chapter A19 living_room_day | Living with a rebuilt worldview
So what is the result of all this rebuilding? It isn't the old worldview, restored. It's a new one, shaped by what happened. Most people who rebuild, if you ask them, say that they're more aware that bad things can happen, and also more aware of how much they value what they have. They hold their assumptions more lightly, and their relationships more closely.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Janoff-Bulman | Free Press, 1992 | Shattered assumptions: towards a new psychology of trauma
Janoff-Bulman wrote that survivors who rebuilt their worldview often arrived at a more realistic, and in some ways more robust, set of beliefs. They no longer assumed that the world was benevolent or that they were invulnerable. But they also found a way to believe that life is still worth living, and that there are good people and good things within it. The new assumptions were less naive, and in many cases more durable.

@list A03 living_room_day | What a rebuilt worldview often looks like | Less certainty, more humility ;; More awareness of fragility, and of what matters ;; Beliefs held more lightly ;; Trust that is chosen, not assumed
That last line captures a great deal. Before a shock, trust is a default. After, it becomes a choice. And a chosen trust, made with open eyes, can be stronger than the old kind, even though it was bought at a price nobody would have chosen to pay.

@char A12 living_room_day | A final caution | Not everything has to be improved
We should end with the same honesty with which we began. Not every loss leads to wisdom. Not every wound turns into a strength. Some things are simply sad, and stay that way. It's enough to survive them, and to live as well as we can alongside them. A rebuilt worldview doesn't have to be better. It only has to be livable.

@list A12 living_room_day | Words to keep from the whole video | Assumptive world ;; Assimilation and accommodation ;; Just-world belief ;; Resilience ;; Dual process ;; Narrative identity
Six terms to take away. The assumptive world is the set of basic beliefs we live by. Assimilation and accommodation are the two ways we repair it. The just-world belief is the wish for fairness that can make us blame. Resilience is the common ability to keep functioning. The dual process model is the swing between loss and life. And narrative identity is the story we tell about who we are.

@list A03 living_room_day | Five things to take from this video | Your shock is normal and has a name ;; There are no stages and no deadlines ;; Most people come through ;; Other people help more than any technique ;; Help is available when it does not ease
If you take only five things from this video, let them be these. First, if you've been shaken, your reaction is normal, and it has a name. You have lost a foundation, and not only an event. Second, there are no stages you must pass through and no deadline to meet. Third, most people come through, and you are more likely to than not. Fourth, other people help more than any technique, so let them in. And fifth, if the pain does not ease, or it pushes you toward harming yourself, reach out for professional help. It's available, it works, and it's a sign of good sense. Whatever has broken in your world, you do not have to rebuild it alone, and you do not have to rebuild it fast.

@char A15 cabin_fireplace | A gentle thought | Be patient with yourself
If you take one last thing with you, let it be patience. The people in these studies took months and years to rebuild. They did it unevenly, with setbacks, and with help. You are allowed to do the same. There's no prize for speed, and there's no shame in time.

@char A08 cabin_fireplace | Looking back | What the whole video taught us
So what did we learn? That our basic assumptions are a hidden foundation. That when they shatter, recovery means rebuilding them, rather than going back. That resilience is common, that people help, and that meaning and growth can come but are never required. And that when the pain will not ease, help is there.
