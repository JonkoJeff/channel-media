@chapter A14 conspiracy_board | What it all means
We've travelled a long way tonight. We began with a dress that split the internet, and we're ending here, with a question that the whole journey has been circling. If your brain is a prediction machine that fills gaps, edits time and sees faces in toast, why would evolution have built it that way?

@char A12 living_room_day | The obvious answer | Speed and survival
The first answer is simple. Perception is for survival, and not for accuracy as such. An animal that took a full second to build a perfect picture of a charging predator would not survive. A fast, approximate guess that is right most of the time is worth much more than a slow, perfect one.

@char A04 attic_rain | A thought experiment | The cheap mistake and the costly one
We touched on this with the faces. Mistakes come in different sizes. Seeing a rope as a snake costs you a moment of fright. Seeing a snake as a rope can cost you your life. A system that has evolved under that kind of pressure will lean towards the cheap mistake, and every so often, you'll see a snake that isn't there.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Von Uexküll | Streifzuge durch die Umwelten, 1934 | Each animal lives in its own perceptual world, its Umwelt
The Estonian biologist Jakob von Uexküll proposed that every species lives in its own perceptual world, which he called its Umwelt. For a tick, the world is made of just a few signals: the smell of butyric acid from a mammal's skin, the warmth of blood and the feel of hair. Everything else is, for the tick, not there.

@char A10 attic_rain | Other worlds | What other animals see
Bees see ultraviolet patterns on flowers that are invisible to us. Many birds see colours and polarised light that we can't. Dogs live in a world of smell that is richer than anything we can imagine. And the mantis shrimp, which has twelve to sixteen types of colour receptor, turns out to be worse at telling similar colours apart than we are, an unexpected twist reported in 2014.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Thoen, How, Chiou & Marshall | Science, 2014 | A different form of color vision in mantis shrimp
Hanne Thoen and colleagues at the University of Queensland found that mantis shrimp struggle with small differences between colours. They suggested that the animal uses its many receptors as a fast, direct code, like a set of barcode readers, rather than comparing signals the way we do. It's a good reminder that more isn't always better, and that different is often just different.

@list A03 living_room_day | What other animals teach us | Each species senses what matters to it ;; No animal gets the whole of reality ;; More receptors does not mean better vision ;; Our own world is one among many
That last line is worth holding on to. Our experience feels like the world itself, but it's the world as filtered by a primate who evolved to find fruit, read faces and throw things.

@chapter A12 living_room_day | Is perception true?
Some scientists have gone further and argued that evolution may not favour accurate perception at all. The cognitive scientist Donald Hoffman at the University of California, Irvine, has argued that organisms tuned for fitness would not see reality as it is, but would see a simple interface that guides action, a bit like the icons on a computer desktop.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Mark, Marion & Hoffman | Journal of Theoretical Biology, 2010 | Natural selection and veridical perceptions
Hoffman and his colleagues ran simulations in which agents that saw the truth competed against agents that saw only fitness payoffs. In their models, the fitness-tuned agents tended to win. The idea is provocative, and it has been the subject of much criticism, from those who say that the models are too simple and that accurate perception often does help fitness.

@char A04 attic_rain | A fair reading | An argument, not a result
It would be wrong to present this as settled. Many perception scientists think that our senses are, for the most part, good guides to the things that matter, such as the size, distance and solidity of objects. Without that, we couldn't catch a ball or cross a road. The debate is really about how deep the truth goes.

@list A12 living_room_day | Two positions, both reasonable | Perception is a useful, mostly reliable guide ;; Perception is an interface tuned for survival ;; Both agree it is not a perfect copy ;; Both agree it can be fooled in revealing ways
That's the practical middle ground. Our senses are good enough to live by, and they are not the same as the world. That's all that this video has needed to claim, and it's enough to change how you look at a morning.

@chapter A06 cafe_sunset | What to do with this
So what do we do with all of this? Here are a few quiet habits that follow from what we've seen. None are dramatic, and none require any special equipment. They follow from taking the research seriously, and from trying to live a little more gently with a brain that guesses.

@char A01 cafe_sunset | The first habit | Hold your certainty lightly
The first is to hold your certainty lightly. The dress showed that two sincere people can see two different colours. The gorilla showed that feeling sure that you saw everything is not the same as having seen it. When someone disagrees with you about what happened, a first thought can be, maybe we saw different things.

@char A19 cabin_fireplace | The second habit | Ask what the other person noticed
The second habit is to ask what the other person noticed. Attention is a narrow beam, and it's shaped by experience, culture and mood. Someone who has spent their life as a gardener or a doctor or a driver will notice different things in the same room. Their answer is not a mistake. It's another piece of the picture.

@char A12 living_room_day | The third habit | Slow down when it matters
The third is to slow down when it matters. Prediction is fast and efficient, and it's at its weakest when we're rushed, stressed or distracted. Before a big decision, or an accusation, or a drive through a busy junction, a pause gives the senses a chance to correct the guess.

@char A04 attic_rain | The fourth habit | Check the pattern
The fourth is to check the pattern. We are pattern-finding creatures, and we find them most when we feel out of control. When something seems to be a sign, ask how many examples there are, and what would count against it. Enjoy the pattern, and test it.

@char A15 cabin_fireplace | The fifth habit | Be gentle with the editor
And the fifth is to be gentle with your own brain. It does an extraordinary job, in real time, on a very small energy budget. It fills the gaps, smooths the cuts and builds a steady world for you. The occasional mistake is the price of a system that's fast enough to keep you safe.

@list A03 living_room_day | Five quiet habits | Hold your certainty lightly ;; Ask what the other person noticed ;; Slow down when it matters ;; Check the pattern ;; Be gentle with the editor
Five short lines. If you remember only those, you will have taken away most of what this long evening had to offer. And if you forget them, don't worry. The brain forgets, and then relearns, and that is part of the story too.

If this video has done its work, you may find that ordinary things look a little stranger. A cup on a table, a face across a room, the time on a clock. Each is a small achievement of the brain, produced in a moment. There's a kind of quiet wonder in that.

And it's not a reason for distrust. You can still trust your senses to get you across a road, to recognise a friend, to taste a good meal. What changes is that you trust them with open eyes. You know that your picture is a good one, but a picture, drawn by a hand that sometimes guesses.
