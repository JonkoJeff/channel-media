@chapter A19 living_room_day | Feeling good, doing good
Does mood affect kindness? Most of us suspect so. We feel more generous after good news, and less patient after a bad day. But in the 1970s a group of psychologists decided to test the idea, and they did it in an unusually simple way. They arranged for small pieces of good luck to befall unsuspecting strangers, and then watched what they did next.

@char A04 attic_rain | The cookie study | A treat in the library
Alice Isen and Paula Levin, working in the early 1970s, gave some people a free cookie in a university library, and then gave everyone in the room a chance to help someone in a small way. They found that those who had just received a cookie were more likely to help than those who hadn't.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Isen & Levin | Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 1972 | Effect of feeling good on helping: cookies and kindness
In the library setting, the people who had received cookies helped more often than a comparison group. The researchers concluded that a small boost in mood could lead to a noticeable boost in helpfulness. It was a modest effect from a small treat, but it made the point. The same paper contained a second study, which became even better known.

@char A12 living_room_day | A famous follow-up | The dime in the phone booth
A second study became even more famous. In a shopping mall, some people found a dime in the coin return of a public telephone, which had been left there on purpose. Moments later, a woman walked by and dropped a folder full of papers. The question was whether the person who had found the coin would help her pick them up.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Isen & Levin | Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 1972 | Effect of feeling good on helping: the telephone booth study
The result was dramatic. Almost everyone who had found the dime stopped to help with the papers. Among those who had not found anything, almost nobody did. The tiny stroke of luck seemed to turn on a switch. The study has been widely cited, though, like many classic experiments, it involved small numbers and has been debated.

@list A03 living_room_day | The finding in plain words | A small piece of luck raised helping sharply ;; The effect appeared within minutes ;; It occurred in a natural setting, not a lab ;; The sample sizes were small
It's a good moment to be cautious. Later researchers have not always found such large effects, and some have questioned how well the dime study replicates. But a larger body of work has supported the broad idea.

@chapter A13 rooftop_dusk | What the combined evidence says
Many studies of mood and helping were gathered into a single review. The authors looked at dozens of experiments in which researchers made participants feel good, bad or neutral, and then measured how much they helped.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Carlson, Charlin & Miller | Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 1988 | Positive mood and helping behavior: a test of six hypotheses
Michael Carlson, Victor Charlin and Norman Miller analysed the research and found that, on average, a positive mood increased helping. They also tested several explanations. They found support for several explanations at once, including the idea that good moods bring more positive thoughts, and that people want to keep a good mood going.

@char A12 living_room_day | A twist | Why it isn't just about warmth
There's a second explanation worth knowing. When people feel good, they tend to see others in a more positive light, and so they're more inclined to see requests as reasonable. They are also more likely to take the helper's perspective. A good mood, in a sense, makes the world look friendlier.

@list A12 living_room_day | How a good mood might lead to helping | We think kinder thoughts about others ;; We want to keep feeling good ;; We feel more secure and less threatened ;; We notice more of what is around us
This is an encouraging idea. It suggests that kindness and wellbeing support each other. Doing small things to lift your own mood, from a good meal to a walk in sunshine, may make you more generous as well.

@chapter A06 cafe_sunset | Does a bad mood help too?
Surprisingly, bad moods can also lead to helping, under certain conditions. In a classic study, researchers asked people to do something that made them feel guilty, such as appearing to have disrupted someone's work, and then gave them a chance to help someone. The guilty people helped more.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Cialdini, Darby & Vincent | Journal of Experimental Social Psychology, 1973 | Transgression and altruism: a case for hedonism
Robert Cialdini and his colleagues proposed the negative-state relief model. According to this idea, people who feel bad sometimes help in order to feel better. Helping becomes a way of repairing a mood. The model was supported by experiments in which guilt or sadness increased helping, unless the person had already been cheered up in some other way.

@char A01 cafe_sunset | An honest point | Helping can be selfish and still good
This raises a question that philosophers have long argued about. If we help in order to feel better, is it still kindness? Psychologists generally avoid the question of purity of motive. A kind act that also benefits the helper is not a lesser act. The person in need is helped either way.

@list A03 living_room_day | Words to keep from this chapter | Positive mood ;; Negative-state relief ;; Hedonism ;; Replication
Four terms from this chapter. Positive mood is feeling good. Negative-state relief is helping in order to relieve one's own bad feelings. Hedonism, here, means acting to feel pleasure. And replication is repeating a study to see if the result holds.

@list A12 living_room_day | Simple ways to look after your own mood | Eat something and drink water before you decide ;; Step outside, even for five minutes ;; Message a friend you like ;; Do one small kind thing for yourself
Here are some simple ways to look after your own mood, which the research suggests may help your kindness. Eat something and drink water before you decide important things. Step outside for five minutes. Message a friend you like. And do one small kind thing for yourself. Picture this scene: a tired nurse at the end of a long shift, sitting in the car park with a cup of tea, five minutes of quiet, and then the drive home, where a neighbour needs a hand with shopping bags. The nurse helps, and feels better for it. The tea mattered more than it seemed.

@list A12 living_room_day | Pause and reflect | What lifts my mood reliably? ;; Do I notice I am kinder when I feel good? ;; What do I do when I feel guilty? ;; Who benefits when I am steady?
A pause to think about your own moods. What reliably lifts yours? A walk, a phone call, a good meal, music? And have you noticed that you are kinder on days when you feel well? Many people notice this when they look back, and feel a little embarrassed to discover that their patience is partly a matter of blood sugar and sleep. The research suggests there is no need for embarrassment. It simply shows that kindness is connected to the rest of us. Also think about guilt. When you feel you have let someone down, do you tend to do something generous afterwards? That is the negative-state relief that Cialdini described, and it is not a flaw. It is a way that conscience turns bad feelings into repair. Seeing these patterns in yourself is the first step toward using them well.

@photo lf27_b04_01.jpg | A coin in the return slot
A small coin in the return slot of a telephone. It is a very small piece of luck. The experiment suggested that even something this small could lift a mood and, with it, the chance that a stranger would be helped. There is something quietly hopeful in the idea that a biscuit or a coin can nudge a person toward kindness. It suggests that we can be generous to the people around us simply by being cheerful, and that cheerfulness spreads.

@char A15 cabin_fireplace | A gentle thought | Tend to your own warmth
There is nothing selfish about looking after your own mood. The research suggests that when we feel steady and well, there is more of us available to others. A kind life is usually built on a reasonably kind relationship with oneself. There is no pressure here to be cheerful on demand. It is enough to notice that your state of mind travels, and to give yourself small, ordinary reasons to feel a bit better, knowing that the people around you may feel the difference.

@char A08 cabin_fireplace | Looking back | What mood taught us
So what did we learn? That small pleasures raised helping in early experiments. That the combined evidence supports a modest, real effect of positive mood. That guilt and sadness can also lead to helping. And that the motives behind kindness can be mixed without making it any less kind.
