@chapter N09 attic_rain | Real laughs and polite ones
Nadia thinks back to the beginning of the evening, before the teapot. Her friend Sam had told a long story about a meeting at work, and at the end, everyone had laughed politely. It was a friendly laugh, but it was nothing like the helpless laughter later on. Even at the time, everyone at the table could probably hear the difference.

@char A04 conspiracy_board | Two kinds of laugh | Spontaneous and chosen
Researchers often separate laughter into two broad kinds. One is spontaneous: the laugh that bursts out of us before we've decided anything, like the teapot laughter. The other is volitional, a laugh we choose to make, often to be polite, to show agreement or to keep a conversation friendly. Both are real behaviour. But they're produced somewhat differently.

@study A13 living_room_day | Matthew Gervais and David Sloan Wilson | Quarterly Review of Biology, 2005 | The evolution and functions of laughter and humor: a synthetic approach
In 2005, the researchers Matthew Gervais and David Sloan Wilson published a long review of laughter research. They argued that these two kinds of laughter have different histories. The spontaneous kind, they suggested, is the older one, rooted in the play signals we met earlier. The chosen kind may have developed later, as humans gained more control over their faces and voices.

@char A03 rooftop_dusk | Different control systems | One older, one newer
There's support for this from brain research. Spontaneous laughter seems to depend heavily on older brain systems involved in emotion, while posed or chosen laughter depends more on areas that control deliberate movements, like speaking. That's one reason why a forced laugh can sound a little stiff. It's being produced by a different set of controls.

@study A10 autumn_overlook | Carolyn McGettigan, Sophie Scott and colleagues | Cerebral Cortex, 2015 | Individual differences in laughter perception reveal roles for mentalizing and sensorimotor systems in the evaluation of emotional authenticity
In a brain-imaging study published in 2015, Carolyn McGettigan, Sophie Scott and colleagues played volunteers recordings of real laughter, produced while watching funny videos, and posed laughter, produced on request. The volunteers were simply asked to listen. The brain treated the two differently, even when the listeners weren't asked to judge them.

@char N14 attic_rain | Working out the meaning | Why is this person laughing?
Posed laughter, in particular, activated brain areas involved in thinking about other people's minds, as if the listener's brain was asking, why is this person laughing, what do they mean by it? Real laughter didn't need that extra work as much. It seemed to be understood more directly, as a simple sign of amusement.

@study A19 rooftop_dusk | Gregory Bryant and colleagues | Psychological Science, 2018 | The perception of spontaneous and volitional laughter across 21 societies
Can people tell the two kinds apart? In 2018, Gregory Bryant and a large team of colleagues tested this around the world, in 21 different societies, from big cities to small rural communities. Listeners heard short recordings of laughs and were asked whether each one was real or fake. Their task was simply to guess.

@char A12 cabin_fireplace | Better than chance everywhere | Real laughs are recognisable
Across all those societies, listeners did better than chance at telling spontaneous laughs from posed ones. They weren't perfect, but the ability appeared everywhere, in every kind of community. It seems that recognising a genuine laugh is a widely shared human skill, not something learned from one particular culture or language.

@list N09 autumn_overlook | How real laughs tend to sound | Higher in pitch ;; More irregular ;; Breathier, with less control ;; Faster bursts
Researchers have pointed to some of the features that set spontaneous laughter apart. It tends to be higher in pitch and more irregular. It's often breathier and sounds less controlled, as if the voice has been taken over. And its bursts can come faster than in posed laughter. In short, a real laugh sounds like it happened to the person, rather than being made by them.

@char N01 cafe_sunset | Polite laughter is not fake | It's a kindness
It's important not to think of chosen laughter as dishonest. A polite laugh is often a kindness. It tells the speaker, I'm listening, I'm with you, I appreciate you sharing this. Sam's work story might not have been hilarious, but the friendly laughter at the end said, we're glad you told us. That's a perfectly good reason to laugh.

@study A20 autumn_overlook | Disa Sauter, Frank Eisner, Paul Ekman and Sophie Scott | Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 2010 | Cross-cultural recognition of basic emotions through nonverbal emotional vocalizations
There's evidence, too, that the sound of laughter itself is understood across very different cultures. In 2010, Disa Sauter and colleagues played recordings of emotional sounds, like screams, sighs and laughter, to members of the Himba community in northern Namibia, who had very little contact with Western culture, and to British listeners.

@char N27 street_day | Understood across the world | Laughter travels well
Laughter was among the sounds that both groups recognised best in each other's recordings. Himba listeners recognised the laughter of British speakers, and British listeners recognised Himba laughter. Some other emotional sounds, by contrast, were harder to recognise across cultures. Laughter, it seems, is one of the most universal sounds we make.

@char N20 autumn_overlook | A note of caution | Labs and recordings
A note of caution, as always. Most of these studies use short recordings, heard without the context of a real conversation. In everyday life, we have faces, situations and history to help us. So these experiments probably underestimate how well we read laughter in person, and also miss how often we simply don't notice whether a laugh is chosen or not.

@list A03 cabin_fireplace | What we can say | There are spontaneous and chosen laughs ;; Brains treat them differently ;; People everywhere can often tell them apart ;; Laughter is recognised across cultures
So here's what we can say. There seem to be two broad kinds of laughter, spontaneous and chosen. Brains treat them a little differently. People around the world can often tell them apart, better than chance. And laughter as a sound is recognised across very different cultures. It's one of the clearest signals in the human repertoire.

The helpless laughter at the teapot made everyone feel like students again. One was chosen, one wasn't, and both were, in their own way, gifts to each other.

Could a stranger, listening to a recording of two people laughing together, tell whether they're old friends or people who have only just met? Some researchers tried exactly that.
