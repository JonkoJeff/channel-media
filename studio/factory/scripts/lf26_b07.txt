@chapter A06 cafe_sunset | Growth after trauma
You may have heard the phrase, what doesn't kill you makes you stronger. It's one of the most repeated lines about suffering, and in the last thirty years, psychologists have taken it seriously enough to test. They found something interesting, and something more complicated than the slogan.

@char A12 living_room_day | The idea | Posttraumatic growth
In the mid-1990s, two psychologists at the University of North Carolina, Richard Tedeschi and Lawrence Calhoun, noticed that many people who had been through terrible events described positive changes as well as pain. They coined the term posttraumatic growth, to describe it. It doesn't mean that the trauma was good. It means that, as a result of the struggle with it, some people report certain changes.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Tedeschi & Calhoun | Journal of Traumatic Stress, 1996 | The Posttraumatic Growth Inventory: measuring the positive legacy of trauma
They created a questionnaire, the Posttraumatic Growth Inventory, and tested it with hundreds of people. It asked about changes in five areas. Relating to others. New possibilities. Personal strength. Spiritual change. And appreciation of life. Many participants who had experienced a traumatic event reported at least some positive change in one or more of these areas.

@list A03 living_room_day | Five areas of reported growth | Closer relationships ;; New possibilities and directions ;; A sense of personal strength ;; Spiritual or existential change ;; Greater appreciation of life
The researchers were careful to say that growth wasn't the same as the absence of suffering. People who reported growth could be in pain at the same time. In their account, growth comes out of the struggle to rebuild shattered beliefs, which fits closely with Janoff-Bulman's theory.

@chapter A13 rooftop_dusk | The critics
As the idea spread, it was embraced by many clinicians and by the public, and then challenged by researchers. The most pointed question was whether the reported growth was real. Asking people if they've changed for the better is not the same as measuring whether they've changed.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Frazier, Tennen, Gavian, Park, Tomich & Tashiro | Psychological Science, 2009 | Does self-reported posttraumatic growth reflect genuine positive change?
Patricia Frazier and her colleagues followed college students over time, and measured their actual levels of functioning before and after a traumatic event, as well as their perceived growth. They found that students' ratings of how much they'd grown were not related to actual changes in their measured functioning. Actual positive change, measured objectively, was associated with different things than perceived growth.

@char A04 attic_rain | The problem | Perceived is not the same as real
This doesn't mean that people are lying. It suggests that the feeling of having grown may be a way of coping, a story that people tell themselves about what happened, and that this story is valuable on its own terms, but not necessarily a measure of change. Perceived growth may be an illusion that helps, in the sense of Taylor and Brown's positive illusions.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Jayawickreme & Blackie | European Journal of Personality, 2014 | Post-traumatic growth as positive personality change: evidence, controversies and future directions
Eranda Jayawickreme and Laura Blackie reviewed the field and concluded that the evidence for genuine, lasting positive change after trauma was mixed. Some studies found real increases in things like empathy and personal strength in some people, but the results varied, and the field needed better studies that follow people before and after, and that don't rely only on self-report.

@list A12 living_room_day | The fair summary | Many people report positive changes after hardship ;; Self-reports don't always match objective change ;; Some growth appears to be real, in some people ;; Growth doesn't cancel pain or make hardship good
That last line is crucial. It's easy for the idea of growth to turn into pressure. The pressure to find a silver lining, to be grateful, to show that the suffering was worth it. The research doesn't support that pressure, and neither do most of the people who have been through the worst.

@chapter A12 living_room_day | The pressure to grow
There's a danger in the popular version of this idea. If people believe that suffering ought to lead to growth, then those who don't feel grown may feel that they're failing. And others, who would like to comfort them, may offer growth as a consolation, which can feel like an insult.

@char A19 cabin_fireplace | A warning | Growth is not a duty
You don't owe anyone a lesson from your pain. If you find yourself changed for the better, that's a real and valuable thing. If you don't, that's also fine, and common. The goal is not to turn suffering into a success story. The goal is to live as well as you can, and to be gentle with yourself while you do.

@char A01 cafe_sunset | A balanced view | Both pain and change
What most researchers now accept is that pain and positive change can exist side by side. A person can be glad of a deeper friendship that came from a loss, and still wish that the loss had never happened. Holding both isn't a contradiction. It's a feature of an honest, grown-up relationship with what life has done.

@list A03 living_room_day | Words to keep from this chapter | Posttraumatic growth ;; Perceived growth ;; Benefit finding ;; Self-report
Four terms from this chapter. Posttraumatic growth is positive change reported after a struggle with a traumatic event. Perceived growth is the sense of having grown, which may differ from measured change. Benefit finding is noticing good that has come from hardship. And self-report is what people say about themselves. Picture this scene. A young man recovers from a serious accident and, a year later, says that he has become a calmer person and a better friend. His doctor is pleased, but his sister notices that he still wakes up at night with his heart racing. Both things are true. The research on growth tells us not to choose between them. He can value the change, and also wish the accident had never happened, and nobody should ask him to tidy that into a lesson. The honest version of his story has room for the gain and the cost.

@list A12 living_room_day | If you notice a change in yourself | Name it without making it a rule for others ;; Notice what you'd still wish were different ;; Share it with someone who'll listen ;; Don't force a silver lining
Here's a gentle way to treat any change you notice. If something in you has shifted for the better, such as closer friendships, a clearer sense of what matters, or a new kind of courage, you can name it, and be glad of it. But you don't have to turn it into a rule for others, or pretend that you'd choose to go through it again. Notice what you'd still wish were different. And don't force a silver lining. If it comes, it comes gradually, and on its own terms. The research suggests that growth is something that happens to people when they're allowed to take their time, not something to be demanded of them.

A green shoot growing through a crack in the stone. It's the usual symbol of growth after hardship, and it's a fair one. The crack was real, and so is the shoot. But the stone wasn't a gift, and the plant didn't need the stone to grow.

@char A15 cabin_fireplace | A gentle thought | Pain is not a prize
If you've been told that your suffering must have a purpose, you can put that down. You don't have to find the good in it. You only have to find a way to live with it, and the research says that most people do. Whatever grows, grows in its own time.

@char A08 cabin_fireplace | Looking back | What growth taught us
So what did we learn? That many people report positive changes after hardship. That reported growth doesn't always match measured change. That some growth appears to be real, in some people. And that growth should never become a demand.
