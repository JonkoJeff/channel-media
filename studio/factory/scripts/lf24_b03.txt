@chapter A12 living_room_day | What a like does
Think of the smallest unit of social media: a like. A tiny thumb or a heart, a single tap. It carries almost no information, and yet many people check their counts, feel a lift when the number is high, and feel a small sting when it isn't. Psychologists have wondered whether a like works as a social reward in the brain.

@char A06 cafe_sunset | The question | A reward made of people
Human beings are intensely social. For most of our history, being accepted by the group was a matter of survival, and a rejection could be dangerous. It would be surprising if our brains didn't treat signs of approval as rewards, and signs of disapproval as threats. The likes just make those signs countable.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Sherman, Payton, Hernandez, Greenfield & Dapretto | Psychological Science, 2016 | The power of the like in adolescence: effects of peer influence on neural and behavioral responses to social media
At the University of California, Los Angeles, Lauren Sherman and her colleagues invited thirty-two teenagers into a brain scanner. They showed them a series of photos, one hundred and forty-eight in all, on a mock photo-sharing app. Each image appeared with a number of likes, supposedly from other teens, though the numbers were assigned by the researchers.

@stat A14 conspiracy_board | Teenagers in the UCLA scan | 32 | shown 148 photos, some with many likes and some with few
The teenagers also saw some of their own photos, again with likes that the researchers had assigned. So the brain scans could compare what happened when a photo was popular, and when it wasn't, while the real quality of the photo was kept out of the picture. It's a neat way of isolating the effect of the number itself.

@char A04 attic_rain | The result | Brain regions that respond to reward
When the teenagers saw their own photos with many likes, regions linked to reward were more active, including the nucleus accumbens, a region that is involved in many kinds of reward, from food to money to praise. Areas involved in social cognition and attention also responded. In plain language, a high number of likes lit up circuits that normally respond to things we value.

@list A03 living_room_day | What the UCLA scan suggested | Popular photos activated reward circuits ;; Own photos with many likes drew a stronger response ;; Teenagers were also more likely to like popular photos themselves ;; The number of likes shaped judgement
That last point is subtle and fascinating. The teenagers were more inclined to like photos that already had many likes, whatever the content. A like, in other words, doesn't only reward the person who gets it. It also shapes what the next viewer thinks about the photo.

@chapter A13 rooftop_dusk | A cautious reading
It's worth slowing down here. Thirty-two teenagers is a small sample. The scanner session is not the same as real life, and the likes were fake. The study also can't tell us whether any of this is harmful, or whether it differs from the thrill of a compliment in person. What it does show is that the social meaning of a number can be strong enough to be visible in the brain.

@char A12 living_room_day | A broader view | Social rewards are old
It also helps to remember that none of this is new. In earlier research, scientists found that being included, praised or valued activates similar reward regions, whether it happens online or in a room full of people. The likes are a new form, and the underlying machinery is ancient.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Eisenberger, Lieberman & Williams | Science, 2003 | Does rejection hurt? An fMRI study of social exclusion
Naomi Eisenberger and colleagues asked people to play a simple ball-tossing game on a screen while in a scanner. Partway through, the other players stopped throwing to them. The participants showed activity in a region called the anterior cingulate cortex, which is also involved in the distress of physical pain. The study has been highly influential, though later work has argued that the overlap with physical pain is more complicated than first claimed.

@char A01 cafe_sunset | The mirror image | Missing likes can sting
If being left out of a game can register in the brain as distress, then it isn't far-fetched to think that a post that no one reacts to can feel unpleasant. Many people describe exactly that: a post that gets little response, followed by the urge to delete it. The social pain system doesn't care that the exclusion is only a number.

@list A12 living_room_day | Why likes matter to us | We are wired to care about acceptance ;; Counts make approval visible and comparable ;; They arrive unpredictably ;; They shape what other people think too
Each of those four features adds something. The first is old, the second is new, the third echoes the pigeons, and the fourth adds a social loop. Together, they make a very strong signal from a very small tap.

@chapter A06 cafe_sunset | What happens when the likes go away?
A natural question follows. If likes are so powerful, what happens if you hide them? In 2019, Instagram tested hiding like counts in several countries, and some researchers, as well as the platforms, were curious about the effects on users. The results were less dramatic than many people expected.

@char A12 living_room_day | What testing found | Hidden counts, modest change
Several small experiments have tried hiding the numbers, and the general picture has been consistent. People report a little less pressure about how a post will be received, and somewhat less comparison, but their overall mood and wellbeing don't change in a big way. They still look at the photos, still compare themselves, and still care how others respond. Exact results differ between studies, so it's safest to say the effect is small.

@char A04 attic_rain | A pattern worth noticing | Small levers, small effects
This is a pattern that we'll see again tonight. In the debate about social media, there's often a dramatic story about one feature, and then careful experiments find that changing that one feature does something modest. That doesn't mean the feature is harmless. It means that the experience of a feed is made of many parts, and removing one rarely changes the whole.

@char A19 cabin_fireplace | A reflective moment | The thumb and the heart
There's something moving, if you think about it, in the idea that a tap on a screen can reach a person's reward system. It says that we are, at bottom, creatures who want to be seen. That wish is not shameful. It's part of what makes us human. The question is only whether the way we are seen online fits the way we need to be.

@photo lf24_b03_01.jpg | Paper hearts and a scan
A paper heart, a paper thumb and a scan of a brain. The study at UCLA brought all three together, and what it showed, in the end, was an old truth: approval matters to us, and our brains notice when we get it.

@list A12 living_room_day | Words to keep from this chapter | Nucleus accumbens ;; Social reward ;; Anterior cingulate cortex ;; Social proof
Four terms from this chapter. The nucleus accumbens is a region involved in reward. Social reward is the value we find in approval and belonging. The anterior cingulate cortex responds to distress, including social distress. And social proof is our tendency to follow what others seem to approve of.

@list A03 living_room_day | A kinder way to treat likes | Post for the few people you'd tell in person ;; Check the numbers at one set time, not all day ;; Notice how a low count changes your mood ;; Remember that the count says little about the post
If likes tug at you, a small structure can help. Many people find it useful to check reactions once or twice a day, at a time they've chosen, instead of dipping in whenever the phone buzzes. That turns an unpredictable reward into a predictable one, and the pigeon research tells us that predictable rewards are much less sticky. It's also worth remembering what the UCLA study showed, that the number itself shapes how people judge a photo. A low count may say more about timing, the algorithm or the audience than about you or your post.

@char A15 cabin_fireplace | A gentle thought | Count what counts
If likes tug at you, you aren't being shallow. You're being a person. A useful habit is to ask who you're hoping will respond to a post, and whether their response is the one that matters. Often the answer is a handful of people, and their warmth doesn't need a number next to it.

@char A08 cabin_fireplace | Looking back | What the likes taught us
So what did we learn? That likes activate reward circuits in teenagers, in a small but careful study. That the numbers also shape what others think. That rejection can register as distress, even from a game. And that hiding the numbers has a modest effect, because the pull comes from many parts.
