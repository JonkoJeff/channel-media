@chapter A12 living_room_day | The words that open doors
Imagine that you're about to tell someone that you disagree with them. You could say, you're wrong about that, or you could say, I see why you'd think so, and I have a slightly different view. Obviously the second sounds gentler. But does the wording actually change how the other person responds? Researchers have tested this with an unusual level of precision.

@char A04 attic_rain | The idea | Conversational receptiveness
Michael Yeomans, Julia Minson and their colleagues call it conversational receptiveness. It's the use of language that signals that you've heard the other person, and that you're open to their view, even while you're disagreeing. They wanted to know which words do this, and whether it changes how people respond.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Yeomans, Minson, Collins, Chen & Gino | Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes, 2020 | Conversational receptiveness: improving engagement with opposing views
In a series of experiments, the team asked people to write responses to opposing views on controversial topics, such as policy and politics, and to rate how receptive other people's responses were. They then used machine learning to identify the features of language that people read as receptive. They tested whether those features made a difference to how people reacted, in real exchanges.

@list A03 living_room_day | Features of receptive language | Acknowledging the other view: I understand that... ;; Hedging claims: it seems to me, perhaps ;; Finding points of agreement: we both want... ;; Positive framing, with fewer negations ;; Avoiding blunt openings like no or but
These features are quite specific, and they go beyond mere politeness. Acknowledgement and hedging, in particular, signal that you've taken the other person's view seriously, and that you hold your own with some humility.

@char A12 living_room_day | The finding | Receptive language is returned
When one person used receptive language, the other person was more likely to reply in kind. People who were addressed receptively were more willing to engage with the topic, and more likely to want further contact with the other person. Receptiveness tended to produce receptiveness, a bit like a smile being returned.

@chapter A13 rooftop_dusk | A cautious note
It's worth saying what the study doesn't claim. It doesn't show that wording will change someone's mind about the topic itself. The effect was on engagement and willingness to continue, which is a necessary first step. A conversation can't change anyone's view if one person has left. But the further step, persuasion, is a separate question, and we'll come to it.

@char A01 cafe_sunset | A fair reading | Opening, not winning
This is a modest but important claim. Language of this kind keeps the door open. It makes it likelier that both sides will stay at the table and keep listening. Whether anyone is persuaded is a longer story. But a conversation that ends in the first two minutes can't even start it.

@list A12 living_room_day | Phrases and their effect | I see why you'd think that: shows acknowledgement ;; I might be wrong, but: shows humility ;; We both want this to work: finds common ground ;; You're just wrong: closes the door
It's tempting to read these as tricks. But the research is about sincerity. People can detect insincere versions, and they backfire. The point is that when you do mean it, a few words can show it more clearly.

@chapter A06 cafe_sunset | Stories beat statistics
Another question is whether we should lead with facts or with experiences. Many of us assume that, in a disagreement, the best weapon is evidence. But a series of studies by Emily Kubin and her colleagues found something different when the goal was to reduce moral disrespect.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Kubin, Puryear, Schein & Gray | Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 2021 | Personal experiences bridge moral and political divides better than facts
Across several studies with thousands of participants, the researchers compared arguments based on facts with arguments based on personal experiences. People who shared a story of how they'd come to their view were seen as more rational and more respectable, and the people who heard them felt less animosity, even if their view didn't change. Facts did less to reduce hostility.

@char A04 attic_rain | Why stories work | A story is hard to dismiss
A personal story is difficult to argue with. Someone's experience is their own, and it tells you why they think as they do, without asking you to concede anything. It makes the person a human being, and not a position. And it leaves room for the listener to remain themselves, which keeps defensiveness low.

@list A03 living_room_day | Facts and stories | Facts can persuade, but often provoke counter-arguments ;; Stories show why someone cares ;; Stories lower hostility more than facts did ;; Both have a place, in that order
The last line is worth noticing. Many people find that it helps to begin with the story, to establish respect, and bring in the evidence afterwards. The reverse order, a barrage of facts followed by a personal remark, tends to feel like an attack.

@list A12 living_room_day | Words to keep from this chapter | Conversational receptiveness ;; Hedging ;; Common ground ;; Moral respect
Four terms from this chapter. Conversational receptiveness is language that shows openness. Hedging is softening a claim to show that you might be wrong. Common ground is a point of agreement that both sides share. And moral respect is seeing someone's view as thoughtful, even if you disagree with it.

@list A03 living_room_day | Rewrite a sentence | Take a blunt sentence you might say ;; Add one acknowledgement of their view ;; Add one hedge ;; Add one point of agreement
Try this exercise with a sentence from a real disagreement. Take the blunt version, the one in your head. Then add three things: an acknowledgement of the other person's view, a small hedge that shows you could be wrong, and a point of agreement. Read the two versions aloud. Many people are surprised by how different they sound, and by how little of their actual position they had to give up. The substance can stay the same. Only the door has changed.

@photo lf25_b06_01.jpg | A door left ajar
A door slightly open, with a thread of warm light on the floor. It's a picture of receptive language: not a surrender, not an invitation to walk in, only a signal that the conversation is still possible.

@list A19 living_room_day | Making a disagreement sound human | Open with what you appreciate ;; Say I think, not it is ;; Name the common goal ;; Offer to be corrected
Here are four small moves that make a disagreement sound human. Open with something you genuinely appreciate, whether it's their honesty, their effort or the fact that they raised the issue. Say, I think, or, it seems to me, and not, it is, because that leaves room for the other view. Name the shared goal, such as, we both want this to work. And offer to be corrected, with something like, tell me if I've got this wrong. Each of these is a few words long. None of them requires you to give up your position. They simply show that you're a person with a view, and not a machine delivering a verdict, and people tend to respond to people differently than to machines.

@char A15 cabin_fireplace | A gentle thought | Gentle isn't weak
It's a common worry that softer language means giving ground. But the studies suggest that the opposite is true. People who use it are not seen as weaker. They're seen as more reasonable, and they're more likely to be heard. Gentleness is a way of keeping a conversation alive, so that your own view has a chance to be considered.

@char A08 cabin_fireplace | Looking back | What words taught us
So what did we learn? That acknowledgement and hedging signal openness. That receptive language is often returned. That stories reduce hostility more than facts do. And that wording keeps the door open, even if it can't force anyone through.
