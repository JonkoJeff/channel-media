@char A20 rooftop_dusk | A second team | Young and old compared
A few years later, in 2010, the psychologists William Friedman and Steve Janssen published another careful look at the same question. In one study, they compared two groups. One group was made up of university students, with an average age of about twenty one. The other was made up of older adults, with an average age of about sixty eight. Both groups answered the same questions about how fast time seemed to pass.

@study A13 cafe_sunset | William Friedman and Steve Janssen | Published in 2010 | Students around 21 and adults around 68 rated the speed of time
And the result surprised a lot of people. Very few differences emerged between the two groups. The students said time was flying by about as often as the older adults did. On most questions, the last week, the last month, the last year, the two groups looked remarkably alike. Age simply didn't seem to matter very much for how fast those periods felt.

@char A12 living_room_day | One exception | The last ten years
There was one exception. When people were asked about the last ten years, the older adults did tend to say that decade had gone by more quickly. But even here, the effect of age was small. So the one place where age showed up was the longest stretch of time, the kind you can only judge by looking back over a big piece of your life.

@char A10 cafe_sunset | A much bigger sample | Nearly two thousand people
The same researchers then ran a second, much bigger survey over the internet, with nearly two thousand Dutch participants aged between sixteen and eighty. That's a lot of people, across a wide span of life. And again, the pattern was similar. Age had only a small link to how fast time seemed to pass, and the clearest link was, once more, for the last ten years.

@stat A13 living_room_day | Age and the last ten years | 4% | of the differences explained by age
Here's how small that link was. For the question about the last ten years, age accounted for only about four percent of the differences between people. For the general sense of how fast time passes, it was about one percent. So when we picture time speeding up dramatically with every birthday, the numbers in these surveys tell a much quieter story.

@char A03 attic_rain | Something bigger | Feeling rushed
But one thing did matter more than age in that survey. How busy and rushed people felt. People who said they often felt pressed for time, with too much to do and not enough hours, were more likely to say that time was flying. In fact, this sense of being rushed explained about ten percent of the differences, more than age did.

@bars A13 cafe_sunset | What explained the speed of time? | percent of differences | Age, last ten years:4 ;; Age, in general:1 ;; Feeling rushed:10
Let's put those numbers side by side. Age, for the last ten years, about four percent. Age, for the general speed of time, about one percent. Feeling rushed, about ten percent. These are rough numbers from one set of studies, and they don't explain everything. But they gently turn our attention away from birthdays, and toward the way we live our days.

Picture a desk at the end of a long day. A paper calendar, its squares filled with little notes. A cup of tea going cold. A pen lying where it was dropped. Weeks that look like this, one after another, can feel as if they vanish. Not because the person is old, but because every hour is spoken for, and there's never a pause to notice time passing.

@char A15 cabin_fireplace | Hanna in her twenties | Already rushing
That fits rather well with Hanna's diary from her late twenties. That year, she was rushing everywhere. A new job, a move, deadlines, always one more thing to do. And she wrote, even then, that the year had gone by in a flash. It wasn't her age that made the year feel short. It was the pace of her life, and perhaps the feeling that there was never quite enough time.

@char A12 street_day | Women and time pressure | One more detail
The survey found one more interesting detail. On average, the women in the study reported feeling more rushed than the men, and they also tended to say that time was passing more quickly. That doesn't mean anything fixed about men or women. It most likely reflects how busy people's lives were, and how many tasks they were juggling at the time.

@quote A19 rooftop_dusk | Busy lives can feel like fast lives. | What the surveys hint
So here's what the surveys seem to be hinting at. Busy lives can feel like fast lives. That's not the whole story, and we'll see other pieces tonight. But it's a helpful correction to the popular idea that time speeds up automatically with every year. Age may play a small part, especially for the longest stretches. But how we fill our days seems to matter at least as much.

@char A03 living_room_day | Verdict on the second myth | Partly true, but small
So what's the verdict on our second popular belief? It's partly true. Older people, on average, are a little more likely to say the last ten years flew by. But the effect is much smaller than most of us assume, and it doesn't show up clearly for shorter periods like a week or a month. Many young people feel it too, especially when life is hectic.

@char A10 bedroom_night | So why the old idea? | It still feels true
And yet, the feeling that time speeds up with age is so common, and so strongly held, that people have tried to explain it for well over a century. One of the oldest explanations is beautifully simple. It doesn't involve the brain or the body at all. It's just a matter of proportion. And it's worth looking at carefully, because you've almost certainly heard some version of it.

@chapter A12 cabin_fireplace | The ratio idea
In 1877, the French philosopher Paul Janet suggested that we judge a stretch of time in proportion to the whole of our life so far. A few years later, in 1890, the American psychologist William James discussed this idea in his book The Principles of Psychology. And since then, it's been repeated countless times, in books, in classrooms, and in late night conversations about where the years have gone.

@char A20 street_day | A simple sum | A year as a slice of life
Here's how it works. To a five year old, one year is a fifth of their whole life. That's a huge slice. To a fifty year old, one year is a fiftieth of their life, a much thinner slice. So, the idea goes, each new year feels shorter and shorter, because it's measured against a longer and longer life. It's elegant, and it seems to explain the feeling at once.
