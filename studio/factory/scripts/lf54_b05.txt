As the train gathers speed again, Oskar finds himself thinking about his sister, who showed him a scan picture of her baby a few months before it was born. A grey curve, a tiny hand, the outline of a face. What he didn't know then is that if the scan had run long enough, he might well have watched his nephew yawn.

@char N09 living_room_day | Yawning in the womb | Long before the first breath of air
Babies yawn before they're born. Doctors and researchers watching ultrasound images have noticed it for years: the jaw opens slowly, stays wide for a moment, and closes more quickly. It has the same lopsided shape as the yawn you did this evening. And this happens in someone who is surrounded by fluid and has never taken a single breath of air.

@char N20 autumn_overlook | A clue about oxygen | No air to gulp
That last detail is worth pausing over. A baby in the womb receives oxygen through the placenta. Opening its mouth wide doesn't bring in any air at all. So whatever a fetal yawn is doing, it can't be fetching oxygen in the way the popular story describes. It's another quiet reason to doubt that fetching air is the whole point of the movement.

@study A03 cabin_fireplace | Nadja Reissland, Brian Francis and James Mason | PLoS ONE, published 2012 | Yawns on four-dimensional ultrasound
But how do you know a fetal yawn really is a yawn, and not just a mouth opening? In 2012, Nadja Reissland and her colleagues set out to separate the two. They used four-dimensional ultrasound, which is moving three-dimensional imaging, to watch fifteen healthy fetuses, eight girls and seven boys, at roughly twenty-four, twenty-eight, thirty-two and thirty-six weeks of pregnancy.

@list A10 cafe_sunset | Telling a yawn from an open mouth | A yawn opens slowly ;; Holds at its widest ;; Closes faster than it opened ;; An ordinary mouth opening lacks that shape
The key was timing. In a yawn, the opening phase takes noticeably longer than the closing phase. The researchers measured how long each mouth movement spent opening, holding and closing, and used that to sort true yawns from other openings. Across fifty-eight scans, they counted fifty-six yawns and twenty-seven mouth openings that didn't have the yawning shape. Almost all the yawns showed that slow rise clearly.

There's something touching about the thought that one of the first things a person ever does with their face is also one of the things we'll do on the last tired evening of an ordinary week. @stat N27 street_day | Fetal yawns between 24 and 36 weeks | Fewer | the average count fell steadily to none at the last scan
There was also a change over time. The average number of yawns observed fell from about two at twenty-four weeks to fewer at each later visit, and none were counted at the thirty-six week scans. The ordinary mouth openings declined with age too. Girls and boys didn't differ. The authors suggested this changing pattern might reflect how the brain is maturing.

@char N22 bedroom_night | Fifteen babies | A small window
Again, a note of caution. This was fifteen fetuses, watched for limited stretches of time. Not seeing a yawn during a scan doesn't mean a baby never yawns at that stage, and newborns certainly yawn. What the study offers is a careful way to identify fetal yawns and an early hint that their frequency changes during pregnancy. Larger studies would help to fill in the picture.

@char N14 attic_rain | What could it be for? | Ideas about development
So why would anyone yawn in the womb? One idea is that the movement plays some part in development, perhaps in the growth of the jaw or in the maturing of the brain areas that control it. Many movements that will later be useful are rehearsed before birth. For yawning, these remain suggestions. The honest answer is that we don't yet know.

@char N29 street_day | Newborns | Yawning from day one
Once babies arrive, yawning is unmistakable. Anyone who has held a newborn has probably watched that tiny face open into an enormous, serious yawn, often followed by a little shudder and a return to sleep. They haven't copied it from anyone. They're too young for that, as we'll see later. This is the spontaneous yawn in its purest form, built in from the start.

@list N22 attic_rain | One behaviour, many moments | Before birth ;; In the first days of life ;; On waking and before sleep ;; In dull and tense moments
Look at how wide the range is now. Before birth, in the first days of life, on waking, before sleep, during dull half hours, and perhaps before nerve-racking moments. Any explanation of spontaneous yawning needs to live comfortably with all of these. That's a demanding test, and it's one reason the question has stayed open for so long.

@char A12 street_day | The arousal idea | Shifting gears
One family of explanations says yawning helps regulate arousal, meaning how alert and activated the brain is. The deep breath, the stretch of the jaw and the quickening heart might help nudge the system when it's sagging, or help it through a change of state. It fits the daily peaks, the dull viewing and the backstage nerves reasonably well.

@char N14 bedroom_night | The difficulty | Does it actually wake you up?
The difficulty is showing that a yawn changes your alertness in a lasting way. The changes in heart rate and breathing that researchers record are brief, and some of them look like the ordinary effects of any very deep breath. If a yawn is a wake-up call, it seems to be a short one. Oskar can confirm that his last yawn didn't leave him feeling like a new man.

@char N09 living_room_day | Signals to others | A third kind of idea
Another family of ideas looks outward. A yawn is very visible and, often, audible. Perhaps in group-living animals it carries information: I'm winding down, I'm waking up, my state is changing. If others pick up that signal and shift along with it, a group might stay better in step. We'll return to this when we meet some lions, who have plenty to say on the subject.

@quote N10 cafe_sunset | An old movement may serve more than one purpose | A reasonable way to hold the question
It's worth remembering that these ideas aren't necessarily rivals. A behaviour that's very old can end up serving several purposes. It might help with a bodily adjustment in a single animal and also, in social species, work as a cue to others. Insisting on exactly one purpose may be asking nature to be tidier than it usually is.

@list N01 rooftop_dusk | Where we are | Fetuses yawn without breathing air ;; True yawns have a slow opening ;; Their number fell during pregnancy in one study ;; The purpose is still open
So far from the womb we've learned that fetuses yawn without any air to breathe, that a yawn can be told apart by its slow opening, and that in one small study the count fell as pregnancy went on. The purpose remains open. But there's one idea about purpose that has drawn a lot of attention, and it involves something unexpected.

@char N27 autumn_overlook | Next stop | A matter of temperature
The idea is that yawning helps to cool the brain. It sounds odd the first time you hear it. A yawn as a kind of ventilation, not for oxygen but for heat? Yet it leads to some testable predictions, and researchers have tried them out with warm packs, cold packs, careful breathing, and questions put to strangers on city streets.
