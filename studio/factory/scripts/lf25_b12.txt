@chapter A06 cafe_sunset | Speaking another person's values
Suppose you want to persuade someone who doesn't share your outlook. A natural way is to make the case in the terms that convince you. But what convinces you may not move them at all, because the values behind the argument are different. A group of researchers asked what happens if you make the same argument, but in the other person's moral language.

@char A12 living_room_day | The background | Moral foundations
The psychologist Jonathan Haidt and his colleagues proposed that human morality rests on several foundations, including care, fairness, loyalty, authority and sanctity, or purity. They argued that people across the political spectrum use all of them, but weigh them differently. Liberals, they found, tend to emphasise care and fairness, while conservatives draw on all of them more evenly.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Graham, Haidt & Nosek | Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 2009 | Liberals and conservatives rely on different sets of moral foundations
In large surveys, Jesse Graham and his colleagues found that liberals gave much more weight to harm and fairness, while conservatives gave more weight to loyalty, authority and purity, along with harm and fairness. The theory has been influential, though other researchers have questioned how many foundations there really are, and how well the measures work.

@list A03 living_room_day | The five foundations, in short | Care: protecting people from harm ;; Fairness: reciprocity and justice ;; Loyalty: standing by your group ;; Authority: respect for tradition and order ;; Purity: sanctity and avoiding contamination
The framework is debated, as we noted. But it gives researchers a useful way to ask a practical question: can you move someone by reframing an argument in the values that they care about?

@chapter A13 rooftop_dusk | The experiment
Matthew Feinberg and Robb Willer tested this with a topic where the political divide is deep: climate change. They noticed that environmental messages usually emphasise harm and care, which appeal more to liberals. They wondered whether framing the same issue in terms of purity might reach conservatives.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Feinberg & Willer | Psychological Science, 2013 | The moral roots of environmental attitudes
In experiments, people read about the environment either in terms of harm, or in terms of purity, such as keeping nature clean and avoiding contamination. Conservatives who read the purity framing expressed stronger pro-environmental views than those who read the harm framing, narrowing the usual political gap. Liberals were largely unaffected by the change in framing.

@char A04 attic_rain | The result | Same issue, different language
The substance of the argument didn't change. It was still about protecting the environment. What changed was the moral language in which it was presented. The conservative readers heard something that spoke to their values, and not something that spoke to someone else's.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Feinberg & Willer | Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin, 2015 | From gulf to bridge: when do moral arguments facilitate political influence?
In further studies, the pair tested arguments about topics such as same-sex marriage and military spending. Arguments framed in the moral language of the audience were more persuasive than arguments in the speaker's own. A message about equality for same-sex couples framed in terms of patriotism and loyalty, for instance, was more convincing to conservatives than a message about fairness.

@list A12 living_room_day | Reframing examples | A health argument for conservatives: protecting your family and community ;; An environmental argument for conservatives: keeping the land clean and pure ;; A security argument for liberals: protecting people from harm ;; A tradition argument for liberals: honouring the best of what came before
The idea isn't to pretend to hold views you don't. It's to find, within your own convictions, a reason that your listener can recognise as theirs. That takes some effort, and some real understanding of what they care about.

@chapter A12 living_room_day | The ethics of reframing
It's fair to ask whether this is manipulation. If you choose your words to persuade someone, aren't you steering them? Researchers who work on this generally argue that moral reframing is honest if the argument is one you really hold, and if it's made openly. It's like speaking someone's language, not like lying to them.

@char A01 cafe_sunset | A dividing line | Honest bridging, not trickery
There is a difference between finding a true common ground and inventing one. If you say that a policy protects family and you believe that it does, you're bridging. If you pick a value only to get your way, and don't believe it, people tend to sense the insincerity, and trust suffers. The same test applies as for receptive language: sincerity.

@char A19 cabin_fireplace | A caution | Not all differences are framing
Finally, we should be honest about the limits. Reframing helped to narrow gaps on some topics, but it won't resolve a disagreement that is truly about values. Sometimes two people do care about different things, and no phrase can make them care about the same one. Even then, understanding the other person's values is the first step towards a respectful disagreement.

@list A03 living_room_day | Words to keep from this chapter | Moral foundations ;; Moral reframing ;; Value-congruent message ;; Common ground
Four terms from this chapter. Moral foundations are the basic values that shape moral judgement. Moral reframing means presenting an argument in the other person's values. A value-congruent message fits the audience's values. And common ground is a belief or goal that both sides share.

@list A12 living_room_day | Finding the bridge | Ask what matters most to them about the issue ;; Listen for the value behind the opinion ;; Find a reason from your side that touches that value ;; Say it in their terms, honestly
Here's how to apply it. Begin by asking the other person what matters most to them about the issue. Listen for the value underneath, such as safety, fairness, loyalty or tradition. Then look at your own reasons, and find one that touches it. Say it in their terms, and honestly. You may find that the value was a point of agreement all along, and that you were arguing about the route, not the destination.

#ai lf25_b12_01.jpg | Editorial still-life illustration of a small wooden footbridge over a calm stream between two grassy banks at dusk, soft light, muted palette, calm documentary mood, no people, no text, no logos

@photo lf25_b12_01.jpg | A footbridge over a stream
A small footbridge between two grassy banks. The moral reframing studies suggest that most disagreements are not oceans. They are streams, and what's needed is a bridge made of something that both sides already walk on.

@list A19 living_room_day | Finding the value behind the view | Ask what they're protecting ;; Ask what they're afraid of losing ;; Ask what a good outcome would look like to them ;; Listen for words like safety, fairness, loyalty, tradition
Here are four ways to find the value behind a view. Ask what they're protecting, because most opinions defend something. Ask what they're afraid of losing, which often reveals the stake. Ask what a good outcome would look like for them, in their own words. And listen for the vocabulary of values: safety, fairness, loyalty, tradition, freedom, family. When you hear one, you've found a door. It may be a value you share, and if it isn't, at least you know what they care about. Many disagreements that seem to be about facts or policy turn out, on closer look, to be about what we fear losing, and that's a conversation that people can have with respect.

@char A15 cabin_fireplace | A gentle thought | Find what they love
Behind almost every opinion is something that a person loves, or fears losing. Their children, their home, their tradition, their freedom. If you can see that, and say it back, the disagreement becomes a conversation between two people who care. And that is a very different kind of conversation.

@char A08 cabin_fireplace | Looking back | What values taught us
So what did we learn? That people draw on different moral foundations, and weigh them differently. That purity framing narrowed the gap on environmental views among conservatives. That arguments in the audience's language were more convincing. And that honesty is the line between bridging and trickery.
