@study N09 cafe_sunset | Lassi Liikkanen | Psychology of Music, 2012 | A national survey of earworms in Finland
In 2012, the Finnish researcher Lassi Liikkanen published the results of a very large online survey about earworms, carried out in Finland, in the journal Psychology of Music. More than eleven thousand people took part, which makes it one of the biggest studies of the experience ever done. It was designed to find out how common earworms are, and which people have them most.

@stat N10 living_room_day | Earworm at least once a week | 90% | of people, in a large Finnish survey
The headline finding has been quoted in almost every earworm paper since. Over ninety percent of people said they had an earworm at least once a week. In other words, for the great majority of us, this isn't an occasional oddity. It's a regular part of ordinary life, something that happens most weeks of the year, and often more than once.

@quote N07 cafe_sunset | At least 90 percent of people once a week. | Victoria Williamson, speaking to NPR, 2012
The music psychologist Victoria Williamson, then at Goldsmiths, University of London, put the same idea simply in a radio interview in 2012. Earworms, she said, happen to at least ninety percent of people once a week. When something happens to nine people in ten, every week, it's no longer really a quirk. It's a feature of how human minds work.

@char N09 cafe_sunset | How long do they last? | Surveys and diaries disagree
So, how long does an earworm last? Here, the two methods used by Beaman and Williams gave quite different answers. In their survey, where people described their earworms from memory, a typical episode was said to last several hours. But in the diary study, where people wrote down each earworm as it happened, the typical episode lasted about twenty-seven minutes.

@bars N10 living_room_day | How long an earworm lasted | minutes | Diary study, typical episode:27
Twenty-seven minutes, in the diary study. That's still a while to have a song looping in your head, but it's much less than several hours. One likely reason for the difference is that the most stubborn earworms are the ones we remember best. When we look back, the earworm that lasted all afternoon stands out, and the dozens of short ones simply fade from memory.

@char N20 cabin_fireplace | Most don't come back | Gone by tomorrow
The diaries showed something else that's reassuring. Very few earworms came back again on the same day, once they had faded, and most were gone by the following day. So the picture of an earworm as something that haunts you for days, like the jingle in Twain's story, is the exception. The usual pattern is a visit of half an hour or so, and then it's over.

@stat N07 cafe_sunset | Earworms described as unpleasant | 33% | in the four-week diary study
And here's perhaps the biggest surprise. In the diary study, only about a third of earworms were described as unpleasant. Two thirds were neutral or even pleasant. We tend to talk about earworms as annoying, because the annoying ones are the ones we complain about. But most of the time, a tune in the head is just company, a bit of background music that the mind supplies for free.

A kettle on the stove in a quiet kitchen. Ruth's earworm right now is probably like most of them: not distressing, just there. If she stopped to rate it, she might call it mildly irritating, or she might admit that she rather likes the chorus. Researchers have found that how we feel about an earworm depends a lot on the song, on our mood and on what we're trying to do at the time.

@char N09 living_room_day | The songs people like | Not usually the worst ones
That links to another finding, from Ira Hyman and his colleagues at Western Washington University. They found that earworms are usually songs that people know and like, not the most annoying songs they've heard. We remember the irritating ones, of course, because they stand out. But the typical earworm is a tune we're quite fond of, which may be part of why it plays so freely.

Let's gather the numbers so far. In the large Finnish survey, over ninety percent of people had an earworm at least once a week. In the diary study, a typical episode lasted about twenty-seven minutes, most were gone by the next day, and only about a third were described as unpleasant. That's a gentler picture than the one most of us carry around.

@char N20 cabin_fireplace | Why this matters | A normal mind at work
These numbers matter for a simple reason. If you sometimes worry about how often songs get stuck in your head, it helps to know that almost everyone has the same experience, and that it's usually short and harmless. Researchers study earworms precisely because they're so normal. They're a way of watching the ordinary memory system doing something visible.
