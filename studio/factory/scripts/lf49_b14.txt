@chapter N10 cabin_fireplace | Making peace with wandering
Anton is lying in bed with the lamp on low, and the quiet notebook beside him. He feels, for the first time this evening, that he doesn't need to do anything about his wandering mind. And that's a good place to be, because one of the most hopeful ideas in this field is about exactly that.

@char N02 living_room_day | A different relationship | Noticing without fighting
Many of the approaches that researchers have tested don't try to stop the mind from wandering at all. Instead, they try to change how a person relates to it. To notice the drift sooner, and with less judgment, and to return, again and again, without a fuss. That's the heart of what's often called mindfulness practice.

@char N19 cafe_sunset | A simple exercise | Following the breath
The classic exercise is as plain as it sounds. You sit, and follow the sensation of your breathing. At some point, your mind will wander, as it always does. When you notice, you gently bring your attention back to the breath. That's the whole exercise. The noticing is not the failure. The noticing is the repetition that builds the skill.

@study N07 autumn_overlook | Judson Brewer and colleagues | PNAS, 2011 | Experienced meditators and novices were scanned during different kinds of meditation
In 2011, Judson Brewer and colleagues published a study in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, in which they scanned the brains of experienced meditators and of beginners. The experienced meditators showed less activity in key regions of the default mode network during several kinds of meditation. And they showed different connections between that network and regions linked with control and attention.

@char N23 rooftop_dusk | What that might mean | A caution
That's an intriguing result, and it fits the idea that practice changes how the brain handles its own wandering. But it was a comparison between groups, not a test of changing people with training, so it can't tell us that meditation caused the difference. People who take up meditation for years may differ from others in many ways.

@study N07 cafe_sunset | Michael Mrazek and colleagues | Psychological Science, 2013 | Undergraduates took a two-week mindfulness class or a nutrition class, and their reading comprehension, working memory and mind wandering were tested
A better test of cause came from Michael Mrazek and colleagues, in Psychological Science in 2013. They assigned undergraduates at random to a two-week mindfulness course or a nutrition course, and tested them before and after. The mindfulness group showed improvement on a reading-comprehension test, and on working memory, and reported less mind wandering during a laboratory task.

@char N10 living_room_day | A fair note | Short, small, and early
It's important to say that the study was small, and short, and its participants were students. Other studies of mindfulness have had mixed results, and some researchers have warned that the field has sometimes run ahead of its evidence. In 2018, Nicholas Van Dam and colleagues wrote a well-known paper in Perspectives on Psychological Science called Mind the hype, which asked for exactly this kind of care.

@quote N27 cabin_fireplace | Notice the drift. Return. Repeat. That's the whole skill. | a plain summary of attention practice
That's a plain summary of the practice. Notice, return, repeat. Whether it changes the brain in lasting ways is still being studied. But as a way of relating to a wandering mind, it's gentle, free, and available to anyone.

@char N19 street_day | A connection to our last evening | Music and the wandering mind
There's a pleasant link here to the last evening we spent together, which was about the chills that music gives us. Researchers have asked how music affects wandering, too. In 2017, Liila Taruffi and colleagues reported in Scientific Reports that sad music, compared with happy music, was linked to more mind wandering, and to differences in default network activity. Quiet music, in other words, may invite the mind to roam.

@list N11 cafe_sunset | What we learned tonight | Minds wander for nearly half of waking moments ;; The default network is busy when you're not focused ;; Thoughts lean toward plans and memories ;; Wandering has costs in learning and gifts in creativity ;; Content matters: gentle drifts versus worry loops ;; Bedtime helps: write it down, don't fight it
Here's what we've covered. Minds wander in a large share of our waking moments, and the brain is far from idle while they do. Thoughts tend to lean towards plans and memories. Wandering costs us in lectures and reading, and gives us something back in creativity and planning. The content matters, a gentle drift being very different from a loop of worry. And at bedtime, writing things down seems to help.

@char N02 living_room_day | The big idea | A mind that keeps working
If there's one idea that ties it together, it's that the wandering mind isn't a malfunction. It's the brain's default way of working when the outside world asks for less. It rehearses, remembers, imagines and worries, and which of those it does depends on the task, the mood, the hour, and the habits we've built.

@char N19 cabin_fireplace | What this doesn't tell us | Humility
It's also worth being honest about what the research doesn't tell us. Much of it depends on people's own reports of what they were thinking, which are imperfect. Many studies are small, or use students in a laboratory. And plenty of findings, such as the link between wandering and mood, are about associations that we can't yet fully untangle.
