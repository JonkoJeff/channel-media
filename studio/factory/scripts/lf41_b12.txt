@chapter A19 cabin_fireplace | Just the first step
If procrastination is often about a bad feeling at the start, then one of the most useful tricks may also be the simplest. Make the start smaller. Much smaller. Not write the speech, but open the document and type one memory. Not clean the house, but clear one shelf. When the first step is tiny, the bad feeling that comes with it is tiny too.

@char A10 living_room_day | Once we begin | Things often look different
And something interesting often happens once we begin. The task that seemed so heavy from the outside turns out to be lighter from the inside. Ben writes one memory, about Clara at six years old insisting on wearing a cape to school for a whole month. He smiles. Then a second memory comes. Many people find that starting was the hardest part, and the rest follows more easily.

@chapter A13 cafe_sunset | Unfinished business
There's a famous old idea that may help explain why. In 1927, the psychologist Bluma Zeigarnik published a study suggesting that people tend to remember interrupted, unfinished tasks better than finished ones. It's often called the Zeigarnik effect. Later studies have found mixed results on the memory part. But the broader idea, that unfinished goals keep tugging at our attention, has stayed with researchers ever since.

@char A18 attic_rain | A tug in the mind | The open loop
You've probably felt that tug. An unfinished task tends to pop back into your head at odd moments. In the shower. In the middle of a film. Just as you're falling asleep. For Ben, the speech has been doing exactly that for weeks. It's like a small door left open somewhere in the mind, letting in a little draught.

@study A10 cafe_sunset | E. J. Masicampo and Roy Baumeister | Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 2011 | Does making a plan quiet thoughts about unfinished goals?
In 2011, E. J. Masicampo and Roy Baumeister published a set of studies that looked at that draught. They found that unfinished goals did tend to intrude on people's thoughts and get in the way of other tasks. But there was a twist. When people made a specific plan for how and when they would complete the goal, those intrusive thoughts eased, even though the task itself was still unfinished.

@quote A19 rooftop_dusk | A plan can close the door, even before the work is done. | A comforting finding
That's a comforting idea, especially at bedtime. You don't always have to finish something to quiet it. Sometimes it's enough to decide exactly when and how you'll do it. Writing it down can help too. The mind seems to relax once it knows the task has a place to go. The door isn't locked, but it's gently closed.

@chapter A20 cafe_sunset | If this, then that
This leads us to one of the best studied tools in the whole of motivation research. Psychologists call it an implementation intention, which is a rather grand name for a very simple thing. It's a little if-then plan. If a certain situation arises, then I will do a certain thing. If it's Monday at seven, then I'll sit at the kitchen table and write three memories of Clara.

@study A13 cafe_sunset | Peter Gollwitzer and Paschal Sheeran | Meta-analysis, published 2006 | 94 independent studies with more than 8,000 participants
In 2006, the psychologists Peter Gollwitzer and Paschal Sheeran gathered the research on these if-then plans in one large analysis. It brought together ninety four independent studies, with more than eight thousand participants in total. The studies covered all sorts of goals, from health habits to study tasks, and compared people who made if-then plans with people who simply intended to act.

@list A10 living_room_day | What the analysis found | 94 studies ;; Over 8,000 people ;; Effect size d = 0.65 ;; Medium to large
And across those studies, making an if-then plan had what researchers call a medium to large effect on reaching the goal. The number they reported was an effect size of zero point six five. That's a solid effect for something so simple. It doesn't mean the plans always work, but on average, people who decided when, where and how tended to follow through more often.

Picture a little notebook on a kitchen table. On one page, just a few lines. Monday, seven o'clock, kitchen table, three memories. Wednesday, after dinner, two funny stories. Thursday, read it aloud once. That's the whole plan. It fits on a single page. But it turns a vague, heavy cloud of should into a few small, clear moments that Ben can actually picture.

@char A12 street_day | Why it works | The decision is already made
One reason these plans seem to help is that the decision has already been made. When Monday at seven arrives, Ben doesn't have to wonder whether now is a good time, or whether he feels inspired. The cue arrives, and the action follows, almost like a habit. It takes away one of the hardest moments of all, the moment of choosing to begin.
