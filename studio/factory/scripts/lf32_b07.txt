@chapter A03 living_room_day | Three things that feed the spark
After years of arguing about rewards, Edward Deci and Richard Ryan turned the question around. Instead of asking what damages our interest, they asked what feeds it. What does a person need in order to feel that what they're doing is truly their own?

@study A12 autumn_overlook | Richard Ryan & Edward Deci | Published in American Psychologist, 2000 | Summarised self-determination theory: three basic psychological needs
In 2000, Ryan and Deci published a summary of what they called self-determination theory. At its heart are three basic psychological needs. Autonomy, competence, and relatedness. When these are met, they argued, people tend to be more motivated from the inside, and to feel better while they do it.

@list A13 cafe_sunset | Three needs | Autonomy: this is my choice ;; Competence: I can do this ;; Relatedness: I'm connected to others
Autonomy is the feeling that what you're doing comes from you, that you've chosen it or at least agree with it. Competence is the feeling that you can do it, and are getting better. And relatedness is the sense of being connected to other people, of mattering to someone.

@char A19 rooftop_dusk | Not independence | A common mix-up
Autonomy is easy to misread. It doesn't mean doing everything alone, or never taking advice. You can follow a recipe, or a teacher, or a rule, and still feel autonomous, if you understand it and agree with it. The opposite of autonomy isn't help. It's feeling pushed.

@char A15 living_room_day | A glimpse of yourself | The same task, two ways
Think of washing the dishes. On one evening, you do it because you've decided you want a clean kitchen before bed. On another, you do it because someone told you to, in a tone you didn't like. The dishes are the same. The water is the same. But one feels like yours, and the other feels like a weight.

@chapter A10 cabin_fireplace | The plants in the nursing home
One of the most touching studies about autonomy took place far from any university classroom.

@study A05 living_room_day | Ellen Langer & Judith Rodin | Published in 1976 | Gave nursing home residents more choice and responsibility, including a plant to care for
In 1976, the psychologists Ellen Langer and Judith Rodin worked with residents of a nursing home in Connecticut. On one floor, the residents were told, warmly, that they were responsible for themselves, that they could make decisions about their rooms and their days. Each was offered a plant, and they could choose whether to take one, and care for it themselves.

@char A13 cafe_sunset | The comparison floor | Kind, but done for them
On another floor, the residents were also spoken to kindly. They were told the staff wanted to make their lives as good as possible. They were given plants too, but the staff would take care of them. The message was caring, but it put the decisions in someone else's hands.

@char A02 autumn_overlook | What changed | A little more alive
Over the following weeks, the residents who had been given more choice and responsibility were rated as happier, more active and more alert, by themselves and by staff who didn't know which group they were in. They took part in more activities. Small choices, it seemed, made a real difference.

@char A08 cabin_fireplace | The follow-up | A finding to hold gently
A follow-up study, about a year and a half later, reported that fewer people had died in the more-responsibility group than in the comparison group. That finding is often quoted, but it came from a small number of people, so it's best held gently. The broader message, though, has been echoed many times since.

@photo lf32_b07_01.jpg | Something to care for
Picture a small plant on a sunny windowsill, with a little watering can beside it. It's an ordinary thing. But being the one who waters it, the one who notices when it needs light, can quietly change how a day feels.

@chapter A04 attic_rain | Is more choice always better?
If choice is good for motivation, then surely more choice is even better. That's where one of the most famous studies in this area comes in, along with an important correction.

@study A16 street_day | Sheena Iyengar & Mark Lepper | Published in 2000 | Set up a jam-tasting table in a grocery store with either 6 or 24 flavours
In 2000, Sheena Iyengar and Mark Lepper set up a tasting table in an upscale grocery store in California. Sometimes it offered six flavours of jam. Sometimes it offered twenty-four. More shoppers stopped at the big display. But of the people who stopped, those who saw only six flavours were far more likely to go on and buy a jar.

@char A11 cafe_sunset | The idea it sparked | Too much choice
That study, and others like it, became the foundation of a popular idea, sometimes called choice overload. Too many options can leave us overwhelmed, less likely to choose at all, and less happy with what we pick. It's an appealing story, and it spread quickly.

@study A18 rooftop_dusk | Benjamin Scheibehenne, Rainer Greifeneder & Peter Todd | Published in 2010 | Combined dozens of choice-overload experiments
But in 2010, Benjamin Scheibehenne, Rainer Greifeneder and Peter Todd combined the results of dozens of experiments on choice overload. On average, they found that the effect was close to zero. Some studies found that large choices hurt, some found they helped, and many found little difference.

@char A04 neon_alley_rain | So which is it? | It depends
That doesn't mean the jam study was wrong. It means that too much choice can be overwhelming in some situations, perhaps when the options are hard to compare, or when people don't know what they want, but it's not a universal law. The world turned out to be messier than the headline.

@study A01 autumn_overlook | Erika Patall, Harris Cooper & Jorgianne Civey Robinson | Published in 2008 | Combined studies on how choice affects intrinsic motivation
In 2008, Erika Patall, Harris Cooper and Jorgianne Civey Robinson combined many studies on choice and motivation. They found that being given choice tended to increase people's intrinsic motivation, their effort, and how well they did on the task. But the effects were moderate, and they depended on the kind of choice and the number of options.

@char A15 cafe_sunset | Another experiment | Chocolates
In the same paper, Iyengar and Lepper ran another experiment, this time in a lab, with chocolates. Some people chose one from a small selection, and others from a much larger one. The people with the smaller selection reported being more satisfied with the chocolate they picked. With many options, it seemed easier to wonder whether a different one would have been better.

@char A12 living_room_day | The gentle conclusion | Meaningful, not endless
Taken together, the picture is something like this. Having a real say in what you do helps. But it's not about having endless options. It's about having choices that feel meaningful, that connect to what you care about, and that you feel able to make.

@chapter A20 cafe_sunset | Competence and connection
The other two needs are just as important, and they often work together.

@char A15 autumn_overlook | Competence | The feeling of getting better
Competence is the quiet pleasure of getting better at something. The first time you play a few notes that sound like music, or bake something that rises. Tasks that are too easy give us nothing to grow into. Tasks that are far too hard leave us feeling helpless. Somewhere in between, there's the feeling of progress.

@char A05 living_room_day | Relatedness | Doing it with someone
And relatedness is the sense that we're connected while we do it. A class where the teacher seems to care. A team where you feel you belong. A friend who asks how it went. Many things we'd never do alone become easy, even enjoyable, with someone beside us.

@char A10 cabin_fireplace | A glimpse of yourself | When all three are there
Think of a time when you lost yourself in something. You chose it. You were good enough at it to feel the progress. And maybe someone else was there, or would be glad to hear about it. That's what Ryan and Deci think motivation from the inside looks like.

@char A19 rooftop_dusk | The next question | Setting a target
So far, we've talked about where motivation comes from. But often, we try to steer it on purpose. We set goals. And the research on goals turns out to be full of surprises, including the strange finding that dreaming about success can sometimes drain the energy you need to reach it.
