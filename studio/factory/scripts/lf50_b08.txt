@char N10 cabin_fireplace | A glass of wine | The nightcap
Let's give Nora a different evening, one that she tells herself is a fix. After dinner she pours a small glass of red wine. It's warm, it's soothing, and by ten o'clock her shoulders have dropped. She falls asleep quickly, and for a moment, she feels she's discovered the answer. And then, at around three, her eyes snap open, and her mouth is dry, and her heart is thumping.

@chapter N27 rooftop_dusk | The nightcap myth
It's one of the most widely held beliefs about sleep, that a drink helps you drift off. And in a narrow sense, the science agrees. It's the rest of the night where things get complicated.

@char N19 living_room_day | The true part | Alcohol is a sedative
Alcohol really is a sedative. It acts on the brain's calming chemical systems, and in many people it shortens the time it takes to fall asleep, especially after a first drink. That's one reason why so many people reach for it, and why it has been a folk remedy for centuries.

@study N07 cabin_fireplace | Irshaad Ebrahim and colleagues | Alcoholism: Clinical and Experimental Research, 2013 | A review of studies that recorded the sleep of healthy people after they drank alcohol, with sleep measured by electrodes
In 2013, Irshaad Ebrahim and his colleagues brought together the laboratory studies on alcohol and sleep in healthy people. The pattern they described was remarkably consistent. Alcohol shortens the time it takes to fall asleep, and increases deep sleep early in the night. But it also reduces REM sleep, and in the second half of the night, sleep becomes lighter and more broken.

@char N09 cafe_sunset | The second half | Why the night falls apart
Part of the reason is simply that the body processes alcohol over several hours. As the level falls, there's a kind of rebound, in which the body's alerting systems swing back, and sleep becomes shallower and more fragmented. Many people notice it as waking in the early hours, sweaty, thirsty, or with a racing mind.

@char N02 rooftop_dusk | REM and mood | A quieter loss
REM sleep is the stage in which most vivid dreaming happens, and which many scientists think plays a part in emotional processing and memory. Alcohol, particularly in larger amounts, tends to suppress it early in the night and then cause a rebound later. The research on what this means for the next day is still being worked out, but the pattern shows that the sleep isn't the same as it seems.

@char N13 attic_rain | A surprising habit | Using it every night
There's one more twist. In 1999, Timothy Roehrs and Thomas Roth, working with volunteers with insomnia who were allowed to choose, found that people could develop a tolerance to the sleep-inducing effect of alcohol within a few nights, while the sleep disruption remained. That's a classic trap. A remedy that works the first time can, over days, become a habit that stops helping and starts costing.

@char N10 living_room_day | A balanced view | Not a moral lecture
This is not a lecture, and it isn't an argument that anyone must avoid alcohol. It's simply an honest report of what the laboratory shows. If you enjoy a drink with dinner, the research suggests that the closer to bedtime it gets, and the more of it there is, the more it tends to interfere. Many sleep specialists suggest leaving a few hours between the last drink and bed.

@char N19 autumn_overlook | And if you rely on it | A gentle note
And if you notice that you can't fall asleep without a drink, that's something worth mentioning to a doctor, kindly and without embarrassment. It isn't unusual, and it isn't a judgement. It simply means that a tool that was meant to help is starting to take over, and that there are other, better-supported tools available.

@chapter N20 cabin_fireplace | Food, movement and the evening
Alcohol is not the only thing in the evening that shapes the night. What we eat and how we move matters too, though less than people claim.

@char N02 living_room_day | A cup of warm milk | Grandmother's remedy
There's the old remedy of warm milk. Milk does contain tryptophan, a building block of the sleep-related chemicals serotonin and melatonin, but the amount is small, and tryptophan from food competes with many other amino acids to reach the brain. Scientists haven't found strong evidence that a glass of milk itself puts people to sleep.

@char N09 cafe_sunset | Still, rituals work | The comfort of a routine
That doesn't mean it's useless. A warm drink at the same time each night can act as a cue, a small signal to the body and mind that the day is ending. Whatever the chemistry, a routine that you enjoy and trust is one of the more reliable ways of lowering arousal, and arousal, as we've seen, is the real enemy of sleep.

@char N10 rooftop_dusk | Heavy dinners | Eating late
What about a big dinner? Eating a large, rich meal close to bedtime can cause discomfort and reflux in some people, and that can certainly disturb sleep. Other research is less clear. A small study in 2007 by Afaghi and colleagues found that a high-glycaemic meal four hours before bed shortened the time to fall asleep, but it was only twelve men, and the result hasn't settled the question.

@char N19 living_room_day | A better guess | Comfortable, not stuffed
So the cautious takeaway is modest. Most people sleep best when they go to bed neither hungry nor overfull. If a heavy meal at ten o'clock leaves you uncomfortable, an earlier, lighter dinner may help. If hunger wakes you, a small snack might be better than lying there. It is an individual matter, not a universal rule.

@char N27 cabin_fireplace | Exercise | The late workout myth
And what about exercise? A common belief is that working out in the evening ruins sleep. In 2019, Julia Stutz, Remo Eiholzer and Christina Spengler reviewed the studies on exactly that question and found that evening exercise did not harm sleep for most people, unless it was very vigorous and finished within about an hour of bedtime.

@char N10 bedroom_night | And the bigger picture | Regular movement helps
A broader review in 2015 by Matthew Kredlow and colleagues found that regular exercise was linked with better sleep, with modest effects on how long it takes to fall asleep and how well you sleep through the night. Not dramatic, not instant, but real. A walk in the evening, in particular, combines light, movement and a change of scene, which is almost a recipe in itself.

@char N09 autumn_overlook | Another stimulant | Nicotine
Alcohol and caffeine get most of the attention, but another substance deserves a mention, and that is nicotine. It is a stimulant, and studies comparing smokers with non-smokers have often found that smokers take longer to fall asleep and sleep more lightly. Some of the difference may be due to withdrawal during the night, which can wake a regular smoker, and some to the stimulant effect itself.

@char N02 living_room_day | Fluids and fire alarms | The bladder at three
There's also a very practical piece. A large drink just before bed can mean a trip to the bathroom in the small hours, and for people over fifty in particular, waking to urinate is one of the most common reasons for broken sleep. Sleep specialists often suggest tapering fluids a couple of hours before bed. If you wake very often to go, it's worth mentioning to a doctor, since there are treatable causes.

@char N19 rooftop_dusk | Gathering it together | The evening as a runway
Put all of these together and you can see the evening as a kind of runway. The last caffeine is some hours behind you, the last drink and the last large meal are not too close, the lights are lower, and the body has had some movement during the day. None of this is a rule, and nobody does it perfectly. But each small piece lowers arousal a little, which is exactly what sleep needs.
