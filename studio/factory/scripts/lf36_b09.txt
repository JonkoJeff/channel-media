So, the heat. Sam's bus is the kind of bus you've probably been on. It's autumn outside, cool and grey, but inside someone has turned the heater all the way up. The windows are fogged. Coats are still on, because there's no room to take them off. And the air has that thick, slightly stale warmth that makes everyone a little quieter, and a little less patient.

@char A13 living_room_day | Hot and bothered | An old phrase
We even have a phrase for this. Hot and bothered. Hot under the collar. Blood boiling. Our language seems to have decided, a long time ago, that heat and anger belong together. But is that just a metaphor? Or does warmth around us really change how quickly we lose our temper? That's a hard question to test in a lab. So one group of researchers found a very unusual place to look.

@char A03 cafe_sunset | An unexpected place | A baseball field
They looked at baseball. American Major League Baseball, to be precise. And at first that might sound like an odd choice for a question about anger. But baseball has some very useful features. Games are played in all kinds of weather, from cool spring evenings to scorching summer afternoons. And every single pitch, every hit, every moment, has been carefully recorded for decades.

@study A12 cafe_sunset | Larrick, Timmerman, Carton and Abrevaya | Major League Baseball records | published in 2011
In 2011, the psychologist Richard Larrick and his colleagues Thomas Timmerman, Andrew Carton and Jason Abrevaya published a study in the journal Psychological Science. Its title was Temper, temperature, and temptation: Heat-related retaliation in baseball. They wanted to know whether pitchers were more likely to hit batters on hot days.

@stat A13 rooftop_dusk | Larrick and colleagues, 2011 | 57,293 | Major League Baseball games, from 1952 to 2009
And they had an enormous amount of data. Fifty-seven thousand, two hundred and ninety-three games, played between 1952 and 2009. That's more than half a century of baseball. Inside those games were about four and a half million moments where a pitcher faced a batter. Each one with a temperature, a score, and a record of what happened.

@char A10 autumn_overlook | Hit by a pitch | Accident or message?
Now, a little background for anyone who doesn't follow baseball. Sometimes a pitcher throws the ball and it hits the batter. Usually that's an accident. The ball is fast, and it doesn't always go where it's meant to. But sometimes, as fans know, it isn't entirely an accident. Sometimes it's a message. Especially if the other team has just hit one of your own players.

@char A13 cabin_fireplace | A natural experiment | Nobody chooses the weather
And here's what makes baseball such a clever place to look. Nobody chooses the weather. A pitcher can't decide that today will be cool, or that tomorrow will be scorching. The schedule is set long in advance, and the sky does whatever it likes. So the temperature is a little like a coin toss, handed out to games without anyone's say. That makes it much easier to see what heat itself might be doing, separate from everything else.

@list A15 cafe_sunset | What they compared | The temperature that day ;; Whether a teammate had been hit ;; Whether the pitcher hit a batter
So the researchers compared three things. How hot it was. Whether one of the pitcher's own teammates had been hit by the other side. And whether the pitcher then hit a batter. If heat simply made pitchers sloppier, you'd expect more hits on hot days no matter what. But that's not quite what they found.

@char A18 living_room_day | The pattern | Heat plus a reason
The heat effect showed up mainly in one situation. When a teammate had already been hit. In other words, heat on its own didn't do much. But heat plus a reason to retaliate, a sense that we've been wronged, made retaliation more likely. The warmth didn't create the grievance. It seemed to make the grievance harder to let go. On a cool evening, a pitcher whose teammate had been hit might shrug it off and get on with the game. On a sweltering afternoon, the same moment seemed a little more likely to turn into payback.

@bars A12 cafe_sunset | Chance of hitting a batter after a teammate was hit | percent | Games in the 50s F:22 ;; Games in the 90s F:27
Here's one of the numbers from the study. In the situation they looked at, after a teammate had been hit early in the game, the chance of the pitcher hitting a batter was about twenty-two percent when it was in the fifties Fahrenheit. In the nineties, it rose to about twenty-seven percent. A few points. But across millions of pitches, a few points is a real, steady pattern.

I like this study because it's so far from the lab. These weren't students filling in questionnaires. These were professional athletes, in front of crowds, in real games, doing their jobs. And still, quietly, the temperature showed up in what they did.

@char A13 living_room_day | Not about baseball | About all of us
Of course, this isn't really a story about baseball. It's a story about something that happens to all of us. We feel wronged, and then something in our surroundings makes that feeling a little harder to put down. Heat. Noise. Crowding. A long queue. A bag pressing against your knee on a bus. Think of a kitchen on a hot summer evening, with everyone hungry and the oven on. Or a car stuck in traffic with no air conditioning. Most of us have felt how quickly a small remark can catch fire in a place like that. Not because anyone meant harm. But because the air itself seemed to be leaning on everyone's patience.

@quote A19 rooftop_dusk | The warmth didn't create the grudge. It made it harder to let go. | What the baseball study suggests
That's the key idea I'd like to carry forward. The setting rarely creates our anger out of nothing. But it can turn the volume up on a feeling that's already there. And like hunger and tiredness, it does it quietly, without asking our permission.

@char A10 cafe_sunset | Back on the bus | One more layer
So let's go back to Sam. Hungry. Tired. On a warm, crowded bus. And now, holding a message from his brother that feels, at least for the moment, like a small hit from the other team. The conditions are almost perfect for a sharp reply. Not because Sam is an angry person. But because the evening has stacked the deck.

@list A15 autumn_overlook | The small nudges so far | An empty stomach ;; A short night ;; A warm, noisy bus ;; An ambiguous message
Look at that list for a moment. Each of those things on its own would barely matter. On a different day, Sam might have laughed at his brother's message, or not even noticed the tone. It's when they arrive together that they start to add up. Like small weights on one side of a scale.

@char A12 living_room_day | Context, not excuses | Two different things
I want to be careful here, because there's a trap in this kind of thinking. Knowing that heat or hunger can make us angrier doesn't mean we're not responsible for what we do. The pitchers who hit batters still chose to throw that ball. Context helps explain. It doesn't excuse. But understanding it gives us something very useful. It gives us a chance to catch ourselves.

@char A03 cafe_sunset | A third question | What's around me?
So here's a third small question for Sam, next to the other two. When did I last eat? How did I sleep? And what's around me right now? Am I too hot, too squashed, too surrounded by noise? Sometimes the kindest thing we can do, for ourselves and for the people we love, is to wait until we've stepped off the bus.

@quote A18 rooftop_dusk | Wait until you've stepped off the bus. | A small kindness, for you and for them
There's something almost physical about it. You step out into the cool evening, you take a breath of fresh air, and the world seems to shrink back to its normal size. The message on your phone is still the same message. But it no longer feels quite so loud.

@char A13 living_room_day | Bigger than one evening | Where we grew up
So far, we've looked at things that change from hour to hour. Food, sleep, temperature. But there's another influence on our anger that doesn't change from one evening to the next. It's something we carry with us all the time, often without noticing it. It's the place, and the people, that taught us what anger means. And that's where we're going next.
