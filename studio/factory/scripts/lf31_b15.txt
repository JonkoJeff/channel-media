@chapter A05 autumn_overlook | The memories of a whole life
We've followed one ordinary memory. But you don't have one memory, you have a whole life of them, and somehow they hang together as a story, with you in the middle of it. How does that happen? And why does that story begin where it does?

@study A15 cafe_sunset | Martin Conway & Christopher Pleydell-Pearce | Published in Psychological Review, 2000 | Proposed that autobiographical memories are built from general knowledge about life periods and specific event details
In 2000, Martin Conway and Christopher Pleydell-Pearce suggested that a memory of your own life is assembled in two layers. One is general knowledge about periods of life, such as my school years, or when I lived in that flat. The other is specific details of events. When you recall your fifteenth birthday, you build it by combining the two.

@char A12 living_room_day | A life, in layers | Chapters and scenes
In that sense, your life story has chapters and scenes. The chapters give the outline. The scenes give the colour. And the narrative, which the psychologist Dan McAdams has called a narrative identity, is something you keep writing and revising as you go, in the way you tell your own story to yourself.

@chapter A20 rooftop_dusk | The missing years
Now, let me ask you something that might seem strange. What's your very first memory? How old were you? And what about the years before it?

@char A02 cafe_sunset | An empty beginning | Almost nobody remembers
Almost nobody can remember anything from the first two or three years of life. And yet those are years of extraordinary learning. Babies learn to recognise faces, to walk, to speak. Sigmund Freud noticed this gap in 1905, and called it infantile amnesia, now more often known as childhood amnesia.

@char A01 autumn_overlook | When memories begin | Around three
In studies where adults are asked for their earliest memory, the average age tends to fall at around three to three and a half years, although many people's earliest memories are from later, and some are probably partly shaped by stories told by family. Memories are only sparse until about seven. Then, gradually, the record becomes continuous.

@study A03 rooftop_dusk | Sheena Josselyn & Paul Frankland | Published in Learning & Memory, 2012 | Proposed that the rapid growth of new brain cells in infancy may make it hard to keep old memories
Why does this happen? There are several ideas. One, put forward in 2012 by Sheena Josselyn and Paul Frankland, is that the infant hippocampus is still growing a great many new cells. Those new cells may reshuffle the connections, making it hard to keep older memories intact.

@study A12 living_room_day | Katherine Akers and colleagues | Published in Science, 2014 | Changed the rate of new cell growth in the hippocampus of young and adult mice
In 2014, Katherine Akers and colleagues tested the idea in mice. When they increased the growth of new cells after learning, adult mice forgot more. When they reduced it in infant mice, the infants kept memories better. It's a fascinating hint, though mice are not children, and other explanations, such as language and a sense of self, almost certainly play a part.

@char A19 cabin_fireplace | Not a loss of everything | What stays
It's worth knowing that the early years do leave a mark. They shape how you speak, how you move, what feels safe and what doesn't. The experiences are not gone. What's missing is the ability to retell them as scenes.

@chapter A03 autumn_overlook | The years we remember best
There's another peculiar pattern in a life story, and you may find it in your own.

@study A05 cafe_sunset | David Rubin, Tamara Rahhal & Leonard Poon | Published in Memory & Cognition, 1998 | Asked adults of different ages to recall memories from across their lives
In 1998, David Rubin, Tamara Rahhal and Leonard Poon asked adults, including older adults, to recall events and things they knew from different times in their lives. A strange bump appeared. People remembered a disproportionate amount from the years between about ten and thirty. It's called the reminiscence bump.

@char A15 rooftop_dusk | Why those years? | A few ideas
There are several ideas. These years are full of firsts, first loves, first jobs, first homes, and firsts are memorable. They are also the years when our identity forms, so the events feel like they matter. And, perhaps, they are a time of fast change, where the mind is working hard to record it all.

@study A20 living_room_day | Dorthe Berntsen & David Rubin | Published in Memory & Cognition, 2004 | Asked people to list the events they expected in a typical life, and compared them with their memories
Berntsen and Rubin also noticed something curious in 2004. When asked to list the key events of a typical person's life, people across a culture tend to agree. Falling in love. Leaving home. Having children. Those expected milestones fall mostly in the same years. They called these cultural life scripts, and suggested they steer which events we notice and keep.

@char A02 cafe_sunset | The songs of your youth | A small pleasure
It might explain why the songs of your teens and twenties can feel more powerful than any since. The music was there when everything was new, and the memories attached to it are the best preserved. If an old song takes you straight back, that's the bump, and it's a rather lovely side effect of how a life is stored.

@chapter A12 cafe_sunset | A rosy tint
Before we go back to Tuesday, one last curious habit of autobiographical memory is worth mentioning, because it's rather kind.

@study A01 rooftop_dusk | Richard Walker, John Skowronski & Charles Thompson | Published in the Review of General Psychology, 2003 | Reviewed studies in which people rated how they felt about events at the time and later
In 2003, Richard Walker, John Skowronski and Charles Thompson reviewed research on how the feelings attached to memories change. They found a consistent pattern. The unpleasant feelings linked to a memory tend to fade faster than the pleasant ones. It's called the fading affect bias. Over time, the sting goes, and the warmth remains.

@study A02 autumn_overlook | Terence Mitchell and colleagues | Published in the Journal of Experimental Social Psychology, 1997 | Asked travellers to rate their holidays before, during and after
In 1997, Terence Mitchell and colleagues asked people to rate their trips, such as a bicycle tour or a long holiday, before they went, during, and after. People tended to remember the trip more fondly than they had actually rated it at the time. The researchers called it rosy retrospection.

@char A15 cafe_sunset | A kind quirk | Mostly
It's not true of everyone, and not for every kind of memory. Some people, especially when low, hold on to the painful parts. But for many of us, the mind does a little gentle editing, and keeps the good. It's a good reason to be slightly suspicious of a golden age, and also to enjoy it.

@chapter A12 autumn_overlook | Back to Tuesday
And now, let's go back to the question we started with. What did you have for dinner last Tuesday?

@char A01 living_room_day | Why you can't say | And why that's fine
You probably can't say, and now you know some of the reasons. It was an ordinary moment, so it was processed lightly. It had no emotion to give it a push. Dozens of similar dinners have crowded it, so it's blurred into a gist of dinners in general. And you had no reason to keep it. Your memory did exactly what it was designed to do.

@char A15 cafe_sunset | Why you can say | And why it might not be exact
And if you could say, it was probably because of a cue, a plan, or a habit that gave you a way in. But even then, it was rebuilt just now, out of pieces, and a few of those pieces may be borrowed from other Tuesdays. Both answers are the ordinary working of an excellent system.

@char A05 cafe_sunset | What it means for you | Gentleness
If there's one thing to take away, it might be gentleness. With your own forgetfulness, which is normal. With the odd muddle of a family story. With a friend who remembers an argument differently from you. Memory is not a recording, and no one has a perfect one. We're all doing our best with beautiful, flexible machinery.

And now, there's one thing left that your memory would really like you to do, and it requires no effort at all. Rest. Everything we talked about tonight, the settling, the replaying, the sorting, happens best when you stop trying. So let's slow down, and let the day settle.
