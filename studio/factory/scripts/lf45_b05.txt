@char A13 bedroom_night | One cell, one person | An unusual experiment
Here's one of the more remarkable experiments in the whole of face and memory research. It involved people who were having a serious medical procedure for epilepsy, and who had thin electrodes placed deep in their brains so that doctors could locate where their seizures began. While those electrodes were in place, and with the patients' consent, a research team used them to ask a very different question. What does a single nerve cell do when you see a particular person?

@study A19 cabin_fireplace | Quiroga, Reddy, Kreiman, Koch & Fried, 2005 | Recording single neurons in human patients | Eight patients with epilepsy had electrodes in the hippocampus, amygdala and nearby regions. The team recorded 993 units and showed pictures of people, places and objects.
Here's the study. In 2005, Rodrigo Quiroga and his colleagues recorded from eight patients across twenty-one sessions. Electrodes in the hippocampus, the amygdala, the entorhinal cortex and a neighbouring region picked up 993 units, a mix of single nerve cells and small groups. The patients looked at pictures of famous people, landmarks, animals and objects, about ninety at a time, and the team watched which cells fired.

@char A10 living_room_day | Not many cells | Most were quiet
What they found first was that most cells stayed quiet. Of the 993 units, 132, about 14 percent, responded to at least one picture. And a responsive cell typically fired for only a very small share of the pictures, on average under 3 percent. These weren't cells that said yes to anything with a face in it. They were choosy, and they had favourites.

@stat A12 cafe_sunset | Quiroga et al., 2005 | 7 of 7 | photos of one actress made a single neuron in the left hippocampus fire
And then came the finding that made headlines. In one patient, a single cell in the left hippocampus fired strongly for every one of seven different photographs of the same actress, Jennifer Aniston, out of 87 pictures tested, and for almost nothing else. It's the origin of the phrase, the Jennifer Aniston neuron.

@char A15 street_day | What that means | Not a face detector
Notice what this tells us. The cell wasn't about one particular photograph. It responded to many pictures of the same person, which means it was keeping track of who she was, not just how she looked in a single image. A face detector fires for any face. This cell fired for one identity, however it was shown. It sits one level above the visual machinery, closer to knowing than seeing.

@quote A18 rooftop_dusk | The cell didn't care what the photo looked like. It cared who was in it. | A way to picture it
You could put it like this. The cell didn't care what the photo looked like. It cared who was in it. And that's a very different job from the one the face area does. One tells you, that's a face. The other tells you, that's her.

@char A04 attic_rain | A careful note | Not one cell alone
It would be a mistake to read this as a single cell doing all the work for a single person. The recordings only catch a tiny sample of the cells in a brain, and a cell that happened to be found is hardly likely to be the only one. A better picture is a crowd of cells, each with its favourites, who together hold an identity.

@char A13 cabin_fireplace | A surprise about names | Words and faces together
There was one more detail in that paper, which is easy to miss and fits tonight's story well. In some of the responsive units, 8 of them, the cell fired for a particular person and also for the written name of that person. So the brain does connect faces with names, and in at least some cells, those connections can be remarkably direct. The tie between a face and its name can exist. It just isn't always easy to follow in the dark.

@photo lf45_b05_01.jpg | A web of connections
A helpful way to picture this is as a vast web. Each person you know sits in the web as a kind of point, and many threads run into it. The look of their face. The sound of their voice. The things they've said. The place you met them. And somewhere, among all those threads, the word that is their name. Some threads are thick and some are thin, and the thin ones are easily lost.

@char A19 bedroom_night | Marta's web | Threads to a neighbour
Marta's neighbour is a point in her web, too. A thick thread runs to his face, strengthened by hundreds of glimpses. A thick one to his yellow bicycle. A strong one to his daughter's violin on a Thursday. And a thin, wispy thread runs to his name, heard once or twice, a long time ago, mostly in a noisy garden. When the party called for it, the thin thread was too faint to pull.

@char A12 street_day | Strong and weak | Why it matters
That image gives us a first, gentle answer to the evening's puzzle. Recognition doesn't need the thin thread. It can run on the thick ones, and it does, instantly. But producing a name needs the one thread that's been used least. It's not that the name is gone. It's that the path to it is a faint one, and some nights, a faint path cannot be followed.

@char A12 cafe_sunset | Where the cells were | Memory regions
It's worth noticing where those single cells were found. They weren't in the visual areas, the parts of the brain that handle edges and shapes. They were in the hippocampus, the amygdala, the entorhinal cortex and a neighbouring region, parts of the brain involved in memory. In the hippocampus alone, 27 of the 60 responsive units showed this kind of person-specific, picture-independent response.

@char A10 living_room_day | The numbers | A closer look
The numbers give a sense of how choosy those cells were. Of the 993 units, 343 were single cells and 650 were small groups of nearby cells. Only 132 responded to anything at all, and of those, 51 responded in an invariant way to a particular person, place, animal or object. The vast majority of the brain's cells, at least in those regions, stayed quiet for most of what the patients saw.

@char A19 cabin_fireplace | Seeing and knowing | A step up
There's a helpful way to see the difference between the face region we met earlier and these cells. The face region helps you see that something is a face. These cells help you know who it is. One belongs to the world of looking, and the other to the world of knowing. Between them lies the route that Marta's neighbour travelled, from a pattern of light to a familiar person.

@char A04 bedroom_night | Why it matters tonight | The missing link
And notice what the cell for a person is attached to. Not to a sound. Not to a name. It fires for the person themselves, however they're shown. The name, if it's connected at all, is a further link, a thinner one, which in the experiment was found in just a few of the cells. That's the difference between knowing someone, which is deeply wired, and naming them, which is more loosely tied.

@char A19 bedroom_night | A thought about thin threads | Practice thickens them
Threads get thicker with use. Every time Marta sees her neighbour's face, that thread is strengthened a little. Every time she hears his daughter's violin, another thread. But a name that's never said aloud gets no such practice, and its thread stays thin, however well she knows the man. It's no flaw in the person. It's just the arithmetic of use.

@char A12 street_day | A hundred glimpses | Against three hearings
Imagine counting. A hundred glimpses of a face, from the street, from a window, from across the lane. Perhaps three occasions when his name was said in her hearing. The face has a hundred chances to be learned, and the name has three. It would be astonishing if the name were as strong as the face. And yet that's exactly the standard we hold ourselves to, in the middle of a party.

Of course, this is only a picture, and there's a more careful model that we haven't yet looked at. It's one that psychologists have been using for nearly forty years to explain exactly this kind of slip. It sets out the stages that a face has to travel through before it becomes a name, and it explains, rather elegantly, why Marta could know everything about her neighbour except the one thing she needed.
