@chapter A04 attic_rain | When prophecy fails
In the autumn of 1954, a newspaper in Chicago ran a short story with a startling headline. It reported that a woman in a quiet suburb was receiving messages from beings on another planet, who had told her that a great flood would destroy much of the world on a specific date. Most readers shrugged. But a small team of psychologists saw something different. They saw a rare chance to study what happens to a belief when reality tests it.

@char A12 living_room_day | The psychologists | Festinger and his team
Leon Festinger, a social psychologist at the University of Minnesota, along with his colleagues Henry Riecken and Stanley Schachter, decided to investigate. They believed that this was a natural experiment. A group of people had made a firm prediction, had committed to it, and in a few weeks' time, the prediction would clearly be proven wrong. What would they do?

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Festinger, Riecken & Schachter | University of Minnesota Press, 1956 | When Prophecy Fails: a social and psychological study of a modern group that predicted the destruction of the world
The researchers and several observers joined the group in secret, posing as believers, and recorded what happened over several weeks. The group's leader, given the name Marian Keech in the book to protect her identity, said she received messages through automatic writing. The followers included people who had made big sacrifices, such as leaving their jobs, giving away their possessions and breaking with family and friends.

@list A03 living_room_day | What the believers had done | Left jobs and studies ;; Given away money and possessions ;; Cut ties with sceptical relatives ;; Prepared to be rescued by a flying saucer
This detail is the key to the whole study. The more a person has given up for a belief, the more it costs them to admit it was wrong. That's called commitment, and Festinger thought it was the secret to what happened next.

@chapter A13 rooftop_dusk | The night itself
On the night they expected to be picked up, the group waited. Midnight came and went. Then the early hours passed. No visitors arrived. The believers sat in growing confusion, and some wept. For several hours, the group seemed to be on the edge of collapse.

@char A04 attic_rain | The twist | A new message
Then, in the early morning, Keech received a new message. The group's faithfulness, it said, had been so great that the world had been spared. The small band of believers had spread so much light that the destruction had been called off. The mood of the room changed at once. And in the days that followed, something remarkable happened. The group, which had avoided publicity before, began calling newspapers and speaking to anyone who would listen.

@list A12 living_room_day | Before and after the failed prediction | Before: the group avoided the press and publicity ;; After: they actively sought it out ;; Before: they didn't try to convert others ;; After: they tried to convince as many people as possible
This is the part of the study that people remember. The failure of the prophecy didn't reduce the believers' faith. For those who had been together in the group on the night itself, it appears to have increased their enthusiasm in spreading it. Those who had been alone at home when the time passed, on the other hand, tended to drop the belief.

@chapter A06 cafe_sunset | The explanation
Festinger used this to illustrate his theory of cognitive dissonance. When a person holds two ideas that conflict, such as I gave up everything for this prophecy, and the prophecy was wrong, the result is a feeling of discomfort. And people are motivated to reduce it. One way is to drop the belief. But when the cost has been too great, there's another way, which is to find a reason why the belief is still right, and to seek out others who agree.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Festinger | Stanford University Press, 1957 | A theory of cognitive dissonance
Festinger set out his theory in a book the year after. He proposed that people feel tension when their beliefs and behaviour clash, or when two beliefs clash, and that they take steps to reduce the tension. These can include changing a belief, changing a behaviour, or adding new beliefs that bridge the gap. Having social support, he argued, makes it easier to hold on to a belief despite the evidence.

@char A01 cafe_sunset | An important point | Dissonance is not stupidity
It's easy to laugh at the group in this story. But the principle applies to all of us. We all hold beliefs that we've invested in, with our time, our money, our relationships and our identities. And we all find it harder than we'd like to say, I was wrong. Festinger's study isn't a story about foolish people. It's a story about how human minds protect themselves.

@chapter A12 living_room_day | A caution about the story
It's worth adding that the study has been criticised. Some later scholars have argued that the observers may have influenced the group, and that the researchers' own presence and questions might have contributed to what happened. The study had a very small number of participants and couldn't be repeated. And critics also noted that the group did not behave in precisely the way the theory suggested at every point.

@char A04 attic_rain | The limits | A small, unrepeatable study
The group was small, the observers took part in events, and nobody could run the same event again under controlled conditions. So the study is best read as a vivid case, and as a source of an idea that later experiments tested, and not as proof. The core idea, that people who have paid a high price for a belief tend to defend it, has since been supported in many laboratory studies.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Aronson & Mills | Journal of Abnormal and Social Psychology, 1959 | The effect of severity of initiation on liking for a group
Elliot Aronson and Judson Mills asked women to join a discussion group. Some had to pass an embarrassing initiation, reading aloud sexual words, to get in, while others had a mild one. Afterwards, those who had gone through the severe initiation rated the group, which was actually a dull discussion, as more interesting and attractive. The cost they had paid seemed to raise their liking for what they had paid for.

@list A03 living_room_day | The sunk cost pattern | The more we pay for something, the more we value it ;; Admitting a mistake means admitting the cost was wasted ;; A group that shares the belief makes it easier to keep ;; Leaving often means leaving the group too
This pattern helps to explain why people stay in groups, jobs and relationships that no longer serve them. And it connects to this video's main theme, because a shattered belief is the one moment when this protective instinct is put under strain. A person with a deeply invested belief who is shown a clear counterexample has a choice: protect the belief, or rebuild.

@chapter A13 rooftop_dusk | Reading the signs in yourself
How can you tell if you're protecting a belief or really testing it? Psychologists who study this suggest a few simple questions. They're not tests with a right answer, but they help to bring the process into view.

@list A12 living_room_day | Questions to ask of a cherished belief | What evidence would change my mind? ;; Have I ever heard a good argument against it? ;; Am I defending it because it's true, or because I've paid so much? ;; Who would I lose if I changed my mind?
The last question is often the most telling. Beliefs are rarely only about facts. They are tied to communities, families and identities. Changing your mind may mean losing a place at the table. Knowing that doesn't force a different choice, but it does make it clearer what the choice really is.

@char A01 cafe_sunset | A hopeful point | People do change their minds
The same research that shows how hard it is to give up a belief also shows that people do it. They do it more readily when they can keep their dignity, when they have somewhere to go, and when the person who offers the new view treats them with respect. In the last chapter we'll come back to how that kind of change can be made gently.

@list A03 living_room_day | Words to keep from this chapter | Cognitive dissonance ;; Commitment ;; Sunk cost ;; Rationalisation
Four terms from this chapter. Cognitive dissonance is the discomfort of holding conflicting ideas. Commitment is the degree to which we've invested in a belief. Sunk cost is what we've already paid, and can't get back. And rationalisation is finding reasons to protect a belief or choice. Picture this scene. A man has spent years in a business he built with a partner, and the evidence is growing that it is not going to work. His friends gently suggest that it may be time to stop. He feels himself getting angrier with them, and more determined than ever. He is not being stupid. He is protecting years of effort. The most helpful friend is the one who says, I can see how much this means to you, and I will help you whichever way you decide. Respect, not pressure, is what makes it possible to change a mind.

If you've ever clung to a belief a little longer than the evidence justified, you're in good company. It doesn't make you foolish. It makes you someone who invested. The next step, when you're ready, is not to be ashamed, but to be curious about what the belief was protecting.

@char A08 cabin_fireplace | Looking back | What the failed prophecy taught us
So what did we learn? That a group predicted the end of the world and, when it didn't come, became more committed. That cognitive dissonance can make us defend a belief more strongly. That the study was small and has limits. And that the idea itself has been supported by later experiments.
