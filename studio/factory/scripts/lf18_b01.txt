@chapter A06 cafe_sunset | The dress
In February 2015, a photograph of a dress was posted on the internet by a woman in Scotland, who was baffled by an argument at a wedding. The question was simple. What colour is it? Within a day, the picture had been seen by tens of millions of people, and they had split almost down the middle.

@char A12 living_room_day | The two camps | Blue and black, or white and gold
Some people saw a blue dress with black lace. Others saw a white and gold one. Couples argued, friends fell out, and each side was completely certain. What made it so strange was that nobody was joking and nobody was colour blind. Different healthy brains were looking at the same pixels and reporting different worlds.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Lafer-Sousa, Hermann & Conway | Current Biology, 2015 | Colour judgements of the dress across many observers
Within weeks, vision scientists were studying it. A team at Wellesley College and MIT, led by Rosa Lafer-Sousa, asked hundreds of people to describe the dress. They found that the answers weren't random. They clustered into blue and black, white and gold, and a smaller group who saw blue and brown, and they stayed fairly stable when people were tested again.

@char A10 attic_rain | The puzzle | The same light, read differently
The photo itself is the clue. The pixels in the image are a washed-out, bluish tone. They really are blue-ish. But your brain doesn't want the colour of the pixels. It wants the colour of the object. And to get that, it has to guess what kind of light was falling on the dress, and subtract it.

@char A08 cabin_fireplace | Colour constancy | A trick your brain does all day
This subtraction has a name, colour constancy. A white shirt looks white in the yellow light of a kitchen and in the blue light of a shady street, even though the light reaching your eye is very different. Your brain estimates the lighting, removes it, and tells you what colour the shirt is. You never notice, because it works.

@stat A14 conspiracy_board | A quick estimate | About 57% | saw the dress as blue and black in the Wellesley and MIT survey
In that survey of around thirteen hundred people, a little over half described the dress as blue and black, and just under a third as white and gold. The rest saw other combinations. It's worth saying that these are rough shares from one study, and different surveys gave slightly different figures.

@char A04 attic_rain | The two guesses | Shade or sunlight?
So what's going on? The photo is ambiguous about the light. If your brain assumes the dress is in shadow, bathed in bluish light, it subtracts the blue and sees white and gold. If it assumes the dress is in warm, yellowish light, it subtracts the yellow and sees blue and black. Both guesses are perfectly reasonable, and each is confident.

@char A12 living_room_day | A clue about who sees what | Early birds and night owls
A few years later, Pascal Wallisch at New York University ran a larger survey and suggested that people who spend more time in daylight might be more likely to assume the dress is in shade, and so see white and gold. Those who stay up late and live under artificial light might lean the other way. It's an intriguing idea, though the evidence is not strong, and other researchers have urged caution.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Wallisch | Journal of Vision, 2017 | Individual differences in how the dress is seen, and what they may reflect
It's a good example of how a quirky internet moment can turn into a real scientific question. The honest summary is that your assumptions about light matter, those assumptions come from experience, and scientists are still working out exactly how. What no one disputes is the main lesson: the colour you see isn't simply in the pixels.

@chapter A13 rooftop_dusk | Colour is a construction
Colour is a good place to begin, because it feels so solid. Red feels like a property of the apple. But light itself has no colour. It's electromagnetic radiation of different wavelengths, and what we call colour is something the brain builds from the signals of three types of cone cells in the eye.

@char A01 cafe_sunset | Three kinds of cone | Short, medium and long
Most people have three kinds of cone cells, sensitive to short, medium and long wavelengths. Your brain compares their signals, rather like a painter judging a colour by how much of each primary it contains. Everything you've ever seen, from a sunset to a bruise, was built out of comparisons between those three channels.

@char A10 attic_rain | The proof | Same light, different colour
You can see how much depends on the context. A grey square surrounded by dark squares looks lighter than the same grey square surrounded by light ones. The famous checker shadow illusion, devised by Edward Adelson at MIT in 1995, makes two squares that are exactly the same shade look completely different. When you cover the surroundings, you see they match.

@list A03 living_room_day | What the brain uses to decide | Neighbouring colours ;; Guessed lighting ;; Shadows and edges ;; What it expects the object to be
That list is a clue to something much bigger. Your brain isn't a camera recording what's out there. It's a detective, weighing evidence and reaching a conclusion. Usually, it reaches the right one. The dress is interesting because it landed in a rare place where reasonable detectives disagree.

@char A06 cafe_sunset | Do we all see the same red? | A question older than science
Philosophers have long asked whether your red is my red. Science can say something modest about this. If two people have the usual three kinds of cone, they'll make the same judgements about which colours match and which differ. Whether their inner experience is identical is a question that no experiment has been able to answer.

@char A04 attic_rain | A rare variation | Four kinds of cone
Some people may see even more differences. A small number of women are thought to carry a fourth type of cone, because genes for the cone pigments sit on the X chromosome. In 2010, Gabriele Jordan at Newcastle University reported finding one woman who seemed to discriminate colours in a way that others could not. It's a single case, and researchers still debate how common this is.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Jordan, Deeb, Bosten & Mollon | Journal of Vision, 2010 | A search for tetrachromatic vision in women who carry extra cone genes
The study suggested that carrying a fourth pigment is not enough. The brain also has to use the extra signal, and most people who carry the gene may not. It's a reminder that what we see depends on the hardware, and on the software that reads it.

@chapter A12 living_room_day | Colour and language
Another surprise is how much language may shape colour. Many languages divide the spectrum differently. Russian, for example, has separate basic words for light blue and dark blue, goluboy and siniy. In 2007, Jonathan Winawer and colleagues at MIT found that Russian speakers were slightly faster at telling apart two shades of blue when they fell on opposite sides of that boundary.

@stat A06 cafe_sunset | Reaction time effect | A fraction of a second | Russian speakers were faster at the light and dark blue boundary
It's a small effect, measured in milliseconds, and it disappeared when people had to remember a string of numbers at the same time, which suggests that language was quietly helping. This is not the old claim that language limits what you can see. It's a gentler idea, that the words you have can nudge the way you sort what you see.

@char A04 attic_rain | A careful note | Small effects, real debate
There's a caution here, too. Not every study of this kind has found the same thing, and some effects have been hard to reproduce. The sensible position is that culture and language may influence perception at the edges, while the basic machinery of vision is shared by all of us. We'll come back to that idea when we visit cultures in a later chapter.

@list A12 living_room_day | What the dress teaches us | The brain estimates the light before it tells you the colour ;; Different assumptions produce different, confident answers ;; Neither answer is a mistake ;; The sense of certainty is not evidence
That final line may be the most important. When the dress looked white and gold to one person, they felt as sure as the person who saw blue and black. Certainty and accuracy are different things, and we'll meet that theme again and again in the hours ahead.

A piece of cloth, one window, two kinds of light. It looks simple. But your brain is doing a surprising amount of arithmetic every time you glance at it, working out what the cloth is, what the light is and what colour you should be told.

@list A12 living_room_day | Try this at home | Photograph a coloured object in different rooms ;; Compare how the colour looks to you each time ;; Ask two friends to describe the same colour ;; Notice where you disagree
Colour is a good subject for a small home experiment. Photograph the same coloured object by a window, under a lamp and in a shady corner, then compare. You'll often find the colour you remember is not the colour in any of the photos. That gap between memory, camera and eye is the same gap that split the internet over a dress, and you can see it in your own living room.

@list A03 living_room_day | Words for what you just learned | Colour constancy ;; Cone cells ;; Illusory certainty ;; Top-down influence
Four terms are worth keeping from this chapter. Colour constancy is the brain's trick of discounting the light. Cones are the three kinds of colour receptor in the eye. Illusory certainty is the feeling of being sure when the evidence is ambiguous. And top-down influence is the way expectation reaches down into what we see. With these four words, you can describe most of what the dress taught us.

@char A15 cabin_fireplace | A gentle thought | Be curious about disagreement
It's a mild but lovely thought that the next time someone sees something differently from you, they may not be wrong, or lazy, or stubborn. They may simply be running a slightly different guess. That doesn't mean that all views are equally true, but it can make us a little kinder in the first minute of an argument.

@char A08 cabin_fireplace | Looking back | What the dress taught us
So what did a blurry photograph of a dress teach us? That colour is built, not simply received. That our guesses about light come from experience. That different brains can reach different, equally confident conclusions. And that this is not a flaw in the system, but a glimpse of how the system works.
