TITLE: The Crucifixion: What Happened at Golgotha?
THUMB: The Crucifixion
DESC: On the day of the crucifixion, Jesus was executed outside Jerusalem under Pontius Pilate. Religion Unfolded follows the earliest sources, from Tacitus, Josephus and Paul to the four Gospels, to ask what happened at Golgotha, how the accounts differ, what archaeology tells us about Roman crucifixion, and how the cross became the central symbol of Christianity. We distinguish what the texts report, what historians can say, and what later traditions built. CHAPTER: The Governor
[00:16:36] NARRATION: In Roman Judea, the power to execute rested with the governor. Pontius Pilate held that office from about the year twenty-six to thirty-six. A stone found at Caesarea in 1961 carries his name and his title, and it is the only inscription we have that names him.
VISUAL: Pilate Stone, Israel Museum
SOURCE: wikimedia
[00:16:57] NARRATION: The inscription is partly damaged, but it appears to record that Pontius Pilatus, prefect of Judea, dedicated a building to the emperor Tiberius. It confirms two things: that he existed, and that his title was prefect, not procurator, as Tacitus later wrote.
VISUAL: Pilate Stone inscription, Caesarea Maritima
SOURCE: wikimedia
[00:17:17] NARRATION: Pilate normally lived in Caesarea, on the coast, and came up to Jerusalem for major festivals, when crowds swelled and Roman troops were stationed near the Temple. He is said to have stayed either in Herod's palace in the western part of the city, or in the Antonia fortress.
VISUAL: Herod's palace, Jerusalem, reconstruction
SOURCE: wikimedia
[00:17:39] NARRATION: What kind of governor was he? The Jewish philosopher Philo of Alexandria, writing a few years later, portrays him as inflexible, vindictive and cruel, accusing him of corruption and of executions without trial. Philo had reasons to write harshly, but Josephus also records several clashes.
VISUAL: Philo, Embassy to Gaius 299–305
SOURCE: text-card
[00:18:00] NARRATION: Josephus reports that Pilate brought military standards with the emperor's image into Jerusalem, provoking a mass protest, and that he used money from the Temple treasury to build an aqueduct, which led to a riot. In each case he met crowds with force.
VISUAL: Josephus, Jewish War 2.169–177
SOURCE: text-card
[00:18:20] NARRATION: A governor like this had little patience for anyone who might stir up a crowd at Passover. It's the context in which the Gospels place the trial: a tense festival, an anxious governor, and a prisoner brought before him at dawn.
VISUAL: Roman cohort, Trajan's Column relief
SOURCE: wikimedia
[00:18:39] NARRATION: In Mark, Pilate asks him one question: are you the King of the Jews? Jesus answers, you say so. The chief priests accuse him of many things, but Jesus does not respond, and Pilate is amazed.
VISUAL: Mark 15:2–5
SOURCE: text-card
[00:18:55] NARRATION: The title King of the Jews is the charge that matters to Rome. A Jewish religious dispute, such as blasphemy, would not interest a Roman governor. A claim to kingship, however, was a challenge to the emperor. It is the reason that the Gospels say Pilate had to act.
VISUAL: Roman denarius of Tiberius
SOURCE: wikimedia
[00:19:18] NARRATION: Luke adds a scene in which Pilate, learning that Jesus is from Galilee, sends him to Herod Antipas, who was in Jerusalem for the festival. Herod questions him at length, receives no answer, mocks him, and sends him back to Pilate.
VISUAL: Luke 23:6–12
SOURCE: text-card
[00:19:37] NARRATION: John gives the longest conversation. Jesus tells Pilate that his kingdom is not from this world, and Pilate asks, what is truth? In John, the scene is a drama of two kinds of power, with the governor strangely unsure of himself.
VISUAL: John 18:33–38

[00:19:56] NARRATION: The Gospels portray Pilate as reluctant, saying that he found no case against Jesus. Many historians think that this portrait has been softened. CHAPTER: Barabbas and the Crowd
[00:20:46] NARRATION: The Gospels say that it was the custom at the festival for the governor to release one prisoner of the people's choice. A man named Barabbas, held with rebels who had committed murder in an uprising, was in prison at the time.
VISUAL: Mark 15:6–7
SOURCE: text-card
[00:21:06] NARRATION: Pilate asks the crowd whether they want the King of the Jews released. The chief priests stir up the crowd to ask for Barabbas instead. When Pilate asks what to do with the one they call king, they cry, crucify him.
VISUAL: Mark 15:8–14
SOURCE: text-card
[00:21:24] NARRATION: In Matthew, Pilate's wife sends a message about a dream, warning him to have nothing to do with that innocent man. Pilate takes water and washes his hands in front of the crowd, saying that he is innocent of this man's blood.

[00:21:44] NARRATION: Matthew then adds that all the people answered, his blood be on us and on our children. This verse has had a terrible history. It was used for centuries to justify hatred of Jews, though there is no historical basis for treating a crowd's cry as a verdict on a people.
VISUAL: Matthew 27:25
SOURCE: text-card
[00:22:07] NARRATION: Modern scholars point out several problems with the whole scene. There is no other record of a Roman custom of releasing a prisoner at Passover, and it would be unusual for a governor like Pilate to hand over the decision to a crowd.

SOURCE: text-card
[00:22:27] NARRATION: Some have suggested that the Gospel writers, writing after the Jewish revolt against Rome, shifted blame from the Roman governor to the Jewish crowd, to protect their communities in a world where Rome held the power. [00:22:51] NARRATION: The name Barabbas itself is interesting. It means son of the father, in Aramaic. Some manuscripts of Matthew give his full name as Jesus Barabbas, so that the crowd's choice would have been between two men called Jesus. Scholars debate whether that reading is original.

[00:23:11] NARRATION: What the story preserves is a memory that Jesus was condemned in the shadow of a rebel's release, at a time when Roman power and popular unrest were close to the surface. Whatever the details, the outcome is not in doubt: Pilate gave the order.

CHAPTER: The Soldiers' Game
[00:24:14] NARRATION: Before crucifixion, a Roman prisoner was usually flogged. The practice was brutal and was meant to weaken the condemned, and to make a public example. Mark and Matthew mention it only briefly, in a single phrase, without describing the suffering.
VISUAL: Mark 15:15; Matthew 27:26
SOURCE: text-card
[00:24:32] NARRATION: Josephus gives an example of what a Roman scourging could mean. He describes a man named Jesus son of Ananias, who predicted the Temple's destruction in the years before the revolt. The Jewish authorities beat him, then handed him to the Roman governor, who had him scourged until his bones showed.
VISUAL: Josephus, Jewish War 6.300–309
SOURCE: text-card
[00:24:56] NARRATION: After the flogging, the soldiers take Jesus into the governor's headquarters and gather the whole cohort. They dress him in a purple cloak, press a crown of thorns on his head, and salute him with the words, hail, King of the Jews.
VISUAL: Mark 15:16–19
SOURCE: text-card
[00:25:15] NARRATION: They strike his head with a reed, spit on him, and kneel in mock homage. The scene is a parody of a royal court. A Roman soldier knew exactly what a claimed king looked like, and the soldiers made a cruel joke of it.
VISUAL: Roman Imperial court scene, Ara Pacis
SOURCE: wikimedia
[00:25:36] NARRATION: Historians note that mock-kingship scenes appear in other ancient sources. The Jewish writer Philo describes an incident in Alexandria in the year thirty-eight in which a crowd dressed a madman as a king to mock the Jewish king Agrippa. Such games were a known form of humiliation.
VISUAL: Philo, Against Flaccus 36–39
SOURCE: text-card
[00:25:57] NARRATION: The crown of thorns is a vivid image, but historians cannot say exactly what plant was used, and the Gospels do not say how it was made. In art, it has been drawn in countless forms, from a small circlet to a heavy wreath.
VISUAL: Crown of thorns reliquary, Notre-Dame de Paris
SOURCE: wikimedia
[00:26:18] NARRATION: Then, Mark says, when they had mocked him, they stripped him of the purple cloak, put his own clothes back on him, and led him out to crucify him. The mockery ends where the Roman procedure begins.
VISUAL: Mark 15:20
SOURCE: text-card
[00:26:35] NARRATION: Notice how restrained the Gospels are. They describe what was done in a few lines, without lingering. Some ancient readers might have found that surprising, for a world used to public executions. Others may have known precisely what the short phrases implied.
VISUAL: Codex Alexandrinus, Mark 15

[00:26:54] NARRATION: The restraint is part of why later artists filled the silence. Over the centuries, painters and sculptors have imagined the scenes in great detail, shaped by the sufferings of their own times and by their own faith.

CHAPTER: How Rome Crucified
[00:27:28] NARRATION: To understand the next scene, we need to know what crucifixion was in the Roman world. It was not a single method, but a range of cruel public punishments, usually reserved for slaves, rebels and those without citizenship. Roman citizens were normally spared it.
