@chapter A03 living_room_day | Progress: the pull of the unfinished
So far tonight we've talked about where motivation comes from, and how goals can point it somewhere. But there's a quieter force that shows up once you've actually started. It's the strange pull of something half done. Let's look at that pull, and at how a feeling of progress can carry you forward, or let you drift.

@char A19 cafe_sunset | A familiar feeling | The open loop
You've probably felt it. A half-read book on the bedside table. A puzzle with a few pieces missing. A message you started writing and never sent. Somehow, the unfinished things seem to sit in the corner of your mind, a little louder than the things you completed long ago.

@study A13 rooftop_dusk | Bluma Zeigarnik | Berlin, published in 1927 | Interrupted some small tasks and let others be finished, then asked which ones people remembered
In the 1920s, a young researcher called Bluma Zeigarnik was working in Berlin, in the lab of the psychologist Kurt Lewin. She gave volunteers a series of small tasks, like puzzles, counting problems and little handicrafts. Some tasks they were allowed to finish. Others she interrupted before they were done. Afterwards, she asked them to list the tasks they could remember.

@char A11 cafe_sunset | The result | Interrupted tasks stood out
In her experiments, people tended to recall more of the interrupted tasks than the completed ones. It was as if finishing a task let the mind close the file, while an interrupted task stayed open, still humming in the background. This became known as the Zeigarnik effect.

@char A04 attic_rain | A caution | Not so simple
But here's where we need to be careful. Over the following decades, many researchers tried to repeat this, and the results were mixed. Some found the effect, some found the opposite, and some found nothing at all. The memory part of the Zeigarnik effect turned out to depend a lot on the people, the tasks, and how the interruption felt.

@study A10 living_room_day | Maria Ovsiankina | Berlin, published in 1928 | Interrupted people during tasks and watched whether they went back to finish them on their own
A colleague in the same lab, Maria Ovsiankina, looked at the pull from a different angle. Instead of asking what people remembered, she watched what they did. When she interrupted people during a task, and then left them alone with it, many of them went back and finished it on their own, even though nobody asked them to.

@char A12 autumn_overlook | The urge to resume | Better supported
That urge to go back and complete something is sometimes called the Ovsiankina effect. And compared with the memory effect, it has held up rather better over the years. An unfinished task doesn't always stay in your memory. But it does seem to keep a gentle grip on your behaviour, inviting you back.

@char A15 cafe_sunset | A useful trick | Stop in the middle
There's a quiet lesson in that. Some writers like to stop working in the middle of a sentence, rather than at the end of a chapter. The next morning, the half-finished sentence is waiting for them, and it's easier to begin again. Whether or not the science is settled, it's a gentle way to borrow the pull of the unfinished.

@chapter A02 living_room_day | The small wins
Now, let's move from the lab to ordinary working life. What makes a person feel motivated on an ordinary Tuesday? Not in theory, but day after day?

@study A05 autumn_overlook | Teresa Amabile & Steven Kramer | The Progress Principle, 2011 | Collected nearly 12,000 daily diary entries from 238 people working on creative projects
Teresa Amabile and Steven Kramer asked 238 professionals, working in project teams across several companies, to fill in a short diary every working day. In total they collected close to twelve thousand entries. Each day, people described their mood, their motivation, and one thing that stood out about the day.

@char A20 street_day | What stood out | Making progress
When the researchers looked at the best days, the days when people felt most engaged and most positive, one thing appeared more than anything else. It was making progress in meaningful work. Not giant breakthroughs, often. Just a step forward. A problem solved. A piece that finally fit.

@char A01 cafe_sunset | Small steps | Small wins count
And the reverse was also true. The worst days were often the days of setbacks, when the work slipped backwards or got stuck. Amabile and Kramer called this the progress principle. Of all the things that can lift your inner working life, the feeling of moving forward on something that matters is one of the most powerful.

@char A04 bedroom_night | A surprise | Managers missed it
Here's the part that surprised them. When they asked managers what they thought motivates people most, and gave them a short list to rank, progress was ranked last. Recognition, incentives and clear goals all came out ahead. The thing that mattered most day to day was the thing most people overlooked.

@photo lf32_b09_01.jpg | A few pieces still to place
Think of a jigsaw puzzle on a table. Every piece that clicks into place makes the next one a little easier to find. And there's a small, quiet pleasure each time the picture grows. That's what progress feels like, from the inside.

@chapter A13 living_room_day | Done, or still to do?
So progress matters. But that raises a surprisingly subtle question. When you're working towards something, should you look at how far you've come, or at how far there is still to go?

@study A03 cafe_sunset | Minjung Koo & Ayelet Fishbach | Published in 2008 | Tested whether focusing on completed or remaining actions boosts motivation more
In 2008, Minjung Koo and Ayelet Fishbach looked at exactly this. In a series of studies, they nudged people to focus either on what they had already done towards a goal, or on what they still had left to do, and then measured how much effort they put in next.

@char A16 autumn_overlook | It depends | On commitment
The answer was, it depends. When people weren't yet sure how much they cared about a goal, looking back at what they had already done helped. It told them, this matters to me, I've already invested in it. But when people were already firmly committed, looking ahead at what was left gave them more of a push.

@char A12 cafe_sunset | A gentle rule | Look back, then look ahead
That suggests a rather kind rule of thumb. At the start of something new, when your commitment is still fragile, it can help to notice what you've already done. Later on, once you're sure you want it, looking at the distance still to go can sharpen your effort.

@chapter A10 cabin_fireplace | Stuck in the middle
There's one more pattern in progress, and once you notice it, you'll see it everywhere. It's what happens in the middle.

@study A19 rooftop_dusk | Andrea Bonezzi, Miguel Brendl & Matteo De Angelis | Published in Psychological Science, 2011 | Found that motivation tends to be high near the start and the end of a goal, and to sag in the middle
In 2011, Andrea Bonezzi, Miguel Brendl and Matteo De Angelis published a paper with a lovely title, Stuck in the Middle. Across several studies, they found that people's motivation was often highest near the beginning of a goal and near the end, and lowest somewhere in between.

@char A13 living_room_day | Why the middle | Two reference points
Their explanation was about reference points. At the start, you tend to measure yourself against where you began. Every step feels big, because you've gone from nothing to something. Near the end, you measure yourself against the finish line. Every step feels big again, because the gap is closing. But in the middle, both points are far away, and each step feels small.

@char A08 attic_rain | The long middle | Where things fade
That's why so many projects quietly stall halfway through. The first week of learning an instrument is exciting. The last week before a performance is exciting. But somewhere in the middle, the practice can start to feel like it's not counting for much. It isn't laziness. It's the way the distance looks from there.

@char A15 cafe_sunset | Shorter middles | Smaller goals
One gentle way around this is to make the middle shorter. Break a long goal into smaller ones, each with its own start and its own finish. Then you're rarely far from a beginning, and never far from an ending.

@list A03 living_room_day | The ninth stop | Unfinished tasks keep a gentle pull ;; Small daily progress lifts motivation ;; Look back early, look ahead later ;; The middle is where effort sags ;; Smaller goals shorten the middle
So that's this part of the night. Unfinished tasks keep a gentle pull on us. Small daily progress lifts motivation more than almost anything. Early on, look back at what you've done, and later, look ahead. And the middle is where effort sags, so smaller goals can shorten it.

@char A10 bedroom_night | The next question | Can you just push through?
But what about the moments when progress isn't enough, and you simply want something else right now? A sweet instead of a better sweet later. A rest instead of a task. That's the old question of self-control, and it starts with a small plate and a very patient child.
