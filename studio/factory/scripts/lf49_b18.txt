@chapter N10 cabin_fireplace | Thoughts nobody ordered
As Anton walks through the hallway on his way to turn off a light, something strange happens. A word pops into his head. Just that, a single word, which has nothing to do with anything. It's the name of a street he hasn't thought of in twenty years. He stands there, with his hand on the switch, a little startled.

@char N02 living_room_day | An odd experience | A thought from nowhere
Almost everyone has had moments like this. A song fragment arrives, a face from school, a word that seems to come out of the blue. We didn't choose it, we weren't looking for it, and we can't see what triggered it. It's a different kind of wandering from the kind we've been discussing, because there was no drifting at all. The thought simply arrived.

@char N19 cafe_sunset | A name for it | Mind-pops
In the early 2000s, the psychologists Lia Kvavilashvili and George Mandler gave these moments a name. They called them mind-pops, and they set out to study them. They were interested in the very simple, very brief intrusions of an unrelated word, name or tune, which seem to pop up from nowhere.

@study N07 autumn_overlook | Lia Kvavilashvili and George Mandler | Cognitive Psychology, 2004 | People kept records of involuntary semantic memories, such as words, names and tunes that came to mind unprompted
In a paper published in 2004 in the journal Cognitive Psychology, they described a series of studies in which people recorded these experiences, and tried to work out what had set them off. A great many of them, it turned out, did have a trigger, though the person often hadn't noticed it at the time. A smell, a word on a sign, a gesture, or even a word they'd read a few minutes earlier.

@char N23 rooftop_dusk | The surprising part | Hidden triggers
That's the interesting part. Something that feels like it came from nowhere may often have been set off by something small and easy to miss. The mind, it seems, is constantly connecting what's in front of it with what it already knows, and sometimes those connections surface without any warning. It's a little like a hidden spring, which releases when a faint breeze passes.

@char N10 living_room_day | A cousin of the mind-pop | Involuntary memories
A close relative of the mind-pop is the involuntary memory. This is a memory of a particular event in your life that arrives without any effort to recall it. You smell freshly cut grass, and you're suddenly eleven years old, in a school playground. You hear a particular laugh, and you're in your grandmother's kitchen.

@study N07 cafe_sunset | Dorthe Berntsen | Applied Cognitive Psychology, 1996 | Volunteers kept diaries of memories that came to mind spontaneously, with no effort to retrieve them
In 1996, the Danish psychologist Dorthe Berntsen published an influential diary study of these experiences in Applied Cognitive Psychology. She asked volunteers to record the memories that came to them involuntarily, and noted what had preceded them. She found that they were common, that they were often brought on by cues in the surroundings, and that they tended to be of specific, everyday moments.

@char N19 cabin_fireplace | What Berntsen noticed | Cues all around
Berntsen's later work, including a book on the subject in 2009, described how involuntary memories are often triggered by sensory cues, such as smells, sounds and places, and how they appear all through the day, often while we're busy with something else. They seem to be a normal part of how memory works, rather than an oddity.

@list N11 autumn_overlook | Uninvited thoughts are common | Mind-pops: a word, a name or a tune from nowhere ;; Involuntary memories: a scene from your own past ;; Passing images: a flash of something imagined ;; Passing worries: a what-if that arrives uninvited
Putting these together, there's a whole family of thoughts that nobody orders. Mind-pops, involuntary memories, passing images, and passing worries. They aren't the same, but they have a lot in common. They arrive without effort, and they leave without any.

@char N02 street_day | A more difficult kind | Unwanted thoughts
There's one more member of the family that deserves a gentle mention, because it can be distressing. Many people sometimes have unwanted thoughts that seem out of character, such as a sudden, strange image of something bad happening, or an odd urge that they'd never act on. If you've had that, you may have worried that it said something troubling about you.

@study N07 rooftop_dusk | Stanley Rachman and Padmal de Silva | Behaviour Research and Therapy, 1978 | A comparison of the intrusive thoughts reported by people without any psychiatric problem and by people with obsessional problems
Research has found that these experiences are, in fact, very common. In 1978, the psychologists Stanley Rachman and Padmal de Silva published a study in Behaviour Research and Therapy, comparing the intrusive thoughts of people with obsessional problems with those of people with no such problem. They found that most people without any problem reported such thoughts too, and that the content was surprisingly similar.

@stat N11 cafe_sunset | Unwanted thoughts are normal | More than 9 in 10 | people in a large international survey reported an unwanted intrusive thought in the previous few months
A much larger study, published in 2014 by Adam Radomsky and colleagues in the Journal of Obsessive-Compulsive and Related Disorders, surveyed hundreds of people in many countries. More than nine in ten reported at least one unwanted intrusive thought during the previous few months. What differed between people wasn't whether they had the thoughts, but how much they minded them, and what they made of them.

@char N10 cabin_fireplace | The key difference | What we make of it
That's the crucial point. A thought arriving is not the same as a wish, a plan or a sign of who you are. It's just a thought, one of many that pass through a busy mind. What seems to matter is the meaning we give it. A thought that's treated as a dangerous message tends to stick. A thought that's treated as mental weather tends to pass.

@char N19 living_room_day | A gentle caution | When to get help
If unwanted thoughts are frequent, distressing, or getting in the way of your life, that's a good reason to talk to a doctor or a therapist. There are effective forms of help, and nobody should have to manage that alone. But for most of us, most of the time, the occasional strange thought is simply part of having a mind.

@quote N27 rooftop_dusk | A thought is a visitor, not a verdict. | a way of putting the research
That's perhaps a helpful way of putting it. A thought is a visitor, not a verdict. It comes, it may linger, and it goes.
