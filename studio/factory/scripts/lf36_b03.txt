@chapter A18 cabin_fireplace | A body ready for action
At the beginning of the twentieth century, a physiologist at Harvard called Walter Cannon was fascinated by a simple question. What happens inside the body when an animal, or a person, suddenly feels a strong emotion? Not what they think about it. What the body itself does, in the heart and the blood and the muscles.

@study A13 living_room_day | Walter Cannon | Harvard physiologist, a book | published in 1915
In 1915, Cannon published a book with a wonderfully direct title. Bodily Changes in Pain, Hunger, Fear and Rage. In it, he described how strong emotions like fear and rage seemed to set off a whole chain of changes in the body, all happening at once, and all pointing in the same direction.

@list A12 cafe_sunset | What Cannon described | The heart beats faster ;; Blood shifts toward the muscles ;; Sugar is released into the blood ;; The body gets ready to move
The heart beats faster and harder. Blood is shifted toward the big muscles, the ones you'd need to run or to push. Sugar is released into the bloodstream, ready to be used as fuel. The whole body, in other words, gets ready to move, and to move quickly.

@char A10 bedroom_night | Fight or flight | A famous phrase
Cannon is the person most often linked to a famous phrase you've probably heard. Fight or flight. The idea is that when something threatens us, the body prepares for one of two very old responses. Either stand and fight, or get away as fast as you can. And for that, it needs energy, oxygen and strength, all at once.

@char A03 autumn_overlook | Fear and anger | Close cousins in the body
What's interesting is how similar fear and anger look from the inside, at least in this first rush. In both, the heart speeds up and the body gets ready for action. The difference lies more in where that energy is pointed. And that's something we'll come back to in a little while, because it turns out to be the key to what anger is actually for.

@char A19 attic_rain | Back on the bus | What Sam feels
Now let's go back to Sam. Remember what he noticed in those first seconds after reading the message. His face felt warm. His jaw tightened. His hands tensed around the phone. Perhaps his heart beat a little faster. None of that was a decision. It was simply his body doing what bodies have done for a very long time.

@char A13 cabin_fireplace | Built for a different world | Not for text messages
And here's a slightly funny thing about all of this. The fight or flight response was shaped for a world where threats were physical. A rival, a predator, a sudden danger. It wasn't built for a world of text messages. Sam's body is getting ready to fight or run. But the thing he's facing is three words on a small glowing screen.

@char A05 living_room_day | Energy with nowhere to go | A familiar feeling
So the energy arrives, and it has nowhere to go. Sam can't fight the message. He can't run away from it either. He's sitting on a bus. That's often what anger feels like in modern life. A body that's suddenly ready for action, in a situation where action doesn't really make sense. And so the energy finds the nearest outlet. In Sam's case, his thumbs.

@chapter A12 rooftop_dusk | Why it feels so fast
One of the strangest things about anger is how fast it feels. Sometimes it seems to arrive before we've even understood what happened. One second you're fine, the next you're hot and tense and ready to reply. So why is that? Why can the body seem to move faster than the mind?

@char A10 cafe_sunset | An early warning system | The brain's quick check
Part of the answer is that the brain has quick, rough ways of checking whether something matters, and slower, more careful ways of working out what it means. The quick check doesn't wait for the full story. It notices something that might be a threat, or an insult, or a problem, and it gets the body ready, just in case.

@char A18 living_room_day | Better safe than sorry | Most of the time
For most of human history, that was a very sensible arrangement. If you react a little too fast to something that turns out to be harmless, you've lost very little. If you react too slowly to something that really is dangerous, you might lose a great deal. So the body tends to get ready first, and ask questions afterwards.

@quote A13 cabin_fireplace | The body gets ready first, and asks questions afterwards. | A simple way to remember it
The body gets ready first, and asks questions afterwards. That's a simple way to remember this part of the story. And it explains a lot about why our first reaction to something isn't always our best one. The first reaction belongs to the body. The best one usually needs a little more time.

@char A19 bedroom_night | The good news | The rush doesn't last forever
And here's the good news hidden inside all of this. A rush is, by its nature, a rush. The racing heart and the hot face and the tight jaw are designed for short bursts of action. If nothing keeps feeding them, they tend to settle. Not instantly, and not always quickly. But they do settle.

@char A03 autumn_overlook | What keeps it going | The thoughts we add
What often keeps anger going is not the first rush, but what we add to it. The thoughts we keep replaying. The story we keep telling. The arguments we rehearse in our heads. We'll see later tonight that some of the things people do to get rid of anger actually keep feeding it. But for now, let's just notice that the body's part of anger is surprisingly short.

@list A15 cafe_sunset | Anger in the body | Fast, physical, automatic ;; Built for action ;; Not built for screens ;; It settles if not fed
So let's gather what we know about anger in the body. It's fast, physical and largely automatic. It's built to help us act. It wasn't really built for screens and messages. And if nothing keeps feeding it, it tends to settle on its own.

On the bus, Sam is still holding his phone. His thumb is still near that little arrow. His face is still warm. But we know now that what he's feeling right now is a body getting ready for action. It isn't a verdict on his brother, or a final judgement on the situation. It's just the first rush.

There's something quietly powerful about simply noticing this rush while it's happening. Not fighting it, not obeying it. Just noticing. My face is warm. My jaw is tight. My heart is going a little faster. It sounds almost too simple to matter. But later tonight, we'll meet some research suggesting that this kind of noticing really does change things.
