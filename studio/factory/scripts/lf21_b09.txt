@chapter A13 rooftop_dusk | The people who shape us
Think of someone you've spent a lot of time with. A close friend, a partner, a sibling, a flatmate. Have you ever caught yourself using one of their phrases? Laughing at things the way they laugh? Picking up their taste in music, or their way of making tea? Most of us have. We absorb the people around us, often without noticing.

@char A10 cabin_fireplace | A quiet influence | We become a little like them
It's a gentle, everyday kind of change, and it raises a big question. If we pick up small habits from the people we're close to, could we also pick up larger things? Moods, health habits, even parts of our personality? Researchers have looked at this from several angles.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Christakis & Fowler | New England Journal of Medicine, 2007 | The spread of obesity in a large social network over 32 years
In 2007, Nicholas Christakis and James Fowler published a study using records from the Framingham Heart Study, a long-running health study in a town in Massachusetts. They had information on more than twelve thousand people, collected between 1971 and 2003, including who was friends with whom, and how people's weight changed over the years.

@char A12 living_room_day | A surprising pattern | Change travelled along friendships
They reported that when a person became obese, their friends were noticeably more likely to become obese too, more so than neighbours who lived close by but weren't friends. In the years that followed, they published similar analyses suggesting that things like quitting smoking and even happiness seemed to travel along networks of friends.

@char A04 attic_rain | The critics | Is it contagion, or similarity?
These studies caused a sensation, and they also drew serious criticism. The core problem is that friends are often similar to begin with, and they share environments. In 2008, Ethan Cohen-Cole and Jason Fletcher used the same kind of method on traits that shouldn't spread between friends at all, like acne, headaches and height, and still found apparent contagion. That suggested the method might pick up shared circumstances, not just influence.

@char A13 cabin_fireplace | Holding both | Some influence, hard to measure
So it's wise to hold the bold claims lightly. Separating influence from similarity is genuinely hard. But few researchers doubt that the people around us matter in some way. The question is how much, and through what.

@chapter A12 cafe_sunset | Feeling more alike
One way to look more closely is to follow people as they get to know each other, and see whether they grow more alike over time.

@study A04 attic_rain | Anderson, Keltner & John | Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 2003 | Emotional convergence between people over time
In 2003, Cameron Anderson, Dacher Keltner and Oliver John followed pairs of dating partners and pairs of university roommates over several months. They measured how each person tended to respond emotionally to the same situations, and checked again later.

@char A03 living_room_day | Growing alike | Emotions converged
Over time, the pairs became more similar in their emotional responses. Their reactions converged. And the researchers found a hint about who tended to move more. In these pairs, the partner with less power in the relationship tended to change more towards the other person, than the other way round.

@char A10 attic_rain | A gentle warning | Choose your weather
There's something worth noticing in that. If we slowly take on the emotional weather of the people we spend time with, then choosing who we're close to is one of the quiet ways we choose who we become. Not by cutting people off, but perhaps by seeking out a little more time with those who bring out the version of us we like best.

@chapter A15 cabin_fireplace | The sculptor and the stone
And the closest relationships can do more than rub off on us. They can actively help us grow. Researchers have a lovely name for this.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Drigotas, Rusbult, Wieselquist & Whitton | Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 1999 | Close partner as sculptor of the ideal self: behavioral affirmation and the Michelangelo phenomenon
In 1999, Stephen Drigotas, Caryl Rusbult and their colleagues described what they called the Michelangelo phenomenon. The idea comes from the way Michelangelo described sculpture, as revealing a figure that was already hidden inside the stone. In a close relationship, they suggested, a partner can help reveal the person we're hoping to become.

@quote A13 rooftop_dusk | Every block of stone has a statue inside it, and it is the task of the sculptor to discover it. | Popular saying attributed to Michelangelo
That line is widely attributed to Michelangelo, though historians are not sure he ever said it in those words. Either way, the researchers found the idea useful. When a partner sees us as the person we want to be, and treats us that way, we tend to move towards that ideal.

@char A12 cafe_sunset | Being seen kindly | And becoming it
In their studies of couples, when one partner perceived and treated the other in line with that person's ideal self, the person tended to grow closer to that ideal over time. And those couples also tended to report feeling better about their relationships. Being seen in the light of who we hope to become seems to help us become it.

@char A10 cabin_fireplace | A gift you can give | See the best in someone
It's a gift that works in both directions. The people who love us can help sculpt us. And we can do the same for them, simply by noticing who they're trying to become, and treating them as if they're already on the way.

@chapter A13 rooftop_dusk | Love and steadiness
What about the very first serious relationships of our lives? Do they change our personalities?

@study A04 attic_rain | Neyer & Asendorpf | Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 2001 | Personality-relationship transaction in young adulthood
In 2001, Franz Neyer and Jens Asendorpf followed young adults in Germany over several years. They looked closely at what happened when people entered their first long-term romantic partnership. Compared with those who stayed single, people who began a first partnership tended to become less neurotic and less shy, and their self-esteem tended to rise.

@char A03 living_room_day | Roles shape us | The social investment idea
This fits a broader idea that researchers call the social investment principle. As we take on the roles of adult life, like a committed relationship, steady work or caring for others, those roles ask us to be reliable, calm and considerate. And over time, many of us grow into those expectations.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Bleidorn and colleagues | Psychological Science, 2013 | Personality maturation around the world: a cross-cultural examination of social-investment theory
In 2013, Wiebke Bleidorn and her colleagues compared personality data from young people in sixty-two countries. They found that personality maturation happened across cultures, and that in places where people tended to take on adult roles earlier, like starting work or forming families, the maturing of personality also seemed to happen earlier.

It's a reminder that change doesn't only happen inside our heads. It happens between people. In the friendships we keep, the partners we choose, the roles we take on and the way others see us. Each of these is a quiet sculptor, working on us a little every day.

@list A12 living_room_day | Using what we know | Spend time with people who bring out your best ;; Let someone you trust know who you're trying to become ;; See and treat others as the people they hope to be ;; New roles can grow you, give them time
So if you're hoping to change, it might help to let someone in on it. Tell a friend who you're trying to become. Spend a little more time with the people who bring out your best. And offer the same kindness back, by seeing the people you love in the light of who they're hoping to be.
