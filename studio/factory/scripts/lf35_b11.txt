@chapter A02 rooftop_dusk | Myth nine: happiness is a private project
There's a way of talking about happiness that has become very common. Work on yourself. Find your inner peace. Your happiness is your own responsibility, and nobody else can give it to you. And there's some truth in that, of course. But underneath it sits a picture of happiness as a private, inner project. Something you build alone, quietly, in your own head.

@char A13 living_room_day | Alone with the view | Nora's quiet flat
That's our ninth myth. And it's one that Nora, without ever quite deciding to, has been living by. Since the move, she has been busy. The new flat, the new routine, a longer journey to work. Her old friends live further away now. Most evenings, it's just her, the kettle, the window and the darkening sea. A lovely view, enjoyed entirely on her own.

@char A10 cabin_fireplace | A simple question | Who are the happiest people
So let's ask a simple question. If you took the very happiest people you could find, and looked closely at their lives, what would they have in common? Would it be their jobs, their health, their looks, the places they live? Or something else?

@study A18 cafe_sunset | Ed Diener and Martin Seligman | University of Illinois and the University of Pennsylvania | Comparing the happiest students with everyone else
In 2002, Ed Diener and Martin Seligman published a short paper in the journal Psychological Science, simply titled Very Happy People. They studied around two hundred and twenty university students, measuring their happiness carefully in several different ways, over time. Then they compared the happiest ten percent with the rest.

@list A12 living_room_day | The very happiest students | Spent the least time alone ;; Had strong friendships and relationships ;; Were rated as socially connected by others ;; Still had bad moods sometimes
What set the very happiest group apart was not something exotic. They spent the least time alone, and the most time socialising. They had strong relationships, both by their own account and in the eyes of the people who knew them. And they weren't happy all the time. They still had bad moods, now and then. They simply had a lot of good days, and people around them.

@quote A15 autumn_overlook | A necessary but not sufficient condition for high happiness. | Diener and Seligman, on social relationships, 2002
The authors were careful about how they described this. Good social relationships, they suggested, seemed to be necessary for very high happiness, because every one of the happiest people had them. But they weren't sufficient. Plenty of people with good friends were not among the very happiest. Relationships, in other words, looked like one essential ingredient, not the whole recipe.

@char A03 rooftop_dusk | A study of the commute | Strangers on a train
That study was about close relationships. But what about the people we don't know at all? The faces we pass every day without a word? Here's where it gets surprising. Because one of the most charming studies on this question took place not in a laboratory, but on the morning commute.

@study A20 street_day | Nicholas Epley and Juliana Schroeder | University of Chicago | Talking to a stranger on the train or bus
In 2014, Nicholas Epley and Juliana Schroeder published a study in the Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, with the wonderful title Mistakenly Seeking Solitude. They asked commuters on trains and buses in Chicago to do one of three things on their journey. Some were asked to strike up a conversation with a stranger near them. Some were asked to keep to themselves. And some were just asked to travel as they normally would.

@list A12 cafe_sunset | What happened | Talking to a stranger: the most pleasant ride ;; Keeping to yourself: the least pleasant ;; People predicted the opposite ;; Strangers turned out to be willing to talk
The people who talked to a stranger reported the most pleasant journey. The ones who kept to themselves reported the least pleasant. And here's the twist. When other commuters were asked beforehand to imagine these situations, they predicted the opposite. They expected the conversation to be awkward, and solitude to be nicer. They also thought strangers wouldn't want to talk. In practice, people were far more willing than expected.

@char A05 living_room_day | Another forecasting error | We get it wrong again
Notice how this connects to something we met earlier tonight. We are not very good at predicting what will make us happy. That was true of big life events, and it turns out to be true of something as small as a train ride. We guess that other people are a cost to be avoided, when quite often they are a small gift waiting to happen.

@photo lf35_b11_02.jpg | The seat beside you
Think of all the journeys you've made sitting beside someone, both of you looking at your phones or out of the window, neither of you saying a word. It feels like the polite, comfortable choice. And very often, that's exactly what it is. But the commuter study suggests that, at least some of the time, we may be quietly choosing the less pleasant option, simply because we've misjudged how a short conversation would feel, and how the other person would feel about it too.

@chapter A20 cafe_sunset | Weak ties
And the people who matter don't all have to be close friends. Psychologists sometimes talk about weak ties. The barista who knows your order. A neighbour you nod to on the stairs. A colleague from another floor you chat with at the coffee machine. People you know, but not well. It's easy to think of these people as background, the scenery of an ordinary day rather than part of what makes it good. They don't feel important enough to count. But researchers wondered whether that was really true, or whether, like the strangers on the train, these small contacts might matter more than we give them credit for.

@study A18 living_room_day | Gillian Sandstrom and Elizabeth Dunn | University of British Columbia | Counting everyday chats with acquaintances
In 2014, Gillian Sandstrom and Elizabeth Dunn published research in the journal Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin looking at exactly these people. In one of their studies, students kept track of their interactions over several days, counting how many conversations they had with close friends, and how many with weaker ties, people they knew only a little.

@char A12 rooftop_dusk | What they found | Small chats, real lift
And they found that on days when people had more interactions with their weaker ties, they tended to be happier, and to feel a greater sense of belonging. Not just from time with close friends, but from the light, passing contact with people on the edges of their lives. A few friendly words with someone you barely know still seemed to count.

Now come back to Nora. One weekend, an old friend comes to visit for the first time since the move. Nora makes two cups of tea and carries them through. And the friend walks into the room, sees the window, and simply stops. She actually gasps. Oh, look at that, she says. You can see the whole bay.

@char A19 cabin_fireplace | Seeing it fresh | Through someone else's eyes
And something happens to Nora in that moment. She looks at the window again, through her friend's eyes, and for a few seconds it's the first night all over again. The size of the sky. The shine on the water. The little boats. Nothing about the view had changed. But sharing it brought back what adaptation had quietly worn away.

@char A13 living_room_day | Why that works | A view seen by two
In a way, this is savouring again, from the last part of tonight, but shared. When someone else reacts to something we've stopped noticing, their fresh attention borrows ours for a moment. Their surprise reminds us that the thing in front of us is, in fact, remarkable. And afterwards, it often stays a little brighter.

@list A10 rooftop_dusk | Small ways in | Say hello to a neighbour ;; Chat for a minute with a barista ;; Invite someone to see what you love ;; Tell someone about a good moment
None of this requires becoming a different person. You don't have to love parties, or turn into the most outgoing person in every room. The research points to small things. Saying hello to a neighbour. A minute of chat with the person who serves your coffee. Inviting someone round to see something you love. Telling a friend about a good moment, rather than keeping it to yourself.

@char A02 cafe_sunset | The myth, corrected | Happiness is shared
So the ninth myth doesn't quite hold up. Happiness is partly a private thing, of course. But it is also, quite deeply, something that happens between people. And the next morning, Nora knocks on her neighbour's door to return a parcel that was left with her by mistake, and ends up chatting for ten minutes about the storm that's on its way. It's a small thing. It makes the whole morning feel lighter.

@char A15 autumn_overlook | Where we go next | The last myth
Which brings us to the last myth of tonight, and perhaps the most surprising one of all. Because there's one more thing that we tend to do with happiness, almost without thinking about it. We chase it. And the research suggests that the harder we chase it, the stranger things become.
