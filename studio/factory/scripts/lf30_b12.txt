@chapter A05 living_room_day | Seeing what you expect
Here is the belief we'll look at now, and it's a deep one. Seeing is believing. You look, the world shows itself, and only then do you form an opinion about it.

@char A10 cabin_fireplace | The reverse | Believing is seeing
But what if it also works the other way around? What if, at least a little, believing is seeing? What if the brain's expectations reach into perception itself and help to decide what you see?

@chapter A12 cafe_sunset | An idea from the 1800s
This isn't a new idea. One of the greatest scientists of the nineteenth century had already suggested it.

@study A03 living_room_day | Hermann von Helmholtz | Germany, 1867 | Proposed that perception works by unconscious inference: the brain draws quick conclusions from the senses
The German physicist and physician Hermann von Helmholtz completed his great Handbook of Physiological Optics in 1867. In it, he argued that what we see is the result of what he called unconscious inference. The eye delivers patterns of light, and the brain, without our noticing, draws conclusions about what in the world most likely caused them.

@char A19 cabin_fireplace | A detective in the dark | Clues, then conclusions
Helmholtz was saying that your brain is a bit like a detective who never leaves the office. It never sees the world directly. It only gets clues, and from those clues it reaches a verdict, and that verdict is what you experience as seeing. The amazing thing is how quickly and quietly it does all this.

@list A13 cafe_sunset | Unconscious inference, in short | The senses send clues ;; The brain uses past experience ;; It reaches a best guess ;; You experience the guess as reality
It took psychology a long time to be able to test this idea properly. But by the middle of the twentieth century, researchers had found clever ways to catch expectation in the act.

@chapter A18 autumn_overlook | A strange deck of cards
One of the best known tests used nothing more than a deck of playing cards.

#ai lf30_b12_01.jpg | Editorial still-life of a few vintage playing cards spread face up on a green felt table, one card slightly apart from the others, soft warm lamp light, calm curious mood, no people, no text, no logos

@photo lf30_b12_01.jpg | Playing cards, with a twist
Imagine someone shows you playing cards, one at a time, for just a fraction of a second each. Your job is simply to say what each card is. Easy enough. But a few of the cards in the deck have been quietly altered.

@study A03 cabin_fireplace | Jerome Bruner & Leo Postman | Journal of Personality, 1949 | People shown trick cards, such as a red six of spades, often misread them as normal cards
In 1949, the psychologists Jerome Bruner and Leo Postman, at Harvard, published a study called On the perception of incongruity. Most of the cards they flashed were normal, but some were trick cards with the colours swapped, for example a red six of spades, or a black four of hearts.

@list A12 living_room_day | What people said | A red six of spades seen as a six of hearts ;; Or as a normal black six of spades ;; Some saw purple or brownish cards ;; Some just felt something was wrong
At short exposures, people very often reported the trick cards as normal ones. A red six of spades would be seen as a six of hearts, or as an ordinary black six of spades. With longer looks, some people described compromises, a card that seemed purple or brown. And some just felt uneasy, sure that something was off, but unable to say what.

@char A19 attic_rain | The brain's choice | Normal wins
What's going on? Your brain knows from a lifetime of cards that spades are black and hearts are red. When it gets only a quick glimpse, it fills in the most likely answer. The expectation is so strong that it can overrule what is really printed on the card.

@char A10 living_room_day | Learning to see | It got easier
And there was a hopeful twist. Once people had spotted one trick card, they usually recognised the others much more quickly. Their expectations had been updated. The brain had learned that this deck was strange, and it began to look more carefully.

@study A18 cabin_fireplace | Jerome Bruner & A. Leigh Minturn | Journal of General Psychology, 1955 | The same broken shape was read as the letter B among letters, and as the number 13 among numbers
A few years later, in 1955, Bruner and A. Leigh Minturn published a simpler and rather lovely demonstration. They used one ambiguous shape, a little like a capital B with a small gap in it. Shown among letters, people tended to read it as a B. Shown among numbers, the very same shape was more often read as 13. The context told the brain what to expect, and the brain saw what fitted.

@list A12 living_room_day | Context at work every day | Messy handwriting ;; A half-heard word in a noisy room ;; A face in dim light ;; A sign seen from far away
You do this all day long without noticing. You read a friend's messy handwriting because you know what they're likely to write. You catch a word in a noisy café because it fits the sentence. Context fills the gaps, and it usually fills them well.

@chapter A15 cafe_sunset | The face that won't turn inside out
Some expectations, though, are so deep that even knowing the truth doesn't help.

@char A13 cabin_fireplace | A mask from behind | The hollow face
Picture an ordinary face mask, the kind you might wear at a party. Now turn it around, so you're looking at the inside, the hollow side. The nose should point away from you, sinking in. But when you look at it, especially from a little distance, you'll often see a normal face, with the nose pointing towards you.

#ai lf30_b12_02.jpg | Editorial still-life of a plain white blank theatre mask with no features painted, lying on a wooden shelf beside a small candle in a quiet room, soft warm side light and gentle shadows, calm mood, no people, no text, no logos

@photo lf30_b12_02.jpg | A plain mask in soft light
The British psychologist Richard Gregory spent years writing about this hollow-mask illusion. He used it as one of the clearest examples of perception as a kind of hypothesis, a best guess the brain makes about the world.

@quote A10 living_room_day | Perceptions are hypotheses. | An idea associated with Richard Gregory
Gregory's view was that our perceptions are like hypotheses in science. The brain proposes the most likely explanation of the evidence. And since we've seen many thousands of faces, all of them sticking outwards, a hollow face is so unlikely that the brain quietly rejects it. Even when you know it's hollow, you still see it pop out.

@char A19 cabin_fireplace | Knowing isn't enough | A deep rule
That's the remarkable part. You can know perfectly well what is true, and still be unable to see it. Some of the brain's expectations sit so deep that a passing thought can't reach them.

@chapter A12 rooftop_dusk | A picture made of spots
And now the other side of the story. Sometimes expectation doesn't hide the truth. Sometimes, it's the only thing that lets you see it at all.

@char A13 living_room_day | A famous photograph | Black and white spots
There's a famous black-and-white photograph, taken by the photographer R. C. James and often shown in books on perception. At first it looks like a random scatter of dark blotches on a white background. Many people stare at it for quite a while and see nothing at all.

@char A10 cabin_fireplace | Then, suddenly | A dog appears
Then someone says, there's a Dalmatian dog in it, sniffing the ground, under a tree. And suddenly, there it is. The head, the spotted body, the shadow of the tree. And here's the strange thing. Once you've seen it, you can't easily stop seeing it. The spots will never look random to you again.

@list A12 cafe_sunset | What changed | Not the picture ;; Not your eyes ;; Only your expectation ;; And that changed everything
Nothing about the picture changed. Your eyes received exactly the same light as before. What changed was a single piece of knowledge, a hint about what to look for. And that hint transformed what you saw.

@chapter A18 autumn_overlook | Believing is seeing, gently
So where does this leave the belief that seeing is believing?

@list A13 cafe_sunset | What expectation can do | Correct a missing clue ;; Hide something unusual ;; Make a face pop out ;; Reveal a hidden picture
It's still true that we use our eyes to learn about the world. But the brain's expectations are always involved. They can help us read a blurry sign, recognise a friend in the dark, or find a dog among spots. And now and then, they lead us to see a normal card where a strange one really was.

@char A15 living_room_day | Something kind | Patience with others
There's a gentle lesson here. When someone sees a situation differently from you, it may not be because they're careless or stubborn. Their expectations, built from their own lives, may really be shaping what they see. A little curiosity about that can go a long way.

@char A19 cabin_fireplace | One more mystery | The speed of time
And there's one more part of reality that seems to arrive ready-made, flowing at a steady pace, second after second. Time itself. Surely we just feel it pass. But people who've been through a frightening moment often say the world went into slow motion. Did it really? Some researchers decided to find out, in the most dramatic way possible.
