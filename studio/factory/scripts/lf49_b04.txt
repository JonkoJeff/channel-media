@chapter N10 cabin_fireplace | Zoning out
Anton picks up his book again. He reads one paragraph, and then, quite suddenly, he realizes that his eyes have been moving along the lines for a while with no meaning arriving at all.

@char N02 living_room_day | A strange kind of reading | Eyes moving, mind elsewhere
Most of us know this experience very well. You can read a whole page, or even several, and take in nothing. Your eyes move correctly, your lips might even move, and you could pass a quick glance as a perfectly good reader. But when you try to recall anything you've just read, there's a blank where the content should be.

@char N19 cafe_sunset | A name for it | Mindless reading
Researchers call this mindless reading, or simply zoning out. And because it's so easy to produce in a laboratory, it's become one of the favorite ways to study mind wandering. All you need is a long, dull text, a group of volunteers, and a way to ask them, every now and then, whether they were paying attention.

@study N07 autumn_overlook | Jonathan Schooler, Erik Reichle and Diane Halpern | Thinking and Seeing, 2004 | An early series of studies on zoning out during reading, in which people reported when their minds had drifted
An early and influential line of work on this came from Jonathan Schooler and colleagues in the early 2000s. In a chapter published in 2004, Schooler, Erik Reichle and Diane Halpern described several experiments in which volunteers read long texts and were interrupted to say whether they'd been zoning out. Many of them reported that they had, often without realizing it until they were asked.

@char N23 rooftop_dusk | The unsettling part | Without noticing
The part of that work that people find unsettling is that the lapses often went unnoticed. In some of the studies, a large share of the zoning out was caught only when the researchers asked about it, not when the readers noticed it themselves. The mind had been away for a while, and the reader had no awareness of it at all.

@char N10 living_room_day | The eyes tell a story | Eye movements while reading
Then came a clever idea. If the mind has left, perhaps the eyes would give it away. In 2010, Erik Reichle, Andrew Reineberg and Jonathan Schooler published a study in Psychological Science, in which they tracked the eye movements of people reading a text while the researchers waited for them to zone out.

@study N07 cafe_sunset | Erik Reichle, Andrew Reineberg and Jonathan Schooler | Psychological Science, 2010 | Eye movements were recorded while people read, and compared between moments of attentive reading and moments of reported zoning out
The result was clear. During periods of zoning out, the eyes behaved differently. Fixations on words were less sensitive to how common or rare a word was, which is something that attentive readers' eyes are very sensitive to. In other words, the eyes carried on moving, but they'd stopped responding to the content, like a person walking along a familiar path with their mind elsewhere.

@char N02 cabin_fireplace | What that means | A trace in the eyes
That's a helpful finding, because it shows that mind wandering isn't only a private inner event. It leaves a signature in the body. In principle, a computer watching your eyes could tell when you've stopped reading, even if you hadn't noticed yourself.

@char N09 living_room_day | Looking but not seeing | A deeper idea
A bigger idea sits behind this. Smallwood and Schooler, and others, have argued that when the mind wanders, attention turns away from the outside world and towards inner thoughts, in a process they call perceptual decoupling. The eyes still receive the page, but the brain is processing it less deeply. The connection between what's out there and what's in your head has loosened.

@study N07 rooftop_dusk | Jonathan Smallwood and colleagues | Journal of Cognitive Neuroscience, 2008 | Brain responses to events in the outside world were measured while people reported drifting off during a task
In 2008, Smallwood and his colleagues published a study with a wonderfully direct title, Going AWOL in the brain. They used recordings of the brain's electrical activity, and found that when people's minds wandered, the brain's response to events in the outside world was reduced. The signals still arrived, but the brain's analysis of them had been turned down.

@char N20 living_room_day | The picture | Volume turned down
A good way to picture it is a radio with the volume turned down. The station is still playing, and the signal is still coming through, but you're no longer listening. If something very loud happens, you'll still notice it. If something is ordinary, it passes by unregistered.

@char N10 autumn_overlook | Another window | The pupil
Other researchers found a second window. The pupil of the eye changes size with mental effort and arousal, and Smallwood and colleagues, in a 2011 study in PLoS ONE, reported that during periods of mind wandering, the pupil responded less to events in the outside world. And in a 2013 study, Michael Franklin and colleagues looked at pupil size during reading, and found changes before people reported that they'd drifted.

A page of printed lines, a little soft, as if seen through a slow blink. The letters are there, tidy and orderly, and yet there's a feeling of distance, as though the eyes are resting on the paper while the mind has gone to the window.

@char N02 cafe_sunset | What this tells us | A real change in the brain
All of this adds up to a consistent picture. Mind wandering is not just a feeling. When it happens, the eyes, the pupils and the electrical signals of the brain all change. It's a real state, in which the brain turns its attention inward and turns the outside world down. That's why a page can pass in front of you and leave nothing behind.

@char N19 living_room_day | A fair caution | Small samples, careful claims
A fair caution is worth adding. Many of these studies are fairly small, and most involve university students reading texts in a laboratory, which is not the same as a person reading a favorite novel on a sofa. The patterns are consistent, but they should be held a little lightly, as a good start rather than a final word.
