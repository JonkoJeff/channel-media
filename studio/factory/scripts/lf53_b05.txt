@chapter N20 cafe_sunset | Built to stick
The onion is chopped, the pan is warming, and Ruth is trying to remember what the song is actually called. She knows the chorus perfectly. She half knows the verse. But the title and the singer are just out of reach. She sings the hook quietly to Biscuit, who wags his tail, which doesn't help. And she wonders, as many of us have, whether some songs are simply built to get stuck.

@study N09 living_room_day | Kelly Jakubowski and colleagues | Psychology of Aesthetics, Creativity, and the Arts, 2016 | Dissecting an earworm
In 2016, a team led by Kelly Jakubowski, with Sebastian Finkel, Lauren Stewart and Daniel Müllensiefen, published a study called Dissecting an earworm in the journal Psychology of Aesthetics, Creativity, and the Arts. They wanted to know whether there's something in the music itself, in the shape of the melody, that makes certain songs more likely to become earworms than others.

@char N10 living_room_day | The method | Earworms and their twins
About three thousand people named the songs that most often got stuck in their heads, in data collected between 2010 and 2013. The researchers then did something clever. For each frequent earworm, they found a matching song that wasn't often named as an earworm, but had been similarly popular and similarly recent in the UK charts. Then they compared the melodies of the two groups.

@char N20 cafe_sunset | Why the twins matter | Not just popularity
The matching was the key step. Popular songs are heard more often, so of course they get stuck more often. By comparing each earworm with a song of similar chart success, the team could ask a sharper question: among songs that are equally popular, does the melody itself make a difference? The study focused on pop, rock, rap and R and B, the styles most often named.

@list N27 rooftop_dusk | What made a tune stickier | A faster tempo ;; A common overall melodic shape ;; Some unusual intervals or repeated notes
And the melody did make a difference. The earworm songs tended to have a faster tempo than their matched twins. Their melodies tended to follow a common overall shape, rising and then falling, the kind of contour found in many familiar tunes. And they tended to contain something slightly unusual too, such as unexpected leaps between notes, or repeated notes.

@char N09 living_room_day | Familiar, with a twist | The sweet spot
Put together, this suggests an interesting recipe. A sticky tune seems to have a shape that's easy for the mind to follow, because it's like so many melodies we already know, plus a little surprise that makes it stand out. Too predictable, and it might blend into everything else. Too strange, and it might be hard to remember. Somewhere in between, a chorus becomes very easy to replay.

@photo lf53_b05_01.jpg | Sheet music on a piano stand
Sheet music resting on a piano. The rising and falling shape the researchers found is one of the oldest patterns in melody. Many children's songs, hymns and folk tunes follow it. A melody that climbs to a high point and then comes back down is easy to sing, easy to remember, and, it seems, easy for the mind to keep playing on its own.

@list N10 cafe_sunset | Most named earworms in the study | Bad Romance, Lady Gaga ;; Can't Get You Out of My Head, Kylie Minogue ;; Don't Stop Believin', Journey ;; Somebody That I Used to Know, Gotye ;; Moves Like Jagger, Maroon 5
You may be curious which songs people named most often. Near the top of the list were Bad Romance by Lady Gaga, Can't Get You Out of My Head by Kylie Minogue, which is a fitting title, Don't Stop Believin' by Journey, Somebody That I Used to Know by Gotye, and Moves Like Jagger by Maroon 5. Other frequent names included Bohemian Rhapsody by Queen.

@char N20 living_room_day | Popularity still counts | Exposure matters too
The team also found that popularity mattered. Songs that had been higher in the charts, and more recent, were more likely to be named. So a sticky tune is partly about the melody and partly about how often we've heard it. That fits with what Beaman and Williams had concluded years earlier: that earworm potential seems to come from exposure combined with simplicity and repetitiveness.

@char N09 cabin_fireplace | Old songs too | Not only new hits
Ira Hyman's team at Western Washington University found something related in their experiments. They played people songs by the Beatles alongside more recent hits, and the Beatles songs were just as likely to become earworms as the modern ones. A well-built melody doesn't seem to lose its grip with age. If anything, a song we've known for decades has had a long time to settle into memory.

@char N07 cafe_sunset | Ruth's chorus | Does it fit the recipe?
Ruth hums the chorus again, listening to it this time. It's quick and bouncy. It rises up to a high note and comes back down. And there's one odd little jump in the middle, where the melody leaps somewhere she doesn't quite expect. Without knowing it, she has just checked her earworm against the findings of the study, and it fits rather well.

@list N10 living_room_day | Not a formula | Tendencies, not rules
It's important not to overstate this. The study found tendencies across thousands of songs, not a formula that guarantees an earworm. Plenty of fast songs with rising and falling melodies never get stuck in anyone's head, and some slow, strange tunes do. The features shift the odds a little; they don't decide the outcome.

@char N20 cafe_sunset | Different for everyone | Your own sticky songs
There's also a lot of individual variety. Beaman and Williams found little evidence that particular songs were especially prone to becoming earworms across people in general. A song that loops for one person may never bother another. Your own earworms depend on what you've heard, what you like, what you're doing and what you're feeling, as much as on the shape of the tune.

@char N09 living_room_day | The title arrives | Without trying
And then, as she stirs the pan, the title of the song suddenly comes to Ruth, all by itself, along with the name of the singer. She hadn't been searching for it for at least a minute. It's a small, familiar experience: the answer arrives when you stop looking. Memory often works like that, with searches carrying on quietly in the background while we're busy with something else.

@char N07 cabin_fireplace | Looking back | Built to stick
So, in this chapter, we've seen that Jakubowski and her colleagues, comparing earworms with equally popular songs, found that sticky tunes tend to be faster, to follow a common rising and falling shape, and to include a few unusual intervals or repeated notes. Popularity and exposure still matter, and every listener has their own sticky songs.
