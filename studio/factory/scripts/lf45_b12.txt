@char A15 street_day | Faces aren't as easy as they feel | A secret
Here's a secret about faces that surprises almost everyone. We feel as though we're brilliant at them. And with people we know, we are. But with people we don't know well, we're surprisingly poor. The skill that felt so effortless for Marta's neighbour, someone she's seen hundreds of times, turns out to be much weaker for faces she's seen only briefly.

@char A19 cabin_fireplace | Two different skills | Familiar and unfamiliar
Researchers now think of these as two quite different abilities. Recognising a familiar face is superb. You can do it from a blurry photo, from across a street, from the back of a head in a doorway. Matching or remembering an unfamiliar face is far harder. And the strange thing is that we don't notice the difference, because most of the faces we deal with day to day are familiar ones.

@study A10 living_room_day | Jenkins, White, Van Montfort & Burton, 2011 | Variability in photos of the same face | Viewers who did not know a person judged different photos of that person to be different people. Viewers who did know the person identified the same photos correctly.
Here's an experiment that makes it vivid. In 2011, Rob Jenkins and colleagues gave people a pile of photographs of the same person and asked them to sort the photos by identity. Viewers who knew the person sorted them easily. Viewers who didn't know the person saw many different people, even though every photo showed the same face. Within one person, the variation from photo to photo was large.

@quote A18 rooftop_dusk | For a stranger, one face can look like many people. | The key finding
The authors put it sharply. Within-person variability is often very large compared to the differences between people. A stranger's face, photographed in different light, from different angles, with a different expression, can look like several different faces. It takes familiarity to see through all that and know it's one person.

@study A03 attic_rain | Neil and colleagues, 2016 | Sorting photos of the same face | Adults and children sorted 40 photos of two unfamiliar men into piles by identity. Adults saw a median of 5 identities, an average of about 11. Only 2 of 15 adults found the correct answer, two.
A later team repeated it with 40 photographs of just two unfamiliar men. Fifteen adults sorted them into piles by identity. The correct answer, of course, was two piles. But on average, the adults saw about eleven different identities. The middle value was five. And only two of the fifteen adults found the right answer. For children, none did. Two men, forty photographs, and most people saw a crowd.

@stat A12 cafe_sunset | Neil and colleagues, 2016 | 2 of 15 | adults correctly saw just two people in 40 photos of two unfamiliar men
Take in that number. Two out of fifteen adults. Not because they weren't trying, and not because they were poor at faces in general. They were simply looking at unfamiliar people, and for unfamiliar people, our brains are far less good than we think. The skill we're so proud of belongs mostly to the faces we already know.

@char A12 street_day | A street full of blurred lights | Known faces stay sharp
Think of a busy street in the rain, with all its lights blurred and shimmering. Every light is a face you don't know, soft and indistinct. And then, among them, one steady light you do know, sharp and clear, however far away. That's how it feels to be a recognising brain. The known faces stay sharp, and the unknown ones swim.

@char A04 neon_alley_rain | What this means for Marta | Her neighbour is known
Which explains something about Marta's evening. Her neighbour's face is a very familiar one, seen over years, in many lights, from many angles. For a face like that, recognition is effortless, and that's what happened. She didn't have to think. It's a bit like a song you've heard a hundred times. The whole thing comes at once, with no effort.

@char A12 street_day | Misrecognition | The other side of the coin
There's a flip side to all this, and it brings us back to those diaries. Remember that in the 1985 diary study, more than 300 of the 900 or so slips were mistaken identities, people taking one person for another. In 272 cases, someone unfamiliar was mistaken for someone known. That's the same eagerness at work. A brain tuned to recognise well-known people will sometimes see one where there isn't one.

@char A10 living_room_day | A fast guesser | Generous, not perfect
So our face recognition is more like a fast, generous guesser than a precise instrument. It leans toward saying, I know you, because for most of human life, it was better to greet a stranger by mistake than to ignore a friend. Most of the time, it serves us wonderfully. Occasionally, it gives a cheerful wave to a stranger, and we remember it at two in the morning.

@char A19 cabin_fireplace | And names | A comparison
Compared with all that, think about what a name has to do. A name has no look. It doesn't change from photo to photo or from angle to angle. It stays the same wherever you meet the person. It's just a word. Yet we find it harder to retrieve. That's the odd symmetry that's been running through tonight, a complicated face that's easy, and a simple name that's hard.

@quote A13 rooftop_dusk | A face is a great deal of information. A name is almost none. | The odd symmetry
Perhaps that's the real answer. A face is a great deal of information, and the brain is built to handle a great deal of information. A name is almost none, and the brain isn't built to hold almost nothing. We're good at the rich thing and poor at the thin one.

@char A19 attic_rain | A practical consequence | Why strangers blur
There's a practical consequence to all this. If we're poor at matching unfamiliar faces, then relying on a quick look at a stranger's photograph is riskier than it feels. That's true for ordinary people, and, the researchers suggest, for people who check photographs for a living. It's one reason that researchers took such an interest in the few people who seem to be especially good at it, whom we'll meet shortly.

@char A12 cafe_sunset | The role of context | A face in the wrong place
Familiar faces are also affected by where we meet them. You might know a person perfectly well in one place, your dentist in her surgery, a colleague in the office, and fail to place them when they turn up somewhere else, say the supermarket. The face is there. The usual setting, the thing that helps with recognition, is missing. Most of us have experienced that small jolt, and it's yet another ordinary slip.

@char A04 bedroom_night | Marta and the context | The man at the party
Marta's neighbour, by contrast, was in a perfectly normal place. He's a man who lives two streets away, and who was standing in the street. The setting helped her recognise him. It's just that the setting helped with who he was, and had nothing to say about what he was called. Context is a hint for identity, not a hint for names.

@char A10 living_room_day | The upshot | Faces and names
So, to gather up this part of the story. Recognising a familiar face is quick and generous, and sometimes wrong. Recognising an unfamiliar face is hard, and much harder than we expect. And recalling a name is a slow, separate act, which can fail even when everything else has worked. Three different skills, all running together at a party, all of them being a little more fragile than they feel.

@char A12 autumn_overlook | A softer view | Not a competition
There's no need to see faces and names as competitors, one winning and one losing. They're two different kinds of knowledge, each with its own strengths. Faces are rich and wide, and brilliantly handled. Names are slender and arbitrary, and need their own kind of care. Understanding that is the first step to being kinder to ourselves about the second.
