@chapter A01 cafe_sunset | Rebuilding the story
Every one of us carries a story about our own life. It runs something like this: this is where I came from, this is what I've been through, and this is who I've become. Psychologists call it a narrative identity. When a shock comes, that story can be broken. Rebuilding it is part of recovery, and the way it's rebuilt matters more than most people think.

@char A04 attic_rain | The researcher | Dan McAdams
Dan McAdams, a psychologist at Northwestern University, has spent decades studying how people tell their life stories. His idea is that we don't just have a personality. We also have a story that we constantly edit, one that gives our lives unity and purpose. And the way we tell it, he finds, is linked to how well we are doing.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | McAdams | Review of General Psychology, 2001 | The psychology of life stories
McAdams reviewed research on how adults make sense of their lives through stories. He described how people tend to organise memories into a narrative with a beginning, a turning point and a direction. He also found that the themes in these stories, such as agency, meaning and relationships, were linked to wellbeing, and that people could revise the story over time, particularly at moments of change.

@list A03 living_room_day | What a life story gives us | A sense of unity across time ;; A sense of purpose ;; A way of explaining why things happened ;; A guide for what to do next
These are exactly the things that a shattered worldview takes away. After a shock, people often say that they no longer know who they are, or that their life feels cut in two, before and after. Rebuilding the story is a way of putting the pieces back into one line again.

@chapter A13 rooftop_dusk | Stories that heal
Some kinds of stories seem to work better than others. McAdams studied what he calls redemptive stories, in which a bad experience is followed by something good. These are very common in American culture, and people who tell them tend to report higher wellbeing. But the story was not the whole picture.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | McAdams, Reynolds, Lewis, Patten & Bowman | Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin, 2001 | When bad things turn good and good things turn bad: sequences of redemption and contamination in life narrative and their relation to psychosocial adaptation in midlife adults and in students
The researchers asked adults and students to describe key scenes in their lives. They found that people who told more redemption sequences, in which a negative event led to a positive outcome, tended to score higher on measures of wellbeing. People who told more contamination sequences, in which a good event was ruined by something bad, tended to score lower.

@char A12 living_room_day | The nuance | The story follows the life
It's important not to read too much into this. These studies can't tell us whether telling a redemptive story makes people happier, or whether happier people tell redemptive stories. And forcing a redemptive story can backfire, as we saw in the chapter on growth. The healthiest stories may simply be the honest ones, with room for both the loss and the life that went on.

@list A12 living_room_day | Features of a healthy story | It takes the loss seriously ;; It leaves room for change ;; It includes other people ;; It looks forward, not only back
Notice that a healthy story doesn't have to be a happy one. It has to be true, and it has to allow for a future. A story that says, my life was ruined and nothing can ever be good again, closes the door. A story that says, something terrible happened, and I'm finding a way to live with it, leaves it open.

@chapter A06 cafe_sunset | Telling it differently
Therapists use this idea in a method called narrative therapy, and in related approaches. They don't tell clients how to feel. They help them to notice the stories they tell about themselves, and to consider whether another telling is possible, a telling that is just as true but gives more room.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Neimeyer | Death Studies, 2006 | Re-storying loss: fostering growth in the posttraumatic narrative
Robert Neimeyer, a psychologist who studies grief, described how bereaved people can be helped to re-story their loss. He suggested that the aim is not to find a happy ending but to recover a thread, to place the loss in a larger story of the person's life and relationships, and to find a way of carrying the memory of the person who died forward.

@char A01 cafe_sunset | A hopeful idea | You can be the editor
This is a quietly powerful idea. You can't change what happened. But you have some say over how you tell it. The same events can be told as a story of defeat, or as a story of someone who went through something awful and kept going. Neither is false. One leaves more room to live.

@list A03 living_room_day | Words to keep from this chapter | Narrative identity ;; Redemption sequence ;; Contamination sequence ;; Re-storying
Four terms from this chapter. Narrative identity is the life story we carry about ourselves. A redemption sequence is a story in which a bad event leads to a good outcome. A contamination sequence is the reverse. And re-storying is revising the story to include a loss. Picture this scene. A man in his sixties describes the year his marriage ended. At first he calls it the worst year of his life, and says that everything he had built was gone. Asked to tell it again a few years later, he says it was the year he learned who his friends were, and the year he began painting again. The facts are the same. The telling has changed, and so has he. The researchers would say that he has re-storied the event, not by pretending it was good, but by giving it a place in a longer life.

@list A12 living_room_day | Questions to find your thread | What did I value before this happened? ;; What do I still value now? ;; Who helped me through it? ;; What would I want to tell someone who is going through it now?
Here are four questions that follow from this research, which you can think about or write down. What did I value before this happened? What do I still value now? Who helped me through it? And what would I want to tell someone who is going through the same thing today? Notice that none of them asks you to say that it was a good thing, or that you're glad. They ask only what remains, and what you'd pass on. For many people, the last question is the most powerful. When we imagine speaking to someone else in pain, we often find a kindness and a clarity that we can't find for ourselves, and that kindness is a good place from which to begin telling the story again.

A winding path through the hills. It doesn't run straight, and it doesn't have to. A good life story, like a good road, can take bends and long detours and still be a single road.

@char A15 cabin_fireplace | A gentle thought | Still the same author
After a shock, it can feel as though the person who wrote your story is gone. But the research suggests otherwise. You're still the author. The next chapter may be unlike the ones before it, and it may take a long time to begin. But the pen is in your hand.

@char A08 cabin_fireplace | Looking back | What the story taught us
So what did we learn? That we all carry a life story, and shocks can break it. That redemptive stories are linked to wellbeing, though they cannot be forced. That healthy stories take the loss seriously and leave room for the future. And that we have some say in how we tell it.
