The amygdala is one of the brain's most important lookouts for danger. One of the scientists who did most to show how it works is Joseph LeDoux, at New York University. Over many years, he studied how rats learn to fear a simple sound that has been paired with something unpleasant, and he traced the paths that the signal takes through the brain.

@study A01 cafe_sunset | Joseph LeDoux | New York University, 1996 | The Emotional Brain: a fast low road and a slower high road to fear
In 1996, LeDoux described this work in a book called The Emotional Brain. He found that information from the senses can reach the amygdala by two different routes. One is short and quick. The other is longer and slower, but much more detailed.

@list A12 living_room_day | Two roads to the amygdala | The low road: quick and rough ;; The high road: slow and careful
He called them the low road and the high road. On the low road, signals travel from a relay station deep in the brain, called the thalamus, straight to the amygdala. It's a rough sketch, but it's fast. On the high road, the same signals travel up through the outer layers of the brain, the cortex, where they're examined carefully, before the verdict is passed on.

Once the amygdala raises the alarm, it passes the message on, especially to a small region deep at the base of the brain, called the hypothalamus. The hypothalamus is a kind of control desk for the body. It helps manage hunger, thirst, temperature and sleep. And in an emergency, it switches on the body's accelerator.

That accelerator has a rather gentle sounding name. The sympathetic nervous system. It's a network of nerves running from the spinal cord out to almost every organ you have. Within seconds, it can speed up your heart, change your breathing, and tell the inner part of your adrenal glands to release adrenaline, the same chemical Cannon studied, along with its close cousin, noradrenaline.

And then everything happens at once. Your heart beats faster and harder. Your breathing quickens, and the airways in your lungs open wider. Your pupils widen to let in more light. Scientists have learned a great deal about the amygdala from a very small number of people with rare damage to it. One of the best known is a woman known in the research only by her initials, S M. A rare genetic condition had damaged both of her amygdalae.

In 2011, a team led by Justin Feinstein, working with Ralph Adolphs, Antonio Damasio and Daniel Tranel at the University of Iowa, published a study of S M. They took her to an exotic pet store, where she handled snakes and wanted to touch spiders. They took her to a haunted house attraction, where she laughed at the actors.
