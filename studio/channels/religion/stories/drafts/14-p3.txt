CHAPTER: Who Is the Greatest?
Luke records a moment at the supper that almost sounds out of place. Right after the bread and the cup, the disciples start arguing about which of them is the greatest. It is an awkward, very human scene. || Luke 22:24 || text-card
It is easy to imagine how it began. Seating at a banquet carried status. Who reclined nearest the host, who sat at the foot of the table, who was served first? Anyone who has attended a family gathering knows how quickly such things can matter. || Roman banquet couches, triclinium reconstruction || wikimedia
Jesus answers by contrasting two models of power. The kings of the Gentiles, he says, lord it over their subjects, and those in authority are called benefactors. But it must not be so among you. The greatest should become like the youngest, the leader like one who serves. || Luke 22:25–26 || text-card
Then he asks a simple question: who is greater, the one who reclines at the table or the one who serves? The one who reclines, surely. But I am among you as one who serves. In that single sentence, the table is turned upside down. || Luke 22:27 || text-card
This echoes the foot washing in John in a different register. Whether or not the two stories refer to the same moment, they preserve a shared memory: the teacher at this meal defined leadership by service. || The Washing of the Feet, Giotto, Scrovegni Chapel || wikimedia
Luke then adds a promise. Because the disciples have stayed with him in his trials, Jesus says he confers on them a kingdom, so that they may eat and drink at his table in his kingdom and sit on thrones judging the twelve tribes of Israel. || Luke 22:28–30 || text-card
The twelve tribes of Israel is an important phrase. The number twelve was not accidental: it reflects the tribes of Israel, and suggests that Jesus saw his circle as a sign of a renewed Israel. This is widely seen as a deeply Jewish hope. || Twelve tribes map, ancient Israel || map
E. P. Sanders, in his study of Jesus and Judaism, argued that the choice of twelve disciples was symbolic, pointing to a restoration of Israel. Many scholars share that view, though they differ on how Jesus understood his own role. || E. P. Sanders, Jesus and Judaism, 1985 || text-card
It is striking that this vision of a restored Israel is introduced at a meal. Throughout the Gospels, Jesus eats with many kinds of people, and meals become places where the kingdom is imagined as already present. || Jesus eating with sinners, Gospel scene, Byzantine mosaic || wikimedia
The earliest Christians continued that pattern. In Acts, they are described as breaking bread in their homes and sharing food with glad hearts. Meals were the glue of the community, and the Last Supper became the pattern for them. || Acts 2:42–47 || text-card
But the dispute about greatness is also a gentle warning. The very night Jesus speaks of service, his closest followers are still measuring themselves against each other. The Gospels do not hide this. They often show the disciples as slow to understand. || Mark 9:33–37 || text-card
That honesty about the disciples is one reason historians find the Gospels interesting. A movement that remembers its own founders arguing at the last supper is not polishing its image. It is remembering, imperfectly but vividly, what happened. || Gospel of Luke, Papyrus 75 || wikimedia
And so the scene moves from table etiquette to a more sombre note. Jesus has spoken about thrones, but he knows what the next hours will bring. He now turns to one disciple in particular, and warns him about what is coming. || Last Supper, Hans Holbein the Younger || wikimedia
CHAPTER: Peter's Warning
In Luke, Jesus turns to Simon and says that Satan has demanded to sift all of them like wheat, but that he has prayed for Simon that his faith may not fail. And when you have turned back, he says, strengthen your brothers. || Luke 22:31–32 || text-card
Peter responds with confidence. Lord, he says, I am ready to go with you to prison and to death. It is the kind of promise that is made sincerely, and which the next hours will test. || Luke 22:33 || text-card
Jesus replies that before the rooster crows that day, Peter will deny three times that he knows him. In Mark, the saying has a precise detail: before the rooster crows twice. In each version, the prediction is stark. || Mark 14:29–31 || text-card
In Matthew and Mark, this exchange happens on the way out, after the hymn. Jesus tells the disciples that they will all fall away, quoting the prophet Zechariah, I will strike the shepherd, and the sheep will be scattered. || Zechariah 13:7 || text-card
Peter insists that even if all fall away, he never will. Jesus tells him that this very night, before the rooster crows, he will deny him three times. And Peter answers that even if he must die with Jesus, he will never deny him. || Matthew 26:31–35 || text-card
Then Mark adds one more line: and all the others said the same. It is a quiet detail, and it matters. The promise of loyalty does not belong only to Peter. The whole group says it, and the whole group will fail it. || Mark 14:31 || text-card
John tells the story with a slightly different setting. After Judas leaves, Peter asks Jesus where he is going. Jesus says Peter cannot follow now, but will later. Peter asks why he cannot follow now, and says he will lay down his life. || John 13:36–38 || text-card
In all four Gospels, Jesus then predicts the denial. The agreement is remarkable, because the story is not flattering to Peter, who would become one of the most important leaders of the early church. His failure is part of the tradition from the start. || The Denial of Saint Peter, Rembrandt, Rijksmuseum || rijksmuseum
The denial itself will happen in the courtyard of the high priest that same night. We will leave it for the next story, but it casts its shadow over this one. The reader knows what will happen, long before Peter does. || Courtyard of the high priest, Church of St Peter in Gallicantu, Jerusalem || wikimedia
Notice that Luke adds the prayer for Simon. In his version, the prediction is not only a warning but a promise of return. Peter will fail, and then he will turn back, and when he does, he will help the others to stand. || Luke 22:32 || text-card
This is a note of mercy within the foretelling. The Gospels do not treat Peter's denial as the end of his story, any more than they treat the scattering of the others as final. The night has a bleak shape, and also a thread of restoration. || Peter's Tears, Georges de La Tour || wikimedia
Later traditions built many layers upon this. The rooster became a symbol in Christian art, and still crowns many church spires. A small, ordinary animal became a sign of the moment when promises meet reality. || Rooster weathervane, church spire, Germany || wikimedia
Here is something to keep in mind as we continue. The Last Supper is often remembered as a calm and solemn scene. But the Gospel texts show anxiety, argument, grief and fear running beneath it. It is a human supper, not a serene tableau. || Last Supper, Jacopo Tintoretto, 1594 || wikimedia
CHAPTER: Words at the Table in John
Now we turn to the longest part of the evening in the Gospel of John. After the foot washing and Judas' departure, chapters fourteen through seventeen present a series of farewell conversations that take up more space than the entire passion story in some other Gospels. || John 13:31–17:26 || text-card
John begins with a new commandment. Jesus says he gives them a new commandment, that they love one another as he has loved them, and that by this everyone will know they are his disciples. Love is the sign of belonging. || John 13:34–35 || text-card
Then comes a series of questions from disciples. Thomas asks how they can know the way. Jesus replies in a line that has been quoted for centuries: I am the way, the truth and the life. Philip asks to see the Father, and Jesus answers that to see him is to see the Father. || John 14:5–9 || text-card
These conversations show something about how John tells his story. In the Synoptic Gospels, Jesus teaches mostly in short sayings and parables. In John, he speaks in long, reflective discourses, using images like light, bread, vine and shepherd. || John 15:1–11 || text-card
The vine is a good example. In chapter fifteen, Jesus says I am the true vine and my Father is the vinedresser. Remain in me and I in you. The branches cannot bear fruit unless they stay connected. The image fits the closeness the farewell is meant to convey. || Vine and branches, Byzantine mosaic || wikimedia
He also promises the Paraclete. The Greek word means something like one called alongside, a helper, advocate or comforter. In John's farewell, this Spirit of truth will remain with the disciples after Jesus is gone. || John 14:15–17, 26 || text-card
The promise is meant to console. Jesus says he will not leave them as orphans. The disciples are anxious about being left alone, and the discourse answers that fear again and again. Peace I leave with you, he says, not as the world gives. || John 14:18, 27 || text-card
Scholars have long noticed the difference in style between these speeches and the sayings of Jesus in the Synoptics. Many conclude that John's author, drawing on tradition, shaped the discourses to express the community's understanding of Jesus. || Raymond E. Brown, The Community of the Beloved Disciple || text-card
That is a reasonable way to read them. The discourse is less a transcript of a particular evening than a meditation, formed in the life of a community that was trying to understand what Jesus' departure meant. Faith and history are interwoven. || Papyrus 66, John 14 || wikimedia
Chapter seventeen is different again. It is a prayer, often called the high priestly prayer, in which Jesus prays for himself, for his disciples and for those who will believe through their words. It ends with a prayer that they may all be one. || John 17:20–23 || text-card
That prayer for unity has echoed through Christian history, especially in movements seeking reconciliation between divided churches. Whatever one thinks of the question of historical detail, it has been a source of aspiration for many. || World Council of Churches assembly, Amsterdam 1948 || wikimedia
Yet we should be honest about a painful irony. The prayer for unity belongs to a tradition that has often been divided, and that has too often been divided over the very meal we are exploring. We will come to that. || Council of Trent, Titian || wikimedia
For now, picture the room as John describes it. A teacher speaking for a long time to a circle of friends who are tired, frightened and confused, offering them words to carry into the darkness. Then, at the end of chapter seventeen, the speaking stops. || Last Supper, Pietro Perugino, Convent of Fuligno || wikimedia
CHAPTER: A Hymn and a Garden
The Synoptic Gospels end the supper with a simple note. Mark says that after singing a hymn, they went out to the Mount of Olives. It is one of the quietest sentences in the whole passion narrative. || Mark 14:26 || text-card
What hymn did they sing? We are not told. But if the meal was connected with Passover, then the likely candidates are the Hallel Psalms, one hundred thirteen to one hundred eighteen, traditionally sung at the festival. || Psalm 118 Hebrew manuscript, Cairo Genizah || wikimedia
Psalm one hundred eighteen contains some striking lines: the stone that the builders rejected has become the cornerstone, and blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord. These lines appear elsewhere in the Gospels, applied to Jesus. || Psalm 118:22–26 || text-card
It is moving to imagine them singing such words on the way to a night of arrest. We cannot know whether this was literally the case, since Mark does not give the text. But it gives the exit from the room a sound: voices in the dark. || Hallel Psalms, Sephardic Haggadah || wikimedia
They leave the city by the eastern gate, cross the Kidron Valley, and climb toward the Mount of Olives. In John, they enter a garden across the Kidron. Today, a valley of tombs and old olive trees lies between the Temple Mount and that hill. || Kidron Valley and Mount of Olives, Jerusalem || wikimedia
Mark and Matthew call the place Gethsemane, a name that probably derives from a Hebrew or Aramaic phrase meaning oil press. It fits the location, since olive groves covered the slopes, and presses would have been used for the harvest. || Garden of Gethsemane olive trees || wikimedia
In the garden, the mood changes. Mark says Jesus took Peter, James and John with him, and began to be distressed and troubled. My soul is deeply sorrowful, even to death, he tells them. Stay here and keep watch. || Mark 14:32–34 || text-card
He goes a little farther, falls to the ground, and prays that if it were possible the hour might pass from him. Abba, Father, he says, all things are possible for you. Remove this cup from me. Yet not what I want, but what you want. || Mark 14:35–36 || text-card
Notice the word cup returns. The same word that described the wine at the supper now describes the suffering to come. It is a literary thread, tying the table to the garden and making the two scenes into one long night. || Christ in the Garden of Gethsemane, Mantegna, National Gallery || wikimedia
Three times he returns and finds the disciples sleeping. The Gospels do not soften this. The friends who promised to die with him cannot stay awake. Mark has Jesus say the spirit is willing, but the flesh is weak. || Mark 14:37–42 || text-card
Luke adds that an angel appeared and that Jesus' sweat became like great drops of blood. But these verses are missing from some early manuscripts, and scholars debate whether they belong to the original text. || Luke 22:43–44 || text-card
Again, we see the discipline of textual criticism: not to remove difficult details, but to ask honestly where they came from. The Gospels reached us through human hands, and part of understanding them is understanding that history. || Codex Vaticanus, Luke 22 || wikimedia
In every version, the garden is a place of fear and prayer, and the table has led to it. The supper that began with bread and wine now moves to olive trees in the dark. Soon, lanterns and torches will appear across the valley. || Mount of Olives at night, Jerusalem || wikimedia
We will leave the arrest for another story. In this one, the doors of the upper room have closed behind us, and what remains is the long afterlife of a meal. How did a supper in Jerusalem become a ritual in thousands of cities? || Via Dolorosa, Old City Jerusalem || wikimedia
