@char A04 attic_rain | A promise | Evidence, not sermons
Let me say what this isn't. It isn't a lecture on how you ought to behave, and it isn't a claim that people are secretly good or secretly bad. Psychologists who study helping, which they call prosocial behaviour, have found something more interesting than either: whether we help depends heavily on small features of the situation, and those features can be understood, and even changed.

@chapter A12 living_room_day | The puzzle of helping
Start with a puzzle that bothered scientists for a long time. If natural selection rewards individuals who look after themselves, why would anyone ever help a stranger? Evolutionary biologists have offered several answers. We help relatives, because they share our genes. We help those who may help us back. And we help to build a reputation as a good partner or friend.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Trivers | Quarterly Review of Biology, 1971 | The evolution of reciprocal altruism
Robert Trivers, then at Harvard, proposed that helping can evolve between unrelated individuals if the help is likely to be returned later. A favour done today may be repaid tomorrow, so long as the participants can recognise one another and punish those who take without giving. The idea explained a great deal about cooperation, and it still shapes the field.

@char A06 cafe_sunset | The problem | Strangers don't fit
But reciprocity doesn't explain everything. People also help strangers they'll never meet again, in places where no one is watching and no reputation is at stake. That is what makes the topic so interesting for psychologists. They don't ask only whether people help, but when, and what happens in the moment of decision.

@list A03 living_room_day | Questions this video asks | What makes a person step forward? ;; What makes a person hold back? ;; Which of these are traits, and which are situations? ;; Can helping be taught?
That third question is the hinge of the whole field. For decades, the popular assumption was that some people are helpers and some are not. The experiments we'll look at suggest something less comfortable and more hopeful: that the same person can be either, depending on the circumstances.

@chapter A13 rooftop_dusk | The scene that started it all
In March 1964, a young woman named Kitty Genovese was attacked and killed outside her apartment building in Queens, New York. A newspaper story a fortnight later claimed that thirty-eight witnesses had watched and done nothing. The story shocked the country, and it launched an entire field of research.

@char A12 living_room_day | Two psychologists | Darley and Latané
Two social psychologists, John Darley at New York University and Bibb Latané at Columbia, were sitting together when they began to talk about the case. They did not accept the popular explanation, that New Yorkers were callous. They wondered instead whether something about the situation itself, the presence of many onlookers, made help less likely.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Darley & Latané | Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 1968 | Bystander intervention in emergencies: diffusion of responsibility
They designed an experiment in which students, seated alone in small rooms, talked over an intercom about their problems. One of the voices was an actor who appeared to have a seizure. Some students believed they were the only listener. Others believed that there were one or more other listeners too.

@stat A14 conspiracy_board | Students who reported the seizure before it ended | 85% vs 31% | when alone, versus believing four other people were listening
The results were striking. When students believed they were the only one who could hear, eighty-five percent reported the emergency before it ended. When they believed four others were listening as well, only thirty-one percent did. The more people who might help, the less likely each individual was to act.

@list A03 living_room_day | Words to keep from this chapter | Prosocial behaviour ;; Reciprocal altruism ;; Bystander effect ;; Diffusion of responsibility
Four terms to carry forward. Prosocial behaviour is any act intended to benefit others. Reciprocal altruism is helping with an expectation of return. The bystander effect is the tendency for individuals to help less when others are present. And diffusion of responsibility is the feeling that someone else will deal with it.

@list A12 living_room_day | A thought experiment | Think of the last time you walked past someone who might need help ;; What did you assume about the others nearby? ;; What would have changed your mind? ;; What would you do differently now?
Here is a small thought experiment to carry through the video. Think of a moment when you walked past someone who might have needed help. Perhaps a person who looked lost, or an elderly man struggling with a bag. Ask yourself what you assumed about the other people around, and what would have tipped you into stopping. Picture this scene as you listen to the next chapters, and see whether the research gives you a new way to read it. Most people find that the decision wasn't about being good or bad. It was about a quick, half-conscious reading of the situation, and that reading is something we can learn to notice.

@list A12 living_room_day | Pause and reflect | What do you already believe about helping? ;; When did a stranger last help you? ;; When did you last help one? ;; What stopped you, or started you?
Before we go on, here is a pause. Take a moment to think about what you already believe about helping. Most of us carry a theory, even if we have never said it aloud. Perhaps you believe that good people always help, and that people who don't are selfish. Or perhaps you believe that it depends on the day. Think about the last time a stranger helped you. It might have been small, like directions or a held door. Then think about the last time you helped a stranger. What started you, and what, on other days, has stopped you? Hold on to those memories as we go. Many of the studies we will meet look strange at first, with their staged seizures and smoke-filled rooms. But each one turns out to describe something you have felt. The aim is not to give you a new set of rules, but to give names to experiences you already know, and to show where the choice really happens.

An empty bench under a street lamp. The scene of the helping experiments was always an ordinary one: a corridor, a pavement, a waiting room. That ordinariness is the point. Helping, or failing to help, happens in places exactly like this one. If you take one idea from this first chapter, let it be that helping is not a mystery reserved for saints. If you've ever felt a pang of guilt about a time you didn't help, take comfort. The research suggests that the reasons were mostly situational, and that the guilt itself is a sign of caring. The aim of this video is not to blame, but to understand. Take that with you into tomorrow, and let it be enough for now. You do not have to change everything at once. One small, noticed moment of kindness is a perfectly good place to begin, and it is more than most of us manage on a busy day.
