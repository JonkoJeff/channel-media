@chapter A10 autumn_overlook | The long view
For the last of our research chapters, let's climb up to a high place and look out over the whole landscape of a life. Not the next ten days, or the next ten years, but all of it. Childhood, the busy middle years, and old age. What happens to people, on average, as they move through the decades?

@char A13 rooftop_dusk | A common fear | Getting older, getting worse?
Many of us carry a quiet fear about getting older. We imagine that life gradually becomes smaller and sadder, that our best days are behind us, and that old age is mostly about loss. Some of that is real, of course. Bodies change, and people we love are lost. But when researchers actually measured how people feel day to day, they found something unexpected.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Carstensen and colleagues | Psychology and Aging, 2011 | Emotional experience improves with age: evidence based on over 10 years of experience sampling
In 2011, Laura Carstensen and her colleagues at Stanford published results from a study that followed adults of many ages for more than ten years. At several points, participants carried a pager for a week and reported how they were feeling at random moments throughout the day.

@char A12 cafe_sunset | The finding | Emotional life improved
On average, emotional well-being improved with age, at least into the later decades of life. Older adults reported a more positive balance of emotions, and their emotional lives were steadier, with fewer swings into strong negative feelings. Not everyone, and not forever, but the overall direction was surprisingly positive.

@list A10 living_room_day | Socioemotional selectivity theory | Time feels open: gather new people and new information ;; Time feels limited: focus on what is meaningful now
Carstensen has an explanation she calls socioemotional selectivity theory. When we're young, time feels almost endless, so we tend to gather new experiences, new people and new information for an unknown future. As we get older and sense that time is more limited, our priorities shift. We focus more on what feels meaningful right now, especially close relationships.

@char A03 cabin_fireplace | Fewer, closer | Choosing what matters
In practice, this often means that older people have smaller social circles, but closer ones. They let go of relationships that drain them and invest more in the ones that matter. Carstensen's research suggests this shift isn't really about age itself. It's about how much time people feel they have left. When younger people imagine their time is limited, their priorities shift in a similar way.

@char A13 rooftop_dusk | A quiet lesson | We don't have to wait
There's a quiet lesson there for all of us, whatever our age. We don't have to wait until we're old to choose what matters. We can borrow a little of that wisdom now. Spend less time on what drains us. More time with the people and things that make life feel worth living.

@chapter A15 cabin_fireplace | One of the longest studies
And speaking of what matters, there's one study that has followed people for longer than almost any other in history.

@study A04 attic_rain | Harvard Study of Adult Development | Started in 1938 | Followed two groups of men for most of their lives, later including their families
It began in 1938, when researchers at Harvard started following a group of young men who were students at the university. A few years later, a second group was added, boys and young men growing up in some of the poorest neighbourhoods of Boston. Over the decades, the researchers interviewed them, examined their health, and later included their wives and children.

@char A12 living_room_day | Lives unfolding | Decades of change
Over those decades, the men's lives went in every direction. Some became successful, some struggled. Some found lasting love, some went through divorces. Some developed problems with alcohol, and some recovered. The study's long-time director, the psychiatrist George Vaillant, wrote about how much many of the men changed, sometimes in ways nobody could have predicted from their early lives.

@char A10 attic_rain | What mattered most | Warm relationships
So what did the researchers find mattered most for a good, healthy life? The current director, Robert Waldinger, summed it up in a talk in 2015. Of everything they had measured, the clearest message was about relationships. People who were more connected to family, friends and community tended to be happier and healthier, and to live longer.

@quote A13 rooftop_dusk | Good relationships keep us happier and healthier. Period. | Robert Waldinger, TED talk, 2015
Waldinger also described a striking pattern. It wasn't how high the men's cholesterol was in middle age that best predicted how they would age. In their analyses, how satisfied people were in their relationships at age fifty was a better predictor of their health at eighty.

@char A03 cafe_sunset | Careful with causes | A long study, not a lab experiment
Of course, this is an observational study, not an experiment. Healthier people might find it easier to keep good relationships, and other factors play a part. The original groups were also all men, mostly from one country and one era. But the consistency of the finding, over so many decades, makes it hard to ignore.

@char A12 living_room_day | Never too late | Change at any age
And one of the most hopeful things about the study is how often it showed people changing late. Men who had difficult starts sometimes found warmth and meaning in their forties, fifties or later. The story of a life wasn't written in its first chapters.

@chapter A13 rooftop_dusk | Stages of a life
The psychologist Erik Erikson thought about this kind of lifelong development back in the middle of the twentieth century.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Erik Erikson | Childhood and Society, 1950 | Described development as a series of challenges that continue across the whole life span
In his 1950 book Childhood and Society, Erikson described human development as a series of stages, each with its own central challenge, stretching from infancy all the way into old age. Unlike many thinkers before him, he insisted that development doesn't stop when childhood ends.

@list A12 cafe_sunset | Some of Erikson's later life challenges | Young adulthood: intimacy, or isolation ;; Middle adulthood: generativity, or stagnation ;; Late adulthood: integrity, or despair
In young adulthood, Erikson thought, the challenge is intimacy, forming close and lasting bonds. In middle adulthood, it's what he called generativity, caring for the next generation and contributing something that will outlast you. And in late life, it's integrity, looking back over your life and accepting it as your own.

@char A10 cabin_fireplace | Not a strict ladder | A useful map
Modern researchers don't treat Erikson's stages as a strict ladder that everyone climbs in order. But his central idea has lasted. We keep developing, and each part of life brings its own questions to grow through.

@photo lf21_b10_01.jpg | Seasons of a life
Perhaps that's the most hopeful thing we've found tonight. The person you are now is not the final draft. Personality shifts. Emotions often grow steadier. Relationships deepen. New chapters keep beginning, right up until the very end.

The small, repeated steps that reshape the brain and build new habits. The power of identity, of fresh starts, of kindness after a slip. The people who sculpt us.
