@chapter A19 living_room_day | The youngest helpers
Is helping something we learn, or something we are born ready to do? Parents might say both. A toddler who hands you a dropped spoon is doing something remarkable, and psychologists have asked exactly how early it starts, and whether it is a purely human trait.

@char A04 attic_rain | The researchers | Warneken and Tomasello
Felix Warneken and Michael Tomasello, then at the Max Planck Institute for Evolutionary Anthropology in Leipzig, designed a series of simple tests for very young children. In each, an adult tried to do something and ran into a small problem, such as being unable to reach an object or open a cupboard because their hands were full. The question was whether the toddler would notice, and help, without being asked.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Warneken & Tomasello | Science, 2006 | Altruistic helping in human infants and young chimpanzees
Eighteen-month-old children watched an adult drop a marker on the floor and fail to reach it, or struggle to open a cabinet while carrying a stack of books. Most of the children helped, handing the object back or opening the door, in several of the tasks. They did so without being asked or rewarded, and often within a few seconds.

@list A03 living_room_day | What the tasks involved | Out-of-reach objects ;; A stack of books and a closed cabinet ;; Objects that fell and could not be retrieved ;; Control conditions in which the adult did not need help
The control conditions were important. In those versions, the adult threw the object on purpose, or did not need the cabinet opened. The children mostly did not help then. This suggested that they were responding to the adult's actual goal and need, and not just handing things over because it was fun.

@char A12 living_room_day | The comparison | Chimpanzees too
The researchers also tested young chimpanzees in comparable tasks. The chimpanzees helped in one of the tasks, retrieving an out-of-reach object for the human, but not in others, such as those that involved understanding a more complicated goal. The finding suggested that the roots of helping may be older than our species.

@chapter A13 rooftop_dusk | Do rewards help?
If you wanted to encourage a young child to be helpful, you might offer a reward. A sticker, a sweet, a word of praise. But psychologists have asked whether rewards might backfire, by turning a kindness into a transaction.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Warneken & Tomasello | Developmental Psychology, 2008 | Extrinsic rewards undermine altruistic tendencies in 20-month-olds
In this study, toddlers were helped in a series of tasks. For some, a material reward, a toy, was given for helping on early trials. Later, when the reward stopped, those children helped less often than children who had never been rewarded. The researchers concluded that a tangible reward, in this setting, undermined the children's own motivation to help.

@char A04 attic_rain | The caution | One study, one setting
It's worth keeping this in proportion. This is a single set of experiments with small groups of toddlers, in a laboratory. It does not show that every reward or any praise reduces helping. But it raises a thought-provoking possibility, one that other psychologists have explored in other settings: that kindness can be crowded out by a prize.

@list A12 living_room_day | What parents and carers can take from this | Thank and notice, rather than bribe ;; Let children see the effect of their help ;; Invite help in small, real tasks ;; Model helping yourself
These are practical points rather than rules. Psychologists generally suggest that warm acknowledgement of a child's help, like saying thank you and showing that it made a difference, supports the sense that helping is valuable in itself.

@chapter A06 cafe_sunset | Even earlier
There's evidence that preferences for kind behaviour appear before children can even speak. In a famous set of experiments, babies watched a simple puppet show on a small stage.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Hamlin, Wynn & Bloom | Nature, 2007 | Social evaluation by preverbal infants
Kiley Hamlin, Karen Wynn and Paul Bloom showed babies, aged six and ten months, a shape trying to climb a hill. One shape helped it up, and another pushed it down. Afterwards, the babies were offered the two shapes to reach for, and most chose the helper. The researchers suggested that even very young infants may evaluate others on the basis of how they treat other individuals.

@list A03 living_room_day | The puppet show findings in short | Babies watched a shape struggle up a hill ;; Another shape helped it, a third hindered it ;; Most babies preferred the helper ;; The effect was found at six and ten months
This research has been influential, and it has been debated. Some labs have found it hard to replicate parts of it, and others have argued that babies may be responding to simpler features of the display. As is often the case, the more careful claim is that infants seem to show early sensitivity to helping and hindering, and that the exact reasons are still being studied.

@char A01 cafe_sunset | A balanced conclusion | Born ready, shaped by life
Taken together, the studies suggest that humans arrive with a head start toward cooperation, which families and cultures can then strengthen or weaken. It is neither a blank slate nor a fixed instinct. It's a tendency that responds to how the world treats us.

@list A12 living_room_day | Words to keep from this chapter | Spontaneous helping ;; Extrinsic reward ;; Intrinsic motivation ;; Preverbal infants
Four terms from this chapter. Spontaneous helping is helping without being asked. An extrinsic reward is a prize from outside. Intrinsic motivation is wanting to do something for its own sake. And preverbal infants are babies who cannot yet speak.

@list A03 living_room_day | Small ways to nurture helpfulness | Invite a child to help with a real task ;; Say what difference their help made ;; Let them see you help others ;; Be patient when their help is clumsy
Here are some small ways to nurture helpfulness at any age. Invite someone, young or old, to help with a real task. Say what difference their help made. Let them see you helping others. And be patient when the help is clumsy. Picture this scene: a three-year-old tries to carry a bowl to the table, spilling half of the soup. The adult says, thank you, now we have soup on the table and the floor, and hands over a cloth. The child wipes the floor, pleased. The task took longer, and the lesson was worth it.

@list A12 living_room_day | Pause and reflect | How did the adults around me respond when I helped as a child? ;; What do I do when a child helps me? ;; Where do I use rewards instead of thanks? ;; What small task could I invite someone to share?
A pause to think about how helping was received when you were small. When you tried to help, perhaps by carrying something, laying a table or comforting a sibling, how did the adults respond? Were you thanked, corrected, ignored, or paid? Those early reactions may have taught you something about what helping is for. If there are children in your life, you might notice how you respond to their small offers. Do you take the spoon, say thank you, and let the moment matter? Or do you say, I'll do it, it's quicker? Quickness is tempting, but each time we decline a clumsy offer, we may be teaching that help isn't wanted. And think about adults, too. Where do you use incentives and rewards where a simple thank you and an invitation might do the work? What small task could you invite someone to share this week?

@photo lf27_b06_01.jpg | A stool by the counter
A small stool by a kitchen counter, and a child-sized apron on a hook. The invitations to help that matter most are often ordinary ones, repeated every day, in rooms exactly like this. When you watch a toddler help, you are watching a very old thing. It has been shaped by evolution and by every family that welcomed it, and it can be nurtured by something as small as saying thank you.

@char A15 cabin_fireplace | A gentle thought | Helping starts small
If you want to understand where kindness begins, watch a toddler hand you a spoon. The research suggests that this tiny act is the beginning of everything we have been discussing, and that it deserves to be welcomed. If there are children around you, let them help in their clumsy way, and let it take longer. The time you spend is an investment in a person who will, one day, turn to someone else and ask whether they need a hand.

@char A08 cabin_fireplace | Looking back | What the youngest helpers taught us
So what did we learn? That toddlers help without being asked, from about eighteen months. That young chimpanzees help in simple tasks. That rewards may reduce later helping in toddlers. And that even babies seem to prefer helpers, though that finding is still debated.
