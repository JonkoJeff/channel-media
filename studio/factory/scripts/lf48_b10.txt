Ines has noticed something about her song. It isn't the instruments that give her chills, it's the voices. The moment when the choir's high part enters, a single voice rising above the others, is the exact point where her arms prickle.

@char N02 living_room_day | A theory about calls | Jaak Panksepp
In 1995, the neuroscientist Jaak Panksepp proposed an idea about why a voice can give us chills. He had spent years studying the emotional systems of animals, and he'd noticed that young mammals, from puppies to chicks, make loud distress calls when separated from their mothers. Those calls trigger a strong response in the parent.

@study N07 street_day | Jaak Panksepp | Music Perception, 1995 | Suggested chills may come from an old social alarm linked to separation calls
Panksepp suggested that musical chills may be a distant echo of that response. A high, sustained, emotional voice, rising in pitch, resembles the kind of call that says come here, or I am lost. The chill, on this view, is part of an ancient social system, one that makes us pay attention to each other and move closer.

@char N18 cafe_sunset | A hypothesis, not a fact | Appealing but unproven
Let's be careful here. This is a hypothesis, and a hard one to test. Nobody can go back and watch how it evolved. But it fits a number of facts. Chills often come with a solo voice or instrument entering, with a rising line, and with a feeling that has something to do with longing or togetherness.

@char N10 attic_rain | Music and the voice | Shared cues
There's firmer evidence for a related idea. In 2003, Patrik Juslin and Petri Laukka, two Swedish researchers, reviewed more than a hundred studies of how emotion is conveyed in speech and in music. They found that the two use remarkably similar cues. A sad voice is slow, soft and low. A happy voice is faster, brighter and louder. And music uses the same cues.

@stat N11 cabin_fireplace | Juslin and Laukka, 2003 | 100+ | studies compared in their review of emotion in voice and music
That suggests the brain may use the same equipment to read emotion in a voice and in a melody. When a violin sounds like a sob, it's partly because it borrows the shape of a human sob. Instruments, in a sense, imitate the sounds that people make when they feel.

Picture an old stone church with a choir. Dozens of human voices, each a little different, blending into one sound. The brain hears that as a crowd of people, all feeling the same thing together. It's hard to think of a sound that carries more social information in fewer seconds.

@char N20 bedroom_night | Many routes to one feeling | Juslin's list
Juslin went on to argue that music produces emotion through several different routes, and that no single theory explains everything. In a paper written with Daniel Västfjäll in 2008, and developed in later work, he listed a set of mechanisms. A quick reflex to a sudden sound. A learned link between a song and an event. Catching the feeling of a performer. Pictures in the mind. Memories. Expectation. Rhythm. And a judgment of beauty.

@char N23 autumn_overlook | A reflex from the brainstem | The sudden sound
The first of those is the simplest. Very loud, sudden or dissonant sounds trigger a fast reflex in the brainstem, one that exists to warn us of possible danger. In music, the same reflex can be triggered safely, such as by a sudden crash of drums, and may be part of why a dramatic change in volume often gives people chills.

@char N02 living_room_day | Ines's song | Several routes at once
Look at Ines's song again. A rising key, which taps expectation. A solo voice entering, which may carry social meaning. A swell in volume, which engages the startle reflex in a gentle way. And a tune she's known since she was a teenager, which brings memory. It's no wonder that the moment feels so strong. The song takes several routes at once.

@char N19 rooftop_dusk | Babies and lullabies | The first music
Babies seem to respond to music long before they understand words. Mothers in many cultures sing in a soft, slow, high voice to their infants, and infants appear to calm to it. If voice-like sounds are one of the first musical experiences, it makes sense that they can carry such strong feeling when we're older.

@char N10 bedroom_night | A quiet thought | Something in the throat
Many people describe a lump in the throat during these moments. That's a tightening of the muscles that control the voice, a kind of readiness to cry or to sing. It's the body joining in, in a small way, with the voice it's hearing.
