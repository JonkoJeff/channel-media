It's now a week since Nora's sleepless night. The thing that started it, a stressful deadline at work, has long since passed. Her project is done, and her client is happy. And yet, every night, she lies down at ten, and finds herself wide awake at midnight. The cause went away. So why didn't the problem?

@chapter N27 rooftop_dusk | How a bad night becomes a habit
Sleep researchers have a lovely way of explaining this, and it comes from a model that is more than thirty years old.

@char N19 living_room_day | The three Ps | A model from 1987
In 1987, the psychologist Arthur Spielman proposed a model of insomnia that is still widely taught. He described three kinds of factors. Predisposing factors make you more likely to have trouble, such as a sensitive, easily aroused nervous system. Precipitating factors are the triggers, such as stress, illness or a change in life. And perpetuating factors are what keep it going after the trigger has gone.

@char N09 cafe_sunset | The perpetuating part | The habits we build
Perpetuating factors are usually our own sensible reactions. After a bad night, we go to bed earlier to catch up, we lie in bed for longer, we nap, we drink more coffee, and we spend more time worrying about sleep. Each of these is understandable, and each can make the next night a little worse. The trigger fades, but the habits remain.

@char N02 living_room_day | The bed as a cue | Learning the wrong lesson
If you spend many nights lying in bed awake, frustrated and worried, the brain may learn an association. Bed becomes a place for tossing, planning and fretting, not for sleeping. It's a form of conditioning, much like Pavlov's dogs learning that a bell predicts food, only here the bed predicts wakefulness.

@char N13 attic_rain | A clever idea | Retrain the association
In 1972, the American psychologist Richard Bootzin proposed a simple treatment based on exactly this idea. If the bed has become a cue for being awake, then the aim is to make it a cue for sleep again. He called it stimulus control, and it's built from a handful of gentle rules that are still in use today.

@list N11 cafe_sunset | The core ideas of stimulus control | Go to bed when sleepy ;; Use the bed only for sleep ;; Leave the bed if awake for long ;; Return when sleepy ;; Keep a steady wake-up time
Go to bed only when you feel sleepy, not just tired. Use the bed for sleep, and not for working, scrolling or worrying. If you've been awake for what feels like twenty minutes or so, get up, go to another room, and do something quiet in dim light until you feel sleepy again. Then go back. And whatever sort of night you've had, get up at the same time each morning.

@char N10 living_room_day | Why it makes sense | Rebuilding a link
The rules sound strict, and some people find them hard at first. But the logic is gentle. Every time you leave the bed when you can't sleep, and return only when you feel sleepy, you weaken the link between bed and wakefulness, and strengthen the link between bed and sleep. It's not a trick, it's learning.

@chapter N20 cabin_fireplace | What actually works
When people ask what the research says about treating insomnia, the answer is, perhaps surprisingly, not a pill. It's a package of these ideas, called cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia, or CBT-I.

@char N19 autumn_overlook | A package | More than one technique
CBT-I usually combines stimulus control with a few other pieces. One is sleep restriction, where a person temporarily limits their time in bed to roughly the amount they actually sleep, and then gradually extends it as sleep becomes more consolidated. Another is cognitive work on unhelpful beliefs about sleep, for example, the belief that a single bad night will ruin everything. A third is relaxation or related skills.

@char N09 rooftop_dusk | A caution | Not a do-it-yourself crash course
It's worth being careful here. Sleep restriction in particular can cause daytime sleepiness, and it's not suitable for everyone, for example people with certain conditions such as bipolar disorder or epilepsy, or those who drive for work. It's usually done with guidance from a trained clinician, or a well-designed programme. The ideas are public, but the fine print matters.

@study N07 cabin_fireplace | Alexander Trauer and colleagues | Annals of Internal Medicine, 2015 | A meta-analysis of randomised trials of cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia, mostly in adults without other major health conditions
In 2015, Alexander Trauer and his colleagues combined the results of twenty trials of CBT-I in a meta-analysis published in the Annals of Internal Medicine. They found that it reduced the time it took to fall asleep by roughly twenty minutes on average, and reduced the time awake during the night by about the same, with benefits that lasted after treatment ended.

@char N02 living_room_day | The official view | A first-line recommendation
Largely on the strength of this kind of evidence, the American College of Physicians recommended in 2016 that all adults with chronic insomnia should receive CBT-I as the first treatment. Other guidelines in Europe and elsewhere say much the same. Sleeping pills have a place for some people, usually for short periods, but they're not the first thing the evidence points to.

@char N19 cafe_sunset | Even online | Programmes that scale
There's good news for people without easy access to a therapist. In 2019, Colin Espie and his colleagues reported in JAMA Psychiatry a large trial in which more than 1,700 people with insomnia used a digital CBT-I programme online. Compared with people who received their usual care, they slept better, and their mental well-being improved as well.

@char N09 living_room_day | A diary | Seeing the pattern
Most programmes begin with something unglamorous, a sleep diary. For a week or two, you note roughly when you went to bed, how long it took to fall asleep, how often you woke, and when you got up. It sounds like homework, but it replaces a vague, frightening impression with a real picture, and many people discover that their nights are less bad, or more patterned, than they believed.

@char N13 attic_rain | Beliefs under the microscope | Is it really a disaster?
Then there's the cognitive part, which is about gently examining beliefs. Will one bad night really ruin tomorrow? Do I truly need eight hours? What actually happened the last time I slept poorly? Often the answers are kinder than the first fearful guess, and people notice that the feared catastrophe has quietly failed to arrive many times before.

@char N02 cabin_fireplace | A word of patience | It can feel worse first
Programmes like this ask for patience. Sleep restriction in particular can leave people feeling more tired for the first week or two, before sleep consolidates and improves. That's expected, and it's one reason why it helps to have support. The aim is to build a stronger, deeper sleep drive, and that takes a little time to arrive.

@char N19 autumn_overlook | Not the same as hygiene | Why advice often isn't enough
You may have seen long lists of sleep hygiene tips, such as keeping the room cool, avoiding screens and cutting caffeine. They're sensible, and they match much of what we've covered. But reviews have found that, on their own, they're less effective for established insomnia than the full programme. They're like keeping the garden tidy, while CBT-I is a way of changing the soil.

@char N10 rooftop_dusk | A note on pills | A short bridge, perhaps
As for medication, doctors do sometimes prescribe sleeping tablets, usually for a short time, and for some people they are a valuable bridge through a bad patch. But they carry risks, such as next-day drowsiness, dependence and tolerance, and when they're stopped the old problem can return. That is why guidelines place them second. Anyone considering them should talk it through with a doctor.

@char N10 bedroom_night | Back to Nora | Good news and a gentle plan
For Nora, whose bad patch has long outlived its cause, all of this is good news. It means her problem is no longer a mystery of the night, but a set of habits and links that can be gently undone. She can start tomorrow, not with a pill, but with a notebook, a steady wake-up time, and the willingness to leave the bed for a while when sleep won't come.
