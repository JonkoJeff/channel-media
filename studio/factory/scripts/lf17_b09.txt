@chapter A12 living_room_day | The forgetting curve
In the 1880s, in Berlin, a young scholar named Hermann Ebbinghaus did something that sounds almost absurd. He decided to study his own memory, with no equipment but a notebook, a metronome and a long list of nonsense words. He made himself the only participant, and he kept going for years.

@char A01 cafe_sunset | The problem | Memory seemed too slippery
At the time, many people thought that memory was too private and too slippery to measure. You can't see it, and everyone's is different. Ebbinghaus disagreed. He believed that, with enough care, memory could be studied as precisely as any physical process, and that its rules could be written as numbers.

@char A04 attic_rain | The trick | Nonsense syllables
His key idea was to remove meaning. If he used real words, his results would depend on what the words meant to him. So he invented about two thousand three hundred nonsense syllables, such as ZOF, KEB and DAX. Each had a consonant, a vowel and a consonant, and none of them meant anything at all.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Hermann Ebbinghaus | University of Berlin, 1885 | Self-experiments on memorising nonsense syllables
He would learn a list of syllables, read in a steady rhythm, until he could recite it without a mistake. Then he would wait for a while, and try to learn the list again. The question was how much easier the second learning would be. The saving in time or repetitions was his measure of how much he'd remembered.

@char A10 attic_rain | The hard part | Years of boredom
It's worth pausing on how tedious this was. For years, Ebbinghaus recited thousands of nonsense syllables, noted his results, and repeated the process with different delays. He did this alone, with no assistants and no funding to speak of, and he published the results in 1885 in a book called On Memory.

@stat A14 conspiracy_board | His method | Savings | the time saved when relearning a list
He called his measure savings. If it took him a thousand seconds to learn a list the first time, and only six hundred to learn it again after a day, the saving was forty percent. It was a clever way to detect a memory that could no longer be recalled, but which had left something behind.

@chapter A13 rooftop_dusk | What he found
And what he found has been quoted in textbooks ever since. Forgetting is fastest right after learning, and then slows down. In his data, a large part of what he'd learned was lost within the first hour, and by the end of the first day, much more had gone. After that, the curve flattened, and the last traces faded very slowly.

@bars A05 autumn_overlook | Approximate savings after a delay | % of the effort remembered | After 20 minutes:58 ;; After 1 hour:44 ;; After 1 day:34 ;; After 6 days:25 ;; After 31 days:21
These are the figures from his published tables, rounded, and they've been reproduced in textbooks for well over a century. The shape is what matters. A steep drop at the start, then a long, gentle tail. It's called the forgetting curve, and it's one of the oldest results in experimental psychology.

@char A04 attic_rain | The second discovery | Repetition rewrites the curve
Ebbinghaus didn't stop at forgetting. He also showed that the more times you review something, the more slowly it fades. Each repetition flattens the curve, so that the next time, you lose less in the same period. Going over material once, and then never again, is a recipe for losing most of it.

@char A12 living_room_day | The spacing effect | Little and often beats a lot at once
He also noticed that spreading practice over several days worked better than cramming everything into a single session. This is now known as the spacing effect, and it's one of the most reliable findings in the whole of learning research. Hundreds of later studies have found the same thing in schoolchildren, students and adults.

@list A03 living_room_day | What to do with this | Review soon after learning ;; Space your reviews out over days and weeks ;; Test yourself instead of rereading ;; Sleep between sessions
That list is a simple recipe, and it comes from over a century of research. Reading a page three times in a row feels productive, but it's far weaker than reading it once, waiting, and then trying to recall it from memory. Struggling a little is part of what makes it stick.

@chapter A10 attic_rain | Does it still hold?
So did the old results survive? In 2015, two psychologists in the Netherlands, Jaap Murre and Joeri Dros at the University of Amsterdam, did something remarkable. Murre spent about seventy hours over a number of months learning lists of nonsense syllables, just as Ebbinghaus had, and measuring his own forgetting.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Murre & Dros | PLOS ONE, 2015 | A replication of Ebbinghaus's forgetting curve
Their results had a very similar shape to the original. A fast drop, a slowing tail, and even a small bump at the one-day mark that might be linked to sleep. The details differed a little, but the general pattern from 1885 had been reproduced in a modern laboratory, which is rare for a result that old.

@char A06 cafe_sunset | A careful reading | Real life is different
It's worth adding some caution. Nonsense syllables are a special case. Real memories, such as those for faces, songs or meaningful facts, can decay more slowly, especially if they connect to something you already know. The curve is a useful picture, but not a precise law for every memory.

@char A04 attic_rain | A caution | Don't oversell it
And some popular versions of the curve online, with neat percentages for every day, are more confident than the evidence allows. The sensible reading is the simple one: memory fades quickly at first, and the best way to slow that is to practise retrieval at spread-out intervals.

@char A12 living_room_day | Testing helps | The retrieval effect
There's one more finding worth adding to Ebbinghaus's. In 2006, Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke at Washington University showed that students who practised recalling a text did better a week later than those who simply reread it, even though the rereaders felt more confident. Testing yourself is a way of learning, not only a way of measuring.

@stat A06 cafe_sunset | Recalled a week later | More | by students who practised retrieval than by those who only reread
Their paper, published in Psychological Science, has been cited thousands of times. The practical point is that the feeling of fluency, of everything seeming familiar as you read it, can be misleading. It's not the same as being able to produce the answer yourself when it's needed.

@list A03 living_room_day | A simple study routine | Read once, then close the page ;; Write down what you remember ;; Check what you missed ;; Come back to it tomorrow, then next week
It takes a little more effort than rereading, and it can feel clumsy at first. But that clumsiness is a sign that the work is happening. Ebbinghaus, with his nonsense syllables, would probably have approved.

It's an unassuming scene. A notebook, a metronome, a cup of tea. And yet, from this kind of desk came one of the most durable curves in psychology. It's a good reminder that you don't need a large laboratory to learn something true, only patience and honest records.

@char A01 cafe_sunset | An everyday echo | The name you forgot
Think of a name you heard at a party and had forgotten by the time you went home. That's the forgetting curve in action. And think of the name you've heard five times over a month, and can say without effort. That's the spacing effect. Both are Ebbinghaus, in daily life.

@char A15 cabin_fireplace | A gentle thought | Forgetting is normal
It's also worth saying that forgetting isn't a failure of character. It's how a healthy memory works, clearing away what isn't needed. The trick is not to remember everything, but to help the things that matter survive. A bit of review, a bit of sleep, and a bit of patience go a long way.

@char A08 cabin_fireplace | Looking back | What the syllables taught us
So what did Ebbinghaus leave us? A proof that memory could be measured, a curve that has held for over a century, and a practical recipe: review soon, space it out, and test yourself. And he did it alone, with nonsense and a notebook, which might be the best part of the story.

@char A12 living_room_day | Sleep and memory | Why the night helps
Research since Ebbinghaus has added another piece to the picture. Sleep seems to help stabilise new memories. In many studies, people who slept after learning remembered more than those who stayed awake for the same period. During sleep, the brain appears to replay and reorganise what it has taken in, strengthening what matters and letting go of the rest.

@char A15 cabin_fireplace | A kinder way to study | Little and often
That suggests a kinder way to study than an all-night session. A short review in the evening, a good night's sleep and a brief test in the morning is likely to do more than hours of last-minute reading. It's less dramatic, and it feels slower, but it works with the way memory is built instead of against it. Ebbinghaus found the curve, and sleep helps to flatten it.
