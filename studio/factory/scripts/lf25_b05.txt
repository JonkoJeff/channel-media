@chapter A13 rooftop_dusk | The power of a question
Think about a conversation that you remember with pleasure. Chances are that the other person was curious about you. They asked, and they wanted to hear the answer. It turns out that this feeling can be measured, and that one kind of question in particular does most of the work.

@char A12 living_room_day | The puzzle | Why do questions work?
It might seem obvious that asking questions makes you likable. But it isn't, because many people believe that they should talk about themselves to impress others. Psychologists at Harvard Business School wanted to know whether asking is really a better strategy, and which kinds of questions matter.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Huang, Yeomans, Brooks, Minson & Gino | Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 2017 | It doesn't hurt to ask: question-asking increases liking
Karen Huang and her colleagues ran several experiments, including a study of real conversations between strangers and a speed-dating event. They found that people who asked more questions were liked more by their partners, and this was driven mostly by follow-up questions, which build on what the other person has just said. In the speed-dating study, people who asked more follow-up questions were more likely to get a second date.

@char A04 attic_rain | The crucial detail | It's the follow-up
Not all questions are equal. The researchers distinguished several types. Introductory questions open a conversation. Mirror questions return the same question. Full-switch questions change the subject completely. And follow-up questions invite the other person to say more about what they just said. Only the last kind showed a strong link with liking.

@list A03 living_room_day | Four types of question | Introductory: how was your week? ;; Mirror: and how about yours? ;; Full-switch: so, what are you watching? ;; Follow-up: you said it was tough, what happened?
Notice how the follow-up does something the others don't. It proves that you were listening. Anyone can ask a question from a list. A follow-up can only come from a person who heard the last thing that was said.

@chapter A12 living_room_day | Do questions help in hard talks?
These studies were about getting to know people, but the same group asked whether questions also help in tense conversations. They found that follow-up questions reduced the sense of conflict and increased the sense of being understood, as long as they were genuine. A question that is secretly an accusation, or a trap, didn't help, and people could tell.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Yeomans, Brooks, Huang, Minson & Gino | Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin, 2019 | It helps to ask: the cumulative benefits of asking follow-up questions
Michael Yeomans and colleagues showed in further experiments that people who asked more follow-up questions were rated as more responsive, and gained more information from the conversation. People tended to underestimate the benefit of asking, which may explain why we don't do it as often as we should.

@char A01 cafe_sunset | A surprising habit | We don't ask enough
If questions are so useful, why don't we ask more? Part of the answer is that we worry about being intrusive, or about seeming ignorant. Another part is the pull of our own story. In a difficult conversation, we're often busy preparing our next point, and a question feels like a delay.

@list A12 living_room_day | Genuine follow-up questions | What was that like for you? ;; What did you mean by that? ;; What would you want to happen? ;; Can you tell me more about that?
These are simple and open. They invite more than a yes or no. And they can be used even when you strongly disagree, because they ask for understanding, not for agreement.

@chapter A06 cafe_sunset | Going deeper
There's another encouraging finding. Many of us keep conversations on a surface level with people we don't know well, because we expect deeper talk to be awkward. But Amit Kumar, Michael Kardas and Nicholas Epley found in a series of experiments that this expectation is mistaken.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Kardas, Kumar & Epley | Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 2022 | Overly shallow? Miscalibrated expectations create a barrier to deeper conversation
Pairs of strangers were asked to discuss either shallow or deep topics, such as a recent dream or a time they'd felt grateful to someone. Before they started, people expected deep conversations to be more awkward and less enjoyable than shallow ones. Afterwards, they reported the opposite: the deeper conversations felt closer, more enjoyable, and no more awkward than expected.

@char A04 attic_rain | The lesson | Depth is easier than we fear
This is a hopeful result for anyone who dreads a serious talk. People who start with a personal, honest question, and who answer one in kind, usually end up feeling closer than they predicted. The anticipated awkwardness is greater than the real thing, and that holds across many experiments.

@list A03 living_room_day | Words to keep from this chapter | Follow-up question ;; Responsiveness ;; Shallow conversation ;; Self-disclosure
Four terms from this chapter. A follow-up question builds on what the other person just said. Responsiveness is showing that you've heard. A shallow conversation stays on safe topics. And self-disclosure is sharing something personal, which tends to invite the same in return.

@list A12 living_room_day | Try this | In your next conversation, ask three follow-up questions ;; Notice how the other person's answers change ;; Share one personal detail in return ;; Notice how it feels afterwards
Here is a small experiment. In your next conversation, with anyone, try to ask three follow-up questions, each of them starting from something they've just said. Notice what happens to the answers. They tend to become longer, warmer and more specific. Then share a personal detail of your own, and notice the response. Most people find that conversations they'd expected to be routine become surprisingly engaging, and the research suggests that the other person feels the same way.

@photo lf25_b05_01.jpg | A folded question mark
A single question mark folded from paper. It's a small shape, but the research says that the way we use it can change how a conversation feels, and how much we learn from the person across the table.

@list A03 living_room_day | Picture this scene | Two neighbours, one fence ;; A dispute about a tree ;; The first question is about the tree's history ;; The second is about what it means to them
Picture two neighbours at a fence, in a dispute about a tree. The first starts by demanding that it come down. The second starts by asking, how long have you had the tree? It turns out that it was planted by the neighbour's late husband. The question has changed the topic, from a hazard to a memory, and the conversation softens. A second follow-up, what would you want to happen to it, brings real options onto the table, such as trimming. This is an invented scene, but it shows why follow-up questions matter: they take us from positions, such as cut it down, to interests, such as I don't want to lose this.

@list A19 living_room_day | Questions to avoid | Why would you ever do that? ;; Don't you think you should...? ;; Are you serious? ;; You don't really believe that, do you?
Not every question helps, so here are four that tend to hurt. The first, why would you ever do that, sounds like curiosity, but it carries a verdict. The second, don't you think you should, is advice dressed as a question, and people usually hear it. The third, are you serious, signals disbelief and shuts people down. And the fourth, you don't really believe that, do you, is a challenge. These are what researchers call rhetorical or leading questions, and they don't invite an answer. They invite a defence. A good test is to ask yourself whether you really want to know the answer. If you do, ask it plainly. If you don't, you may be making a statement, and it's more honest to say it as one.

@char A15 cabin_fireplace | A gentle thought | Curiosity is generous
To be curious about someone is to tell them that they matter. It's one of the cheapest forms of generosity that there is, and one of the most powerful. You don't need to agree, or to fix anything. You only need to want to know what it's like to be them, even for a few minutes.

@char A08 cabin_fireplace | Looking back | What questions taught us
So what did we learn? That people who ask more questions are liked more. That follow-up questions matter most, because they show you listened. That genuine questions reduce tension. And that deeper conversations are more enjoyable than we expect.
