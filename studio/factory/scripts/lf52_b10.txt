@chapter N10 rooftop_dusk | Strange cases
Every theory of normal experience gets tested at the edges, by the unusual cases that stretch it. For déjà vu, those cases include a few of the most remarkable stories in memory research. They're not typical, and they don't describe what most of us feel. But they show what the familiarity system looks like when it can't switch off, or when a medicine tips it over.

@char N19 cafe_sunset | The man who had seen it all | A case from 2005
In 2005, a team of researchers led by Christopher Moulin, then at the University of Leeds, described a man they called AKP, an elderly retired engineer, who had become convinced that almost everything he met had already happened. He refused to watch television, because he had seen the programmes before. He didn't want to go to the doctor, because he had already been, and knew what they would say.

@study N02 cabin_fireplace | Moulin and colleagues | Neuropsychologia, 2005 | Disordered memory awareness: recollective confabulation in two cases of persistent déjà vécu
The researchers published their findings on AKP, and a second patient, in 2005. They used the term déjà vécu, already lived, because the patients didn't merely feel that things were familiar. They believed, and acted as though, they had actually lived through the same events. And, unlike ordinary déjà vu, the patients didn't realise that the feeling was mistaken.

@char N09 living_room_day | A crucial difference | Belief, not just feeling
This is the crucial difference from the everyday experience. In ordinary déjà vu, as we saw in chapter one, the person knows the feeling is wrong. In chronic déjà vécu, that checking step seems to be damaged. The patients treated the feeling as a fact, and they built stories to explain it, as if the sense of the familiar was a reliable memory.

@char N10 living_room_day | The brain side | A hint about checking
Researchers noted that AKP had memory problems, and they discussed possible involvement of the brain's temporal regions. It's difficult to draw firm conclusions from one man. But the case hints at something important: normal déjà vu may depend on two things. A source of false familiarity, and a working system that checks the feeling against what we know, and rejects it when it doesn't fit.

@list N27 rooftop_dusk | Two ingredients of ordinary déjà vu | A feeling of familiarity with no source ;; A checking system that says: this can't be right
Seen that way, ordinary déjà vu has two ingredients. First, a feeling of familiarity with no source. Second, a checking system that says, this can't be right. In everyday déjà vu, both are present, which is why the experience is so odd. In the unusual cases, the checking system fails, and the feeling is believed.

@study N19 cafe_sunset | Akira O'Connor and Chris Moulin | Current Psychiatry Reports, 2010 | Recognition without identification, erroneous familiarity, and déjà vu
Akira O'Connor and Christopher Moulin, in a 2010 review, drew these cases together with laboratory work. They argued that déjà vu is a window on the metacognitive part of memory, the part that monitors our own memory and decides whether to trust it. The feeling is one thing, they proposed, and the verdict on the feeling is another.

@photo lf52_b10_01.jpg | An old clock and a calendar on a wall
An old clock and a calendar on a wall. Imagine living in a world where each day feels like a repeat of the last, and you can't convince yourself otherwise. That's a glimpse of the experience of persistent déjà vécu, and it shows how much of ordinary life depends on the quiet fact-checker in the mind, which we rarely notice until it fails.

@char N02 living_room_day | A different cause | A medicine and a flu
Not every odd case comes from the memory system alone. In 2001, two Finnish doctors, Tero Taiminen and Sami Jääskeläinen, described a healthy man who developed intense and repeated déjà vu while taking two medicines at the same time for a flu-like illness. One was amantadine, which affects the brain's dopamine system, and the other was phenylpropanolamine, a decongestant.

@study N10 cabin_fireplace | Taiminen and Jääskeläinen | Journal of Clinical Neuroscience, 2001 | Intense and recurrent déjà vu experiences related to amantadine and phenylpropanolamine in a healthy male
According to the report, the man's episodes faded after the medicines were stopped. The doctors suggested that the drugs might have increased dopamine activity in the temporal lobe. It's a single case, and nobody should draw broad conclusions, but it supports the view that déjà vu depends on the chemistry of the memory regions, and can be nudged by it.

@char N19 living_room_day | A caution | A single case isn't a rule
A caution is needed here too. A single case report shows that something is possible, not that it's common. Nobody should stop or change any medicine because of what they've heard in a video, and this video isn't medical advice. If a medicine seems to cause an unusual feeling, that's a conversation for a doctor or a pharmacist, who can weigh the benefits against the side effects.

@char N09 cafe_sunset | What these cases share | The system, pushed
What these cases share is that they push the system beyond its usual range. In AKP, the checking step fails. In the amantadine case, the chemistry shifts. In epilepsy, the electrical activity runs ahead. In each case, the same basic circuit is involved, the one that produces and evaluates familiarity, and it's showing us what it's made of.

@char N10 living_room_day | A useful lesson | Normal is a balance
A useful lesson from the unusual cases is that a normal mind is a balance between competing forces. The feeling of familiarity is helpful when it's accurate. The checking system is helpful when it can override the feeling. And the combination lets us live in a world where most things are as they seem, but where odd moments are noticed and corrected.

@char N02 cabin_fireplace | A thought about the checker | Doubt as a skill
It's tempting to think of doubt as a weakness. But the checker in the mind, the part that says, wait, that can't be right, is one of the most valuable things we have. It's what keeps Elena, in her friend's hallway, from believing that she has literally lived this moment before. It lets her enjoy the strangeness of the feeling, and also dismiss it.

@char N19 cafe_sunset | Elena, once more | Her checker works
In Elena's case, the checker worked exactly as it should. She felt the familiarity. She also noticed that she couldn't place it, and that she had no good reason to think she'd been there. She had the rest of the evening to chat, and the feeling faded before the tea was poured. That is what a healthy memory system looks like at work.

@char N09 living_room_day | Looking back | What we set out to learn
So where do we stand? We've seen that Moulin's team described AKP in 2005, a man with persistent déjà vécu who believed the feeling was real. We've seen that O'Connor and Moulin framed déjà vu as a gap between a feeling and the system that checks it, and that a 2001 case linked unusual déjà vu to medicines affecting dopamine.

In the next chapter, we'll turn the idea inside out and look at the opposite feeling, when something very familiar suddenly seems strange, and what that reveals about how the mind holds on to meaning.
