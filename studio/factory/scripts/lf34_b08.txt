@chapter A19 cabin_fireplace | When the alarm stays on
Let's go a little deeper now. Everything we've seen so far, the racing heart, the wave of cortisol, the sharp focus and the scrambled memory, makes good sense for a short emergency. A few minutes of danger, and then it's over. But modern life doesn't always work like that.

@char A13 living_room_day | Not one morning | Months of mornings
Imagine that Jonah's Monday isn't just one difficult morning. Imagine that every morning feels a bit like that, for months. A job that never quite settles, worries at home, sleep that never quite catches up. No single moment is a disaster. But the alarm never fully goes quiet.

@char A03 cafe_sunset | A useful word | Wear and tear
In 1998, the neuroscientist Bruce McEwen wrote an influential article that gave scientists a useful way to talk about this. He built on a word that two other researchers, Peter Sterling and Joseph Eyer, had suggested a decade earlier. The word was allostasis.

@study A10 living_room_day | Bruce McEwen | New England Journal of Medicine, 1998 | Stress hormones protect us in the short run and can wear us down in the long run
Allostasis means staying stable by changing. When you stand up, your blood pressure adjusts. When you face a challenge, stress hormones rise. That flexibility keeps you alive. But McEwen pointed out that when those systems are switched on too often, or for too long, or can't switch off properly, the adjustments themselves start to cost something. He called that cost allostatic load.

@list A05 autumn_overlook | When the load builds up | The alarm fires too often ;; It stays on too long ;; It doesn't switch off properly ;; It isn't there when it's needed
McEwen described a few ways it can go wrong. The alarm can fire too often. It can stay on too long after the danger has passed. It can fail to switch off properly. Or, in some cases, it can become sluggish and fail to respond when it really is needed. In each case, the very system that is meant to protect us becomes a source of wear.

@chapter A12 cafe_sunset | Why zebras don't get ulcers
The neuroscientist Robert Sapolsky, who has spent decades studying stress in baboons as well as in the lab, put this idea into a memorable picture.

@study A20 cafe_sunset | Robert Sapolsky | Book, 1994 | Why Zebras Don't Get Ulcers
In his 1994 book, Why Zebras Don't Get Ulcers, Sapolsky asks us to think about a zebra on the savannah. A lion appears. The zebra's alarm system fires at full power, and it runs for its life. A few minutes later, either it has escaped, or, sadly, it hasn't. Either way, the emergency is over.

@char A15 autumn_overlook | Back to grazing | The emergency is over
If the zebra escapes, it goes back to grazing. It doesn't lie awake that night replaying the chase. It doesn't worry about whether the lion will be back next Tuesday. Its stress response switched on for a real, physical emergency, and then it switched off again.

@char A13 rooftop_dusk | We are different | We can replay it
We are different. We can switch on the very same alarm for things that aren't happening at all. A conversation from last week. A deadline next month. Something that might never happen. Sapolsky's point is that our wonderful ability to remember and imagine also lets us keep the alarm running, long after any lion has gone.

@char A10 living_room_day | Baboons with free time | A very human problem
Sapolsky spent many summers studying wild baboons in East Africa. And he liked to point out that baboons, unlike zebras, have a lot in common with us here. Food is often easy to find, so they have plenty of free time. And they use some of it to make each other's lives difficult, with social tension that never quite ends. Their stress, like ours, is often social, and it often lingers.

@photo lf34_b08_01.jpg | After the chase, quiet again
Picture the grassland in the evening, after the chase is over. The light is soft and the grass is still. For the zebra, that quiet comes back on its own. For us, it sometimes has to be invited back, gently, on purpose.

@chapter A10 living_room_day | Stress and the common cold
So does long-lasting stress actually show up in the body? This is where we need to be careful, because health is complicated, and a single study never tells the whole story. But some experiments have been remarkably clear.

@study A03 cafe_sunset | Sheldon Cohen, David Tyrrell and Andrew Smith | New England Journal of Medicine, 1991 | Volunteers with more stress in their lives were more likely to catch a cold
In 1991, Sheldon Cohen and his colleagues published a study with about four hundred healthy volunteers in Britain. First, each person filled in questionnaires about the stress in their lives. Then, with their agreement, they were given nose drops containing a common cold virus, and kept in quarantine while the researchers watched to see who became ill.

@bars A12 living_room_day | Developed a cold | % of volunteers | Lowest stress:27 ;; Highest stress:47
The results followed a steady pattern. The more stress people had reported, the more likely they were to develop a cold. Among the least stressed, around twenty seven percent came down with one. Among the most stressed, around forty seven percent did. And the pattern held even after the researchers took account of things like age, smoking and sleep.

@char A19 cabin_fireplace | A careful reading | Not a guarantee
That doesn't mean stress causes colds on its own. Everyone in the study was exposed to a virus, and plenty of very stressed people stayed perfectly well. What it suggests is that a long period of stress may tip the odds a little, in a body that's being asked to do too much at once.

@char A13 living_room_day | Why it was convincing | Everyone met the same virus
What made this study so convincing was its design. Everyone was exposed to a virus in the same controlled way, and everyone was watched under the same conditions. So the difference in who fell ill couldn't simply be explained by some people meeting more germs on the bus than others.

@chapter A13 cabin_fireplace | Healing more slowly
Another study looked at something even more concrete. Not catching an illness, but healing from a tiny wound.

@study A10 living_room_day | Janice Kiecolt-Glaser and colleagues | The Lancet, 1995 | Small skin wounds healed more slowly in long-term family caregivers
In 1995, Janice Kiecolt-Glaser and her colleagues compared two small groups of women. One group were caring for a relative with dementia, often for years, which is known to be one of the most demanding situations a person can face. The other group were similar women without that responsibility. Each woman agreed to have a tiny, standard wound made on her arm, and the researchers measured how long it took to heal.

@stat A03 cafe_sunset | Caregivers' wounds took longer to heal | 9 days | longer, on average
The caregivers' wounds took about nine days longer to heal, on average. Same small wound, same careful treatment. The groups were small, and studies like this can't show exactly which part of the caregivers' lives made the difference. But it's a striking sign that the body keeps track of the mind's burdens.

@char A08 cabin_fireplace | A gentle word | For anyone carrying a lot
If you are caring for someone, or carrying something heavy for a long time, that finding isn't meant to add one more worry. If anything, it's a reason to be kind to yourself, and to accept help when it's offered. Rest isn't a luxury in those seasons of life. It's part of the work.

@chapter A12 cafe_sunset | Not a verdict
It would be easy, at this point, to come away thinking that stress is simply poison. That every racing heartbeat is doing quiet damage. But that isn't what the science says, and it isn't what McEwen or Sapolsky argued either.

@list A05 autumn_overlook | What these studies do and don't say | Short bursts of stress are normal and useful ;; Long, unrelenting stress is what tends to cost ;; Studies show odds, not destinies ;; Rest, sleep and support help the system recover
Short bursts of stress are a normal, healthy part of life. It's long, unrelenting stress, with little chance to recover, that tends to wear us down. These studies talk about odds across groups of people, not destinies for any one person. And the body is built to recover, especially when it gets rest, sleep and support.

@char A19 cabin_fireplace | Just curiosity | Not medical advice
And of course, this is a story told for curiosity, not medical advice. If stress has been weighing on you for a long time, or your body is telling you something isn't right, a doctor is the right person to talk to. There's no prize for carrying it alone.

@char A13 rooftop_dusk | A strange twist | It isn't only the stress
But there's one more twist in this story, and it's one of the most surprising findings in all of stress research. Because it turns out that the effect stress has on you may depend, at least a little, on something you might never have thought about. What you believe stress is doing to you.

@char A10 living_room_day | Remember the question | From the very beginning
Do you remember the question from the start of the night? Whether believing something about stress could actually change what it does to you? It sounds almost too neat to be true. So let's look at the evidence carefully, and with the doubts left in.
