@chapter A12 living_room_day | Senses that talk to each other
It's natural to think of the senses as separate departments. The eyes handle light. The ears handle sound. The skin handles touch. Each department sends its own report up to the brain, and the brain reads them one by one. Neat and tidy. But the brain, it turns out, doesn't keep its departments apart at all.

@char A19 cabin_fireplace | A puzzle | Listening with your eyes
Think about a noisy party. Someone across the table is telling you a story, and the music is loud, but you can still follow along. Part of the reason is that you're watching their lips. You're hearing with your eyes, a little. And in 1976, two researchers in England discovered just how strongly the eyes can steer the ears.

@study A14 street_day | Harry McGurk & John MacDonald | University of Surrey, published 1976 | A video of a face saying 'ga' was dubbed with the sound 'ba'; most people heard a third sound, 'da'
In 1976, Harry McGurk and John MacDonald published a short paper in the journal Nature with a lovely title, Hearing lips and seeing voices. They made a video of a person saying a syllable, and then dubbed a different syllable over it.

@list A03 autumn_overlook | The recipe | The sound says: ba ;; The lips say: ga ;; What people hear: da ;; Close your eyes, and it's ba again
Here's the recipe. The soundtrack says ba. The lips, on screen, say ga. And what most people report hearing is neither. They hear da, a sound somewhere in between, a compromise that the brain invents to make the lips and the sound agree.

@char A06 rooftop_dusk | The strange part | Knowing doesn't help
And here's the part that people find almost unsettling. Even when you know exactly how the trick works, it still happens. You can watch the video, hear da, then close your eyes and hear ba, then open them and hear da again. The sound coming from the speaker never changes. What changes is what your brain decides you heard.

@char A13 attic_rain | What it means | Hearing is a guess too
That tells us something important. What you hear is not just the sound that reaches your ears. It's the brain's best guess about what's being said, using every clue it has. And when the lips and the sound disagree, the brain doesn't show you the conflict. It quietly settles it, and shows you the answer.

@stat A10 living_room_day | Hearing lips and seeing voices | 1976 | the year McGurk & MacDonald published the effect in Nature
This is now called the McGurk effect, and it has become one of the most famous demonstrations in all of perception research. It reminds us that speech is something we partly see.

@chapter A16 autumn_overlook | When sound changes sight
So the eyes can change what you hear. But does it work the other way round? Can sound change what you see? For a long time, many researchers thought vision was the boss, and the other senses simply followed along. Then came a surprisingly simple experiment.

@study A11 cafe_sunset | Ladan Shams, Yukiyasu Kamitani & Shinsuke Shimojo | Caltech, published 2000 | One brief flash with two quick beeps was often seen as two flashes
In 2000, Ladan Shams, Yukiyasu Kamitani and Shinsuke Shimojo, at the California Institute of Technology, published a paper in Nature called What you see is what you hear. They showed people a single brief flash of light on a screen, and at the same time played a series of short beeps.

@list A15 cafe_sunset | The double flash | One flash of light ;; Two quick beeps ;; People see two flashes ;; The light only flashed once
When a single flash came with one beep, people saw one flash, as you'd expect. But when that same single flash came with two quick beeps, people often reported seeing two flashes. The light on the screen had flashed only once. The second flash was created by the brain, borrowed from the ears.

@photo lf30_b08_01.jpg | Light and sound, blended
Think of it like this. When it comes to timing, how many things happened and when, the ears are often more precise than the eyes. So the brain leans on the ears. If they say two things happened, the brain is willing to show you two flashes, even if the eyes only caught one.

@chapter A02 street_day | The ventriloquist's secret
You've probably experienced a version of this yourself, without knowing it had a name.

@char A20 living_room_day | Where does the voice come from? | The puppet speaks
Think of a ventriloquist with a puppet on their knee. The voice is coming from the ventriloquist's mouth, which barely moves. But you hear the voice coming from the puppet, whose mouth is flapping away. Or think about watching a film. The speakers may be on the sides of the room, but the voices seem to come from the actors' faces on the screen.

@char A18 cabin_fireplace | The ventriloquism effect | Sight pulls sound
This is called the ventriloquism effect. Your brain takes the sound and moves it, in your experience, to the place where the eyes say it's most likely to come from. A moving mouth is a very convincing clue. So the sound is relocated to match.

@study A14 autumn_overlook | David Alais & David Burr | published 2004, Current Biology | When the light was blurred, sound pulled the sense of location instead; the brain weighs each sense by how reliable it is
In 2004, David Alais and David Burr published a study in the journal Current Biology that explained when this happens and when it doesn't. They made the light harder to locate by blurring it, and found that the effect could reverse. With a sharp light, the eyes won and pulled the sound towards them. With a very blurry light, the ears won, and the sound pulled the light.

@list A03 living_room_day | How the brain weighs the senses | Each sense sends a guess ;; Each guess has a reliability ;; More reliable senses count more ;; The result is one blended experience
That's an elegant finding. The brain isn't simply obeying the eyes. It's weighing each sense by how trustworthy it is in that moment, and blending them into one experience. Clear vision gets a big vote. Blurry vision gets a small one. It's a little like a sensible committee listening most to whoever knows the most.

@chapter A05 autumn_overlook | Sound you can feel
And the blending doesn't stop at eyes and ears. Even touch can be tuned by sound.

@study A11 street_day | Veikko Jousmäki & Riitta Hari | Helsinki University of Technology, published 1998 | People rubbed their hands together while hearing the sound played back with the high frequencies boosted; their skin felt drier, like paper
In 1998, Veikko Jousmäki and Riitta Hari published a study in Current Biology about what they called the parchment skin illusion. People rubbed their palms together while wearing headphones, listening to the sound of their own rubbing. When the researchers boosted the high, crisp frequencies of that sound, people reported that their hands felt drier and rougher, almost like paper.

#ai lf30_b08_02.jpg | Editorial still-life of a sheet of old parchment paper and a pair of wired headphones resting on a linen tablecloth, soft daylight, calm quiet mood, no people, no text, no logos

@photo lf30_b08_02.jpg | Paper-dry hands
Their skin hadn't changed. Their hands were exactly as soft or as dry as before. But the sound told the brain a story about texture, and the brain believed it, and so the skin felt different. Touch, it seems, is partly heard.

@char A19 cabin_fireplace | One world, not five | The brain blends
So the old picture of five separate senses, each sending its own report, doesn't really hold. The brain is constantly blending them. It combines the sight of lips with the sound of speech, the timing of beeps with the timing of flashes, the crackle of a sound with the feel of skin, and it gives you one smooth, unified world.

@list A12 cafe_sunset | The senses, tested | Lips change what you hear ;; Beeps change what you see ;; Sight moves where sound comes from ;; Sound changes how skin feels
So the belief that each sense works alone turns out to be wrong. Lips can change what you hear. Beeps can change what you see. Sight can move where a sound seems to come from. And sound can even change how your own skin feels.
