@chapter A19 autumn_overlook | Listening to laughter
One of the people who listened most carefully was an American neuroscientist called Robert Provine. In the late twentieth century he did something that sounds almost too simple to count as science. He went out into the world with a notebook and listened to people laughing, in shopping centres, on pavements and in student buildings, writing down what happened just before each laugh.

@study N09 living_room_day | Robert Provine | Laughter: A Scientific Investigation, 2000 | Notes on everyday laughter in public places
His results, gathered in his book Laughter: A Scientific Investigation, published in 2000, surprised even him. He and his students recorded about twelve hundred laugh episodes in everyday conversation. For each one, they noted the remark that came just before the laugh. And then they asked a simple question. How many of those remarks were actually funny?

@stat N07 rooftop_dusk | Remarks before a laugh that were jokes | under 20% | in Provine's everyday notes
The answer was not many. Fewer than one in five of the remarks that came before a laugh were anything like a joke. Most were completely ordinary. Things like, I'll see you later, or, look, it's Andre, or, I hope we all do well. People were laughing constantly, but mostly not at anything funny. Laughter, it seemed, was mainly about something else.

@char N20 living_room_day | So what is it about | If not jokes
If not jokes, then what? Provine's answer was that laughter is mostly social. It's a signal between people, a way of saying I'm friendly, I'm with you, this is play, we're fine. Jokes are one way to set it off, but they're a small part of the story. Most of the time, we laugh to keep the conversation warm.

@char N27 cafe_sunset | Who laughs most | The speaker, not the listener
He noticed something else, too. In his notes, the person speaking usually laughed more than the person listening. If laughter were only a reaction to humour, you'd expect the audience to do most of it. Instead, speakers laughed at their own ordinary remarks, as if to mark them as friendly, or to invite the listener in.

@list A13 attic_rain | The punctuation effect | Laughter comes at the end of phrases ;; Rarely in the middle of a sentence ;; Speech gets priority over laughter
Provine also noticed where laughter falls. It almost always comes at the end of a phrase or sentence, in the natural pauses, rather than in the middle of a word. He called this the punctuation effect. Laughter seems to wait its turn, slotting itself into the gaps in speech like a comma or a full stop.

@char N01 cabin_fireplace | A little breathing trick | Why you can't talk and laugh
That makes sense when you think about the body. Laughing and speaking both use the same breath and the same voice box, and they can't easily happen at the same moment. That's part of why Nadia couldn't speak at the table. Her breath had been taken over by laughter, and her words had to wait until it let go.

@char A03 bedroom_night | What a laugh is made of | Breath, chopped into pieces
So what is a laugh, physically? At its heart, it's a way of breathing out. The muscles around your ribs and belly squeeze in a series of quick pulses, chopping one breath into short pieces. Each piece can come out as a little burst of voice, the familiar ha, ha, ha, or as a breathy snort or a quiet wheeze.

@study A13 autumn_overlook | Jo-Anne Bachorowski, Moria Smoski and Michael Owren | Journal of the Acoustical Society of America, 2001 | The acoustic features of human laughter
In 2001, a team led by Jo-Anne Bachorowski recorded students watching funny film clips and analysed more than a thousand bouts of laughter. They found that laughter is wildly varied. Some laughs are song-like and voiced, with a clear pitch. Others are unvoiced: snorts, grunts, and breathy, hissing sounds. There's no single sound that counts as the human laugh.

@list A05 cabin_fireplace | Some of the kinds they heard | Song-like, voiced laughs ;; Snorts through the nose ;; Grunts ;; Breathy, hissing laughs
Their recordings included song-like laughs that rose and fell in pitch, snorting laughs through the nose, low grunting laughs, and breathy, almost silent ones. Nadia's own laugh, if we could record it, would probably move between several of these in a single bout, starting with a snort and rising into something more musical.

@study A12 street_day | Jo-Anne Bachorowski and Michael Owren | Psychological Science, 2001 | Not all laughs are alike
And the kinds were not all received the same way. In a related study published the same year, Bachorowski and Owren found that voiced, song-like laughs tended to draw more positive reactions from listeners than unvoiced snorts and grunts. People rated them as friendlier and wanted to meet the person laughing. The sound of a laugh, not just the fact of it, seems to matter.

@char A19 bedroom_night | A face that joins in | Eyes, cheeks and mouth
A laugh isn't only a sound. The face joins in. The corners of the mouth pull up and back, the cheeks lift, and the muscles around the eyes tighten so that little creases form at the corners. In a strong laugh, the head may tip back, the shoulders shake, and the eyes begin to water, just as Nadia's did.

@char A20 cafe_sunset | And sometimes the whole body | Weak with laughter
In very strong laughter, the whole body can be involved. People double over, hold their sides, slap the table, or lean on each other for support. Some, like Nadia, feel their arms and legs go oddly weak. We even have a phrase for it in English: weak with laughter. It's one of those old sayings that turns out to describe something real.

@char A10 rooftop_dusk | So the first answer | What a laugh is
So here's our first, simple answer. A laugh is a special way of breathing out, shaped into sound by the voice, joined by the face and sometimes the whole body. And in everyday life, it's mostly not a response to jokes at all. It's something we do with each other, usually at the end of a perfectly ordinary sentence.

@char N14 attic_rain | Back at the table | A second question
Back at the restaurant, the laughter has finally faded into happy sighs. Nadia wipes her eyes and notices something. If she'd seen the teapot lid fall at home, alone, she might have smiled. She certainly wouldn't have laughed until she cried. So why was it so much funnier with her friends around her? That's where we're going next.
