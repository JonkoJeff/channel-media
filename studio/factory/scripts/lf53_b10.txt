@chapter N07 cafe_sunset | Who gets them most
Ruth settles on the sofa with Biscuit beside her and opens the folder of music for Thursday's choir practice. The choir is learning three new pieces this term, and she looks over the parts she needs to practise. As she does, she wonders whether singing every week makes her more prone to earworms. It's a sensible question, and researchers have looked at it from several directions.

@study N09 living_room_day | Lassi Liikkanen | Psychology of Music, 2012 | Musical activities predispose to involuntary musical imagery
The title of Lassi Liikkanen's large Finnish survey, the one with more than eleven thousand people, gives away its main conclusion: Musical activities predispose to involuntary musical imagery. People who were more involved with music, through listening, playing or singing, tended to report earworms more often than people for whom music played a smaller part in daily life.

@char N10 living_room_day | Musicians and earworms | Hyman's survey
Ira Hyman's team found a similar pattern in a survey of two hundred and ninety-nine students. Those who were musicians reported experiencing earworms more often. That makes intuitive sense. Someone who practises music regularly has a large, well-rehearsed library of tunes in memory, and spends more time with music on their mind, both of which might give earworms more opportunities to start.

@stat N20 cafe_sunset | Students surveyed by Hyman's team | 299 | in the survey part of the study
Two hundred and ninety-nine students, in the survey part of the Going Gaga study. Besides the link with being a musician, the survey confirmed several findings we've already met: songs that people enjoyed, and songs they had heard recently, were the most likely to get stuck, and a very wide variety of songs became earworms for different people.

@char N09 living_room_day | But not expertise | Beaman and Williams
Yet Beaman and Williams, in their natural history study, found something a little different. Earworms were not more common in people with musical expertise. What did seem to matter was how important people considered music to be in their lives. Those who rated music as more important were more likely to get earworms, whatever their level of skill.

@char N20 cafe_sunset | Two ideas together | Involvement, not training
These findings can be brought together quite neatly. It may not be formal training that matters most, but involvement: how much music someone listens to, sings, plays and cares about. A professional pianist and an enthusiastic amateur who sings in the car every day may both be prone to earworms, because music fills a large part of their mental lives.

@photo lf53_b10_01.jpg | A choir folder and sheet music on a sofa
A choir folder resting on a sofa. Ruth's weekly singing probably does make earworms a little more likely for her. She spends time each week learning and repeating melodies, she listens to music a lot, and she cares about it. By every measure in these studies, she's the kind of person who tends to report earworms more often, and she doesn't seem to mind.

@list N27 rooftop_dusk | Who tends to report more earworms | People more involved with music ;; Musicians, in Hyman's survey ;; People who say music matters to them ;; Not necessarily trained experts
Here's a summary. In the large Finnish survey, people more involved in musical activities reported more earworms. In Hyman's survey, musicians reported more. In Beaman and Williams's study, people who said music was important to them were more likely to get them, while musical expertise in itself didn't make a clear difference.

@char N10 living_room_day | Thoughts that return | A wider pattern
Some researchers have also looked at whether earworms relate to other kinds of repetitive thinking. Beaman and Williams's paper described earworms as a natural model of intrusive thoughts more generally: thoughts that come unbidden and can be hard to dismiss. Studying a harmless, everyday example like an earworm may help us understand how all such thoughts start and stop.

@char N09 cafe_sunset | Keeping it in proportion | Normal for most people
It's important to keep this in proportion. For the vast majority of people, earworms are simply a normal part of a musical mind, not a symptom of anything. If repetitive, unwanted thoughts of any kind are causing real distress or getting in the way of everyday life, that's worth talking about with a doctor. But a chorus that hangs around after the supermarket is, for most of us, just a chorus.

@char N20 living_room_day | Ruth's choir pieces | Now there are four songs
Ruth begins to hum through one of the choir pieces, a slow, gentle arrangement of an old folk song, to practise her part. For a few minutes, the supermarket chorus disappears, crowded out by the folk tune she's actively singing. When she stops, there's a brief silence. And then, very softly, which tune do you think starts playing in her head?

@list N07 cabin_fireplace | Swapping one for another | A common experience
It's the folk tune. She has swapped one earworm for another. That's a very common experience, and as we'll see later, it's the basis of one of the most popular strategies people use to get rid of an earworm: putting a different song in its place. Sometimes it works, and sometimes, as for Ruth tonight, it simply changes the soundtrack.

@char N10 living_room_day | Variety among people | Not everyone is the same
Even among people with similar musical lives, some report earworms far more often than others. Some find them pleasant, others irritating. Some can dismiss them easily, and others find them stubborn. Researchers wanted to understand these differences more precisely, which led them to develop careful questionnaires, and even to look at the structure of the brain.

@char N09 cafe_sunset | Ruth's question answered | Probably yes
So, does Ruth's choir make her more prone to earworms? The evidence suggests it might, a little, not because of any particular skill, but because music is woven so deeply into her week. She hears it, sings it and thinks about it, and her musical memory is always well stocked. Earworms are, in a sense, a side effect of loving music.

@char N20 living_room_day | A kind thought | The price of a full library
There's a gentle way of seeing that. A musical mind is a well-furnished mind. It holds hundreds or thousands of songs, ready to be recalled, sung and enjoyed. An occasional earworm is a small price to pay for such a rich store. Ruth, putting her choir folder down, would probably agree.

@list N10 cabin_fireplace | Looking back | Who gets them most
So, in this chapter, we've seen that people more involved with music tend to report more earworms, from Liikkanen's Finnish survey of over eleven thousand people to Hyman's survey of two hundred and ninety-nine students. Beaman and Williams found that the importance of music in someone's life seemed to matter more than musical expertise.
