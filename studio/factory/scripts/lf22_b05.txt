@chapter A12 living_room_day | Myth five: copying is fake
Next time you're sitting with a friend in a café, try to notice something without changing it. Look at how you're both sitting. There's a good chance you're mirroring each other. Perhaps you both have your arms resting the same way. Perhaps when one of you leans forward, the other does too, a few seconds later. Most of the time, neither of you has any idea it's happening.

@char A10 attic_rain | A suspicious idea | Isn't copying insincere?
When people learn about this, some feel a little uneasy. Copying sounds like a sales technique, something fake or manipulative. But the research suggests that most of this mirroring is natural, unconscious and closely tied to genuine connection. Let's look at how it was discovered.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Chartrand & Bargh | Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 1999 | The chameleon effect: the perception-behavior link and social interaction
In 1999, Tanya Chartrand and John Bargh invited students to describe photographs together with another person, who was secretly working with the researchers. In one study, this partner either rubbed their face or jiggled their foot throughout the conversation. The researchers filmed the participants without telling them what they were looking for.

@char A12 cafe_sunset | Unconscious copying | Faces and feet
Participants tended to rub their own faces more when their partner rubbed their face, and shake their feet more when their partner shook a foot. When asked afterwards, almost none of them had noticed what their partner was doing, let alone that they'd copied it. Chartrand and Bargh called it the chameleon effect, after the lizard that changes colour to match its surroundings.

@char A03 living_room_day | The reverse | Being mirrored
Then they turned it around. In another study, the partner either subtly mirrored the participant's posture and movements, or simply sat in a neutral way. Afterwards, the participants who had been mirrored said they liked their partner more, and felt that the interaction had gone more smoothly, even though they hadn't noticed being copied.

@char A13 rooftop_dusk | A social glue | Mirroring and liking
So mirroring seems to work in both directions. We tend to copy people we're engaged with, and being copied tends to make us feel more comfortable with someone. Researchers have described this as a kind of social glue, something that helps interactions flow without anyone having to think about it.

@chapter A15 cafe_sunset | Copying more when we want to belong
Here's a further twist, and it suggests that mirroring is closely tied to our deep need to belong.

@study A04 attic_rain | Lakin & Chartrand | Psychological Science, 2003 | Using nonconscious behavioral mimicry to create affiliation and rapport
In 2003, Jessica Lakin and Tanya Chartrand found that people mimicked others more when they had a goal to get along with them. And in another study, people who had just experienced being left out of a group, in a short online ball-tossing game, went on to mimic a new person more than people who hadn't been left out.

@char A10 cabin_fireplace | Reaching out | Without knowing it
It's rather touching when you think about it. When we feel a little lonely or left out, part of us reaches out to the next person we meet, in small, unconscious ways. We lean in a little more. We mirror a little more. Our bodies try to build a bridge before our minds have even decided to.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | van Baaren, Holland, Kawakami & van Knippenberg | Psychological Science, 2004 | Mimicry and prosocial behavior
And mirroring may spread warmth further than a single conversation. In 2004, Rick van Baaren and his colleagues found that people who had been subtly mimicked by an experimenter were later more likely to help when the experimenter dropped some pens. Being mirrored seemed to leave people feeling a little more generous, even towards others.

@chapter A12 living_room_day | Words that match
It isn't only our bodies that fall into step. Our words do too.

@study A04 attic_rain | Ireland and colleagues | Psychological Science, 2011 | Language style matching predicts relationship initiation and stability
In 2011, Molly Ireland, James Pennebaker and their colleagues looked at something called language style matching. Not what people talk about, but small function words, like I, the, and, though and quite. How similar are two people in their use of these little words?

@char A03 cafe_sunset | Speed dates and messages | Matching styles
In one study, they analysed conversations from speed dates. Pairs whose language styles matched more closely were more likely to want to see each other again. In another, they looked at instant messages between couples. Couples with more closely matched styles were more likely to still be together several months later.

@char A13 rooftop_dusk | Without trying | You start to sound alike
Nobody chooses to match their use of the word the. It happens on its own, when two people are tuned in to each other. It's another quiet sign of connection, like the rhythm of laughter, or the timing of replies. Close friends and couples often find they start to sound a little alike, using the same phrases, the same jokes, the same small words.

@chapter A15 cabin_fireplace | Catching yawns
And here's a final, rather sweet example of how closely we're linked to the people we care about. You might want to stifle a yawn as you listen.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Norscia & Palagi | PLoS ONE, 2011 | Yawn contagion and empathy in Homo sapiens
In 2011, Ivan Norscia and Elisabetta Palagi observed yawns among adults going about their everyday lives, in ordinary settings like meals and conversations. Each time someone yawned, they noted whether anyone nearby yawned soon afterwards, and what the relationship between them was.

@list A12 living_room_day | Who catches a yawn most? | Family members ;; Then friends ;; Then acquaintances ;; Least of all, strangers
Yawns were most contagious between family members, then between friends, then acquaintances, and least between strangers. The researchers suggested that how readily we catch a yawn may reflect emotional closeness. The closer we are to someone, the more our bodies seem to follow theirs.

@char A10 attic_rain | A gentle note | Natural, not a technique
Now, an important note. All of this is about natural, unconscious mirroring. Deliberately copying every move someone makes is likely to feel strange, and if they notice, it could make things worse. The mirroring that matters arises on its own, when you're genuinely paying attention to someone and enjoying their company.

@photo lf22_b05_01.jpg | Falling into step
So, is copying fake? Mostly, no. The mirroring we do without noticing is one of the most natural signs of connection there is. It's how two people quietly fall into step, the way two friends walking together end up matching their pace without ever deciding to.

@list A13 rooftop_dusk | Signs that two people are in sync | Mirrored postures and gestures ;; Laughing at the same moments ;; Quick, easy replies ;; Similar small words and phrases ;; Even catching each other's yawns
Mirrored postures. Laughter at the same moments. Quick, easy replies. Similar small words. Even caught yawns. None of these are things we decide to do. They're things that happen when two people are truly with each other. And perhaps that's part of why some connections feel so intense even when little is said. A great deal is being said, just not in words.
