@chapter N10 bedroom_night | Settling the mind for sleep
Anton has put on a small lamp in the bedroom, and set his book on the bedside table. He's tired, and still a little wakeful, and he knows from long experience that this is the point of the evening when his mind will either settle, or start to run.

@char N02 living_room_day | A common experience | Lying awake
Many people know the feeling. You get into bed, the room goes quiet, and the thoughts begin. A list of things to do tomorrow, a conversation from three days ago, a small embarrassment from years back. It's as if the very quiet, which we expected to be restful, has turned the volume up on everything inside.

@char N19 cafe_sunset | A research field | Pre-sleep thoughts
Sleep researchers have studied this for decades. They talk about pre-sleep cognitive arousal, meaning busy, active thinking at the point of trying to fall asleep. And they've found that people who struggle to fall asleep often report more of it, and that it can feel hard to control.

@study N07 autumn_overlook | Allison Harvey | Behaviour Research and Therapy, 2002 | A cognitive model of insomnia, in which worry and monitoring at bedtime keep the mind and body alert
In 2002, the psychologist Allison Harvey published an influential model of insomnia in the journal Behaviour Research and Therapy. She proposed that worry at bedtime, and anxious monitoring of how well you're sleeping, can keep the mind and body in an alert state. And that, in turn, makes it harder to fall asleep, which creates more to worry about. A loop, once again.

@char N23 rooftop_dusk | The paradox | Trying makes it worse
There's a further twist. The harder you try to push thoughts away, the more they seem to come back. This was shown, famously, in a study by Daniel Wegner and colleagues in 1987, in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology.

@study N07 cafe_sunset | Daniel Wegner and colleagues | Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 1987 | People asked not to think of a white bear reported thinking of it frequently, and more so afterwards
Volunteers were asked to speak their thoughts aloud for five minutes, with one instruction. They were not to think of a white bear. Throughout the five minutes, the bear kept returning, on average more than once a minute. And in a later phase, when they were allowed to think of it, the people who had tried to suppress it thought about it more than people who hadn't.

@char N10 living_room_day | The lesson | Pushing away is not a plan
For sleep, the lesson is gentle but clear. Trying very hard not to think about something is unlikely to be the best plan. Better, perhaps, to give the thought a place to land. And one study has tested a simple version of this.

@study N07 autumn_overlook | Michael Scullin and colleagues | Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, 2018 | Fifty-seven young adults wrote either a to-do list or a list of completed tasks for five minutes before bed, and their time to fall asleep was measured
In 2018, Michael Scullin and his colleagues, at Baylor University, published a study in the Journal of Experimental Psychology: General. Fifty-seven young adults came to a sleep laboratory, where their sleep was recorded. For five minutes before lights-out, half of them wrote a detailed to-do list for the coming days. The other half wrote about tasks they'd already completed.

@char N02 cabin_fireplace | What they found | A faster slide into sleep
The people who wrote the to-do list fell asleep faster, by roughly nine minutes on average. And the more specific the list was, the faster they tended to drift off. The result went against what many people might guess, which is that thinking about tomorrow's chores would make you more alert.

@char N19 street_day | A possible explanation | Offloading the worry
The researchers suggested that writing things down may offload them from the mind. A job that's on paper no longer needs to be remembered, and so the mind can stop checking on it. It's a small idea, and the study was short and modest in size, but it's a pleasantly practical finding.

@list N11 cafe_sunset | Ideas the research points to | Write tomorrow's tasks on paper before bed ;; Be specific, so the mind can let go ;; Give worries a time earlier in the day ;; Don't fight a thought, let it pass ;; Keep the room dim and quiet
So a few simple ideas follow. Write down tomorrow's jobs, and be specific, so the mind doesn't need to hold on to them. Give worries a slot earlier in the day. Don't fight a thought, but let it come and go. And keep the evening light dim and quiet.

@char N10 rooftop_dusk | A related technique | A scheduled worry time
That second idea has an older source. In 1983, Thomas Borkovec and his colleagues published a paper in Behaviour Research and Therapy, on the use of stimulus control for worry. Their approach asked people to set aside a fixed period each day, outside of bedtime, to worry deliberately, and to postpone worries that arrived at other times. In small early studies, it seemed to reduce worry.

@char N09 cafe_sunset | A caution | Not a treatment on its own
It's worth being careful with what's said here. These are modest, general ideas drawn from small studies, and they aren't a substitute for help. If sleep is a long-term struggle, a doctor or a trained sleep therapist is the right person to ask. There are well-tested treatments for insomnia, and they work best when someone trained is guiding them.

@char N02 living_room_day | Another approach | Gentle distraction
Another idea, which some sleep researchers have studied, is gentle distraction. In a 2002 study, Allison Harvey and Holly Payne asked people with insomnia to try one of a few approaches at bedtime, one of which was to picture a calm, absorbing scene. In their small study, the people who used imagery distraction fell asleep faster than those using more general distraction.

@char N19 autumn_overlook | A scene to hold | Something neutral and kind
The idea behind this is to give the mind something calm to do, a little like handing a restless child a quiet task. It should be something pleasant but not exciting, with enough detail to keep the mind occupied, but not enough to stir it. A quiet garden, a slow river, a path through a wood.

@char N10 cabin_fireplace | Anton's list | A few lines on paper
Anton picks up the little notebook from his bedside table, and writes three lines. Call the printer about the maps. Send Marta the file. Buy bread. He looks at them, and something in his chest eases. He closes the notebook, and puts it back beside the lamp, with a small, satisfied nod.

@char N09 attic_rain | A gentle thought | Nothing more to hold
It's a very small act, but it's a real one. Everything that needed remembering has been put somewhere that isn't his head. For tonight, his mind can put down its bag, and stop carrying things that no longer need carrying.
