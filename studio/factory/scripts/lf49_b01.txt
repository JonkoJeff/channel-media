@chapter N27 rooftop_dusk | The wandering mind
Let's begin with the simplest question of all. How much of our waking life do our minds actually spend somewhere else?

@char N20 living_room_day | A surprising place to start | An app on a phone
For a long time, scientists who wanted to study this faced a real problem. You can't easily ask people about their thoughts in a laboratory, because the moment you ask, they stop wandering and start paying attention to the question. So researchers needed a way to catch minds in the act, in the middle of ordinary life, without warning.

@char N10 cafe_sunset | The clever trick | A gentle interruption
The trick is called experience sampling. Instead of asking people to remember what they were thinking, you interrupt them at random moments, and ask them right then. What are you doing? Are you thinking about something else? And how do you feel? A beep, three quick questions, and then back to life. Do that thousands of times, with thousands of people, and a picture begins to appear.

@study N07 cafe_sunset | Matthew Killingsworth and Daniel Gilbert | Science, 2010 | An iPhone app asked about 2,250 adults, at random moments in daily life, what they were doing, whether they were thinking about something else, and how happy they felt
In 2010, the Harvard psychologists Matthew Killingsworth and Daniel Gilbert published one of the best-known studies of this kind. They built an app for the iPhone that contacted volunteers at random times of day, and asked three things. How do you feel right now? What are you doing? And are you thinking about something other than what you're doing? In the end, they had hundreds of thousands of answers, from about two thousand two hundred and fifty adults, across a wide range of ages, jobs and countries.

@char N23 living_room_day | The first result | Surprisingly high
The first result was the one that made headlines. People reported that their minds were wandering in a large share of the samples, close to half of them. Not during rare, bored moments, but all through ordinary days. At work, at home, on the way to somewhere, and in nearly every activity that the volunteers listed.

@stat N11 autumn_overlook | Minds on the move | 47% | of the sampled moments, people reported thinking about something other than what they were doing
The exact figure was about forty-seven percent. Nearly half of the moments they sampled. And it was remarkably steady. The only activity where people reported much less wandering was intimacy, and even there, the mind wandered a little. For almost everything else, from washing up to working at a computer, it was the same story.

@char N02 street_day | A closer look | What counts as wandering
It's worth being clear about what that number means. It doesn't mean people were unhappy or broken, or even particularly distracted. It means that, at the moment the phone beeped, they were thinking about something other than the task in front of them. That might be a memory, a plan, a worry, a daydream, or just a thought that had nothing to do with anything.

@char N10 cabin_fireplace | A second finding | The mood link
Killingsworth and Gilbert asked about feelings too, and that led to the second finding, which is the one people still argue about. In their data, people were generally less happy when their minds were wandering than when they were focused on what they were doing. The title of their paper said it directly: a wandering mind is an unhappy mind.

@char N19 rooftop_dusk | A careful reading | Correlation, not cause
Their analysis also tried to say which came first. The researchers looked at the order of events, and found that mind wandering at one moment tended to predict lower mood at the next, but lower mood did not predict more wandering in the same way. That's suggestive. But as with any study based on self-reports, it can't prove that wandering is the cause, and we'll return to that question later tonight.
