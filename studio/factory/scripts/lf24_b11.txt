@chapter A05 autumn_overlook | Bubbles and echoes
One of the most common fears about social media is that it locks us inside bubbles. We follow people who agree with us, algorithms feed us more of what we like, and over time, we stop seeing anything else. Our views harden, we grow more extreme, and we lose the ability to understand people on the other side. It's a compelling story. The evidence is more tangled.

@char A12 living_room_day | Two different ideas | Echo chambers and filter bubbles
It helps to separate two ideas that often get mixed up. An echo chamber is a space in which people mostly hear their own views repeated, often because they choose to. A filter bubble is a similar situation caused by algorithms, which show us more of what they predict we'll like. The first is about our choices, and the second is about the machine's.

@list A03 living_room_day | Two explanations | Echo chamber: we pick people who agree with us ;; Filter bubble: algorithms pick content for us ;; Each could make views more extreme ;; Each is hard to measure
Both ideas are plausible, and both have been argued strongly. But they also make testable predictions, and researchers have begun to test them.

@chapter A13 rooftop_dusk | What do feeds actually show?
In 2015, a team at Facebook, led by Eytan Bakshy, with Solomon Messing and Lada Adamic, analysed the news that over ten million American users saw and clicked on. They wanted to know how much of what we see is shaped by our friends, by the algorithm, and by our own choices.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Bakshy, Messing & Adamic | Science, 2015 | Exposure to ideologically diverse news and opinion on Facebook
They found that people's friends tended to share news that matched their own politics, so the friends' selection already narrowed what people saw. The ranking algorithm reduced the share of opposing views a little further, and people's own clicks narrowed it more. In their analysis, individual choice was the biggest factor, with the algorithm playing a smaller role, though critics pointed out that the data came from the company itself.

@stat A14 conspiracy_board | Facebook users in the news exposure study | About 10.1 million | American users who shared their political affiliation on the platform
It's a good example of why this debate is hard to settle. The data is from the platform, the analysis was run by its own scientists, and the results suggest that the platform's algorithm is less to blame than the choices of users. Some outside researchers questioned the framing and the sampling, and others argued the findings had been presented in too reassuring a way.

@char A04 attic_rain | A reasonable reading | The bubble is partly us
Even so, the finding fits an old pattern in psychology. People prefer information that fits what they already think, a tendency called confirmation bias. They follow people like themselves, which is called homophily. Those tendencies shape a feed before any algorithm touches it. The algorithm may then amplify what we've already started.

@chapter A06 cafe_sunset | A direct test
So what happens if you deliberately break the bubble? In 2018, Chris Bail and his colleagues at Duke University tried it. They recruited one thousand six hundred and fifty-two Twitter users in the United States, who identified as Democrats or Republicans, and paid them to follow a bot that retweeted messages from elected officials, organisations and opinion leaders on the opposite side.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Bail, Argyle, Brown, Bumpus, Chen, Hunzaker, Lee, Mann, Merhout & Volfovsky | Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 2018 | Exposure to opposing views on social media can increase political polarization
For about a month, these users saw a stream of content from the other side. The researchers measured their attitudes before and afterwards. Republicans who followed a liberal bot became more conservative, by a modest but clear amount. Democrats who followed a conservative bot showed a small shift in a more liberal direction, though this one wasn't statistically convincing.

@list A12 living_room_day | What the Duke experiment suggests | Seeing the other side did not soften views ;; Republicans became more conservative ;; Democrats showed a smaller, uncertain shift ;; Exposure alone is not understanding
This was a surprise to many people. It suggests that simply being shown opposing views isn't enough, and may backfire when the views appear in an angry or mocking form, as they often do online. The way we meet the other side may matter as much as whether we do.

@char A01 cafe_sunset | A caution | One study, one context
As always, we should hold this loosely. The study involved a particular set of people, a particular platform, and a particular month. The researchers themselves stressed that their findings don't prove that exposure always polarises. But they challenge the comfortable idea that a bit more exposure would fix things.

@chapter A12 living_room_day | Changing the algorithm
Perhaps the most ambitious tests came around the 2020 American elections. In collaboration with Meta, a group of academics designed several large experiments, in which some users were randomly given a different version of Facebook or Instagram. The results were published in 2023, in Science and Nature, and were widely discussed.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Guess, Malhotra, Pan, Barberá, Allcott & others | Science, 2023 | How do social media feed algorithms affect attitudes and behavior in an election campaign?
In one experiment, tens of thousands of users were switched from the usual algorithmic feed to a simple chronological feed, showing posts in order of time. The change reduced the time they spent on the platform, and altered what they saw, with less political content and fewer posts from like-minded sources, though more content from untrustworthy sources. But after three months, it didn't measurably change their political attitudes or polarisation.

@char A04 attic_rain | A surprising result | Big changes, small effects
This is a result that surprised many people. Even changing the algorithm for months didn't shift beliefs in a measurable way. The researchers noted that the experiment ran for three months, during an election, and that the effects of years of use might differ. But it suggests that polarisation has deeper roots than the feed alone.

@list A03 living_room_day | What the 2020 experiments suggest | Switching to chronological feeds changed what people saw ;; It did not change political attitudes in three months ;; Reducing reshares also had limited effects ;; Polarisation has many causes besides feeds
Careful scientists don't conclude from this that feeds don't matter. They conclude that the simplest story, in which an algorithm is the main driver of political division, isn't supported by the best experiments so far.

Two jars, two colours, and a few marbles rolling in between. It's a picture of the debate: separate groups, some movement between them, and a long argument about what, exactly, keeps the marbles apart.

@list A03 living_room_day | A better way to meet the other side | Ask a question before offering an opinion ;; Look for something you can honestly agree with ;; Avoid quote-posting someone to mock them ;; Move a heated thread into a private conversation
If you'd like to understand people who disagree with you, the research suggests that the form of the encounter matters. Calm, curious questions work better than public arguments, which tend to harden both sides. Looking for even one point of honest agreement lowers the temperature. And moving a heated thread to a private message takes away the audience, and with it a lot of the performance. It won't convert anyone. But it can turn a fight into a conversation, which is a good deal more useful.

@list A12 living_room_day | Words to keep from this chapter | Echo chamber ;; Filter bubble ;; Confirmation bias ;; Homophily
Four terms about bubbles. An echo chamber is a space where we mostly hear our own views. A filter bubble is the same thing produced by algorithms. Confirmation bias is the tendency to favour what fits what we already believe. And homophily is the tendency to befriend people who are like us.

@char A15 cabin_fireplace | A gentle thought | Curiosity is a skill
If you want to climb out of any bubble, the research suggests that anger isn't the way in. A calmer approach is to ask someone on the other side a real question, and to listen to the answer. That's slow, and it's rarely done online. But it's the kind of exposure that studies of conversation suggest actually works.

@char A08 cabin_fireplace | Looking back | What bubbles taught us
So what did we learn? That echo chambers and filter bubbles are different ideas. That users' own choices shaped exposure a lot in a large study. That following the other side, in an angry setting, didn't soften views. And that changing the algorithm for three months didn't shift attitudes.
