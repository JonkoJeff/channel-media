@chapter N27 rooftop_dusk | A night that feels worse than it was
There's a fascinating twist in sleep science, and it concerns the memory of the night more than the night itself.

@char N19 living_room_day | A puzzle in the lab | Sleepers who say they're awake
Researchers who record sleep with sensors have long noticed something strange. People with insomnia often report that they slept far less than the sensors show. Some say they didn't sleep at all, while the recording shows that they slept for several hours. It's called sleep state misperception, and it's more common than you might think.

@char N09 cafe_sunset | Not a lie | The feeling is real
It's important to say what this doesn't mean. It doesn't mean those people are imagining their distress or making it up. The exhaustion, the fear of the night, the heaviness the next day are all real. What differs is the mapping between what the sleeper feels and what the sensors record. The experience of a bad night is real even when the sensors tell a different story.

@study N07 cabin_fireplace | Jennifer Mercer, Richard Bootzin and Leon Lack | Sleep, 2002 | People with insomnia and good sleepers were woken after a few minutes of light sleep and asked whether they had been asleep
In 2002, Jennifer Mercer and her colleagues ran a clever test. They watched volunteers with sensors, and when someone had been in light sleep for several minutes, they woke them and asked a simple question. Were you awake or asleep? People with insomnia were more likely than good sleepers to say that they'd been awake, even though the sensors showed the opposite.

@char N02 living_room_day | Why might this happen? | Light sleep feels like waking
One idea is that light sleep, the stage between waking and deep sleep, can feel remarkably like being awake. You can hear sounds, you can have thoughts that seem rational, and later your memory of that stage is patchy. If you wake up during it, or spend a long time in it, you may remember it as time spent lying awake.

@char N13 attic_rain | Another idea | Thoughts that stand guard
Another idea is that a mind that's still busy during light sleep keeps producing thoughts that feel exactly like waking thoughts. Researchers such as Allison Harvey and Nicole Tang have proposed that worry and attention to bodily signals may add to this blur, so that the brain gives itself a false report. These ideas are still being tested, and probably each of them is partly right.

@char N02 living_room_day | Can the picture be corrected? | Showing people the recording
Some therapists have tried to use this very mismatch. In a 2006 study, Nicole Tang and Allison Harvey showed people with insomnia how their sleep had actually looked on a recording, and found that the people's worry about sleep, and their estimates of how much they'd slept, changed afterwards. It's a small body of work, but it hints that part of the problem lives in the interpretation of the night, and that interpretations can shift.

@char N19 autumn_overlook | A different kind of rest | Even when it isn't sleep
It also reminds us of something that tired people often overlook. Even when the sensors show that you were awake, a night lying in the dark, breathing slowly, is not wasted. Your muscles rest, your heart slows, and your sleep pressure keeps its credit for the next night. It isn't a perfect substitute for sleep, but it is a long way from nothing.

@char N09 cafe_sunset | Where misperception stops | A fair limit
There are also real cases where sleep is poor. Conditions such as sleep apnoea, restless legs, depression and pain can all damage sleep, and in those cases the sensors will tell the same story as the person does. If Nora were having many bad nights every week for months, the sensible step would be to talk to a doctor, not to decide that it's all in the mind.

@char N10 cafe_sunset | An old finding | The first night in the lab
A related discovery goes back to the 1960s. Sleep scientists noticed that volunteers sleep worse on their first night in a laboratory than on the second, a pattern now called the first-night effect. In 2016, Masako Tamaki, Yuka Sasaki and their colleagues published a study in Current Biology suggesting that the reason is partly that one hemisphere of the brain stays more watchful, as if standing guard in a strange place.

@char N19 autumn_overlook | A guard in the brain | A little watchfulness
In the first night, the left hemisphere's resting network showed a lighter sleep in deep stages, and responded faster to odd sounds played in the right ear. It is a lovely echo of what some sea mammals and birds do with half a brain at a time, in a much more modest form. It suggests that our brains are built to stay alert in unfamiliar places.

@char N09 bedroom_night | What this says about Nora | A guard on duty
It also helps to explain why a stressful week, a new bed, or an upcoming exam can make sleep more fragile. The brain is doing something that, by its own old logic, is sensible. It's standing guard. What it can't know is that the danger tonight is a pile of emails and a ceiling, and that it would be safe to stand down.

@chapter N20 cabin_fireplace | Placebo sleep
There's one more piece of this story, and it may be the most surprising. It's about what we believe about last night.

@study N11 cafe_sunset | Rebecca Draganich and Kristi Erdal | Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition, 2014 | Volunteers were given made-up feedback about how much of their sleep was REM sleep and then took tests of attention and memory
In 2014, two psychologists, Rebecca Draganich and Kristi Erdal, ran a study with a trick in it. Volunteers were hooked up to a device that supposedly measured their sleep. Afterwards, some were told that they'd had an above-average amount of REM sleep, and others a below-average amount. The feedback was random, and had nothing to do with their real sleep.

@stat N11 autumn_overlook | A belief about sleep | Test scores shifted | people told they had slept well scored better on an attention test than people told they had slept badly
Yet the volunteers who were told they'd slept well did better on tests of attention than those told they'd slept poorly, even though the feedback was invented. The researchers called it placebo sleep. A belief that you slept badly may, on its own, make you perform worse, and a belief that you slept well may help a little.

@char N10 living_room_day | A careful reading | Not an excuse to ignore sleep
Of course, this doesn't mean that real sleep loss doesn't matter. It does, and we know it from many experiments. But it does suggest that a night of poor sleep is not the disaster it feels like at four in the morning, and that the dread we attach to it, the certainty that tomorrow will be ruined, can itself add to the damage.

@char N02 rooftop_dusk | A calmer thought | A rough night is survivable
So here's a calmer way to think about it. Most people, after a rough night, can get through the next day. They feel groggy, they might be grumpy, and they sleep better the night after, because sleep pressure has grown. The system is more forgiving than our fear of it, and that thought alone can loosen the grip a little.

@char N10 bedroom_night | Back to Nora | A more accurate story
So Nora's morning story, I didn't sleep at all, may not be quite accurate. Perhaps she slept more than she thinks. Perhaps she rested more than she noticed. And perhaps the worst thing about the night wasn't the wakefulness itself, but the verdict she passed on it, which is exactly what we'll look at next.
