@chapter A12 living_room_day | The trap of feeling like you know it
Every study session ends with a quiet question, usually unspoken. Do I know this now? You close the book, you look at the page, and some inner gauge gives you a reading. Good, or not yet. And then, on that basis, you decide whether to carry on or to stop.

@char A04 attic_rain | The hidden problem | The gauge can be wrong
Psychologists call this gauge metacognition, which simply means thinking about your own thinking. It's one of the most useful tools a learner has. But the research is clear that the gauge is often miscalibrated, and that it errs in a predictable direction. It tends to say, I've got this, a little before it's true.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Rhodes & Castel | Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, 2008 | Memory predictions are influenced by perceptual information
Here is one of my favourite demonstrations. Matthew Rhodes and Alan Castel, at Colorado State University and UCLA, showed people a list of words on a screen. Some words appeared in a large font and some in a small one. For each word, participants were asked to predict how likely they were to remember it later.

@char A12 living_room_day | The prediction | Big words feel memorable
People predicted that the large words would be remembered better. It seems natural. Big things stand out. But when the researchers tested recall, the font size made no real difference. A word in a tiny font was remembered about as well as one in a huge font. The feeling of memorability had come from how the word looked, and not from anything that would help it stick.

@list A03 living_room_day | Cues that fool the gauge | A large, clear font ;; A familiar topic ;; A smooth, confident explanation ;; Having just read it a moment ago
This is the heart of the trap. Our feeling of knowing is built from cues, and many of those cues are about how easy something feels right now, not about how well it will be remembered next week. Ease and learning are not the same thing. Sometimes they point in opposite directions.

@chapter A13 rooftop_dusk | Why rereading feels so good
This also explains the rereading habit we met at the beginning. When you read a passage a second time, the words flow. You recognise the sentences before you reach the end of them. That fluency is real, and it creates a strong sense of mastery.

@char A04 attic_rain | The catch | Fluency is borrowed
But the fluency is borrowed from the page, which is still in front of you. A few minutes later, when the book is closed, you may find that the knowledge doesn't come without the cue. Robert Bjork has a phrase for this: illusions of competence. You feel competent while the information is in front of you, and then the competence evaporates.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Koriat & Bjork | Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition, 2005 | Illusions of competence in monitoring one's knowledge during study
Asher Koriat and Robert Bjork made this vivid with word pairs. When a pair was shown on the study screen, with both words visible, learners judged some of them as easy and likely to be remembered. Of course they felt easy, since the answer was right there. But on the later test, learners would only be given the first word, and would have to produce the second without seeing it. For some pairs, that was far harder than learners expected, because the feeling of ease had come from seeing both words together.

@photo lf20_b05_01.jpg | Easy to read, not always easy to recall
The lesson is a simple one, though not easy to follow. Judge your learning by what happens when the page is closed. If you can't produce it from memory, you can't yet count on it, no matter how familiar it looks.

@char A15 cabin_fireplace | A gentle thought | Be kind to the gauge
None of this means you're foolish. Everyone's gauge works this way. It's built from feelings, and feelings are quick and cheap. What we need is a second source of information, one that doesn't depend on mood. And that source is a test.

@chapter A12 living_room_day | The ones who know least
There's another famous pattern that belongs here, and it's one of the most quoted findings in all of psychology. In 1999, David Dunning and Justin Kruger at Cornell University published a paper with a memorable title: Unskilled and Unaware of It.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Kruger & Dunning | Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 1999 | Unskilled and unaware of it: how difficulties in recognizing one's own incompetence lead to inflated self-assessments
Students took tests of humour, logical reasoning and English grammar, and then estimated how well they had done compared with other people. The pattern was striking. The people who scored in the bottom quarter, on average, put themselves at around the sixty-second percentile. Their real score was around the twelfth.

@bars A12 living_room_day | Bottom quarter of scorers | percentile | What they scored:12 ;; What they thought they scored:62
The researchers' explanation was neat. The skills needed to do well at a task are often the same skills needed to judge how well you've done. If you don't know the grammar rules, you also can't spot the grammar mistakes in your own work. In other words, the gap in knowledge hides itself.

@char A04 attic_rain | A fair note | The doubts
It's only fair to add that this finding has been debated. Some statisticians argue that part of the pattern comes from a plain statistical effect, because when people's guesses are noisy, the lowest scorers will always seem to overestimate, and the highest will seem to underestimate. Later work suggests there is a real effect on top of that, but that it may be smaller than the popular version suggests. What holds up is the main point. Most of us are not very good at knowing what we don't know.

@list A03 living_room_day | What the research agrees on | People are poorly calibrated about their own knowledge ;; The error is usually overconfidence ;; Feedback and testing narrow the gap
That last line is the useful one. Calibration can be improved, and the way to improve it is with feedback. When you're forced to compare what you thought you knew with what you could really produce, your gauge learns.

@chapter A06 cafe_sunset | Learning to judge your learning
So how can you set your gauge straight? One of the most effective methods is surprisingly modest. It's called a delayed judgement of learning, and it was studied by Thomas Nelson and Louis Narens, and then by many others.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Nelson & Dunlosky | Psychological Science, 1991 | When people's judgments of learning are highly accurate predictors of subsequent recall: the delayed-JOL effect
Learners studied word pairs. In one condition they judged whether they'd remember a pair right after studying it. In the other they waited a few minutes, and then tried to recall the answer before judging. The delayed judgements were remarkably accurate, much better than the immediate ones. The few minutes of waiting changed what the memory could tell them.

@char A12 living_room_day | Why delay helps | You tested your own memory
Why? Because when you wait and then try to recall, you're actually running a small test. The answer either comes or it doesn't, and the gauge gets real information. Immediately after study, everything is still in your mind, and every item feels solid.

@char A01 cafe_sunset | A simple habit | Wait, then check
In practice, you can do the same. Finish a section, and look away. Pour a cup of tea, and write down what you can recall. Then compare it with the page. The things you missed are not a failure. They're a map of where your learning is thin, and they tell you what to do next.

@chapter A13 rooftop_dusk | When students choose what to study
There's one more place where the gauge matters, and that's in deciding what to spend your time on. Nate Kornell and Janet Metcalfe studied how students allocate their study time, and the results are quite revealing.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Metcalfe & Kornell | Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, 2003 | The dynamics of learning and allocation of study time to a region of proximal learning
Participants could choose which items to study, from easy ones to very difficult ones. People tended to spend their time on the items that were already close to being learned, those at the edge of their knowledge. Metcalfe called this the region of proximal learning. It is a good strategy, in many ways: it favours items where a little effort yields the most gain.

@char A04 attic_rain | The risk | Avoiding what's hard
But there's a catch. Ignoring the hardest items can be sensible when time is short, but it can become a habit even when time is plentiful. And if the gauge is overconfident, we put items down too early, believing we've learned them. The result is a pile of half-learned facts and a feeling of progress.

@list A03 living_room_day | Setting your gauge straight | Test yourself before you judge ;; Wait a little before you decide ;; Return to items you believe you know, because you may have forgotten them by tomorrow ;; Keep returning to the items that failed
This is simple, but it isn't easy. It asks you to trust the evidence over the feeling, and to be a little sceptical of the pleasant sense of having learned something.

@char A15 cabin_fireplace | A gentle thought | Humility helps
There's a kind of humility in this chapter that I find comforting. Not the sort that says, I'm not good enough. It's the quieter sort that says, I might not know this yet, let's find out. It turns learning into a conversation with the facts, rather than a performance for yourself.

@char A08 cabin_fireplace | Looking back | What the gauge taught us
So, what did we learn? That our sense of knowing runs on cues like ease and font size. That fluency, from rereading, can feel like mastery. That the least skilled tend to overrate themselves, with some fair doubts about the size of the effect. And that waiting, testing and feedback tune the gauge.
