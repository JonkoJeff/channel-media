@chapter N10 bedroom_night | The people we catch it from
Oskar's phone shows a message from his oldest friend, Dami, who wants to talk about the weekend. They switch to a video call. Within a few minutes Dami yawns, and Oskar catches it so fast that they both laugh. It feels to him as though a friend's yawn is harder to refuse than a stranger's. He may be onto something.

@char N01 living_room_day | Watching real life | No videotapes
Most of what we've heard so far came from videos and laboratories. Two researchers in Italy took a different route. Instead of showing people clips, they watched yawns happen in ordinary life, where the person yawning might be your sister, your colleague or somebody you've never met, and where nobody had been asked to think about yawning at all.

@study A03 cafe_sunset | Ivan Norscia and Elisabetta Palagi | PLoS ONE, published 2011 | A year of observing everyday yawns
Over about a year, Ivan Norscia and Elisabetta Palagi recorded yawns among one hundred and nine adults from several parts of the world. They analysed four hundred and eighty bouts of yawning in natural settings. Each time, they noted who yawned, who was near enough to see or hear it, whether that person yawned in turn, how quickly, and how the two were related.

@bars A10 cabin_fireplace | Average rate of catching, by closeness | percent | Acquaintances:39 ;; Friends:52 ;; Kin and partners:85
Closeness made the difference. On their measure, the average rate of catching was about thirty-nine percent between acquaintances, about fifty-two percent between friends, and about eighty-five percent between family members and partners. The social bond was the one factor in their analysis that significantly predicted whether a yawn was caught. Sex and country of origin didn't.

@char N09 autumn_overlook | Speed too | Slower for the distant
Timing told a similar story. Strangers and acquaintances tended to take longer to respond than people with closer ties. So a yawn from someone you love was both more likely to be caught and, in a sense, quicker to arrive. The authors read this as support for a link between yawn contagion and emotional closeness, perhaps through empathy.

@char N20 cafe_sunset | Other explanations | Attention again
Do you remember the lesson from the children? Before a yawn can be caught, it must be noticed. We tend to look more at the people we care about. We sit closer to them and read their faces with practised ease. So some of the closeness effect might come from extra attention. That doesn't make the finding less real, but it widens the possible reasons.

@photo lf54_b12_01.jpg | Two mugs, side by side
Two mugs standing close together on a kitchen table. Much of ordinary closeness is like this: sharing a room in the evening, half noticing one another's movements, falling into the same rhythms. It wouldn't be surprising if yawns travelled easily across a table like that, where each person is quietly keeping track of the other.

@study N01 cabin_fireplace | Axel Franzen, Sebastian Mader and Fabian Winter | Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, published 2018 | Yawning, empathy and helpfulness
Now for a different test. In work published in 2018, Axel Franzen, Sebastian Mader and Fabian Winter ran two laboratory studies with students, one with a hundred and seventy-one people and one with three hundred and thirty-three. Participants watched silent videos of people yawning, scratching or laughing, filled in an empathy questionnaire, and made choices that revealed how generous or helpful they were.

@stat A03 autumn_overlook | Students who caught yawns | Higher | empathy scores, by about half a standard deviation
Contagious yawning was related to empathy. Yawn catchers scored higher on the questionnaire, by about half a standard deviation, which is a moderate difference with plenty of overlap between the groups. Catching a scratch or a laugh didn't show the same link. So here the old idea received some support, specifically for yawns.

@char A10 rooftop_dusk | But did they behave differently? | The surprise
Then came the second half of the question. If catching yawns reflects empathy, are yawn catchers also kinder in their actual choices? In these studies the researchers found no evidence of that. People who caught yawns weren't shown to be more helpful or generous. Whatever the link with questionnaire scores means, it didn't carry through to how people behaved.

@char N09 living_room_day | And the Duke study | Another voice
Set that beside what we heard from Duke, where empathy scores no longer predicted contagious yawning once age was taken into account. Different groups, different measures and different analyses gave different answers. Science often looks like this in the middle of a question: not a clean verdict, but several careful studies that haven't yet agreed with one another.

@study N20 rooftop_dusk | Jorg Massen and Andrew Gallup | Neuroscience and Biobehavioral Reviews, published 2017 | Reviewing the empathy claim
In 2017, Jorg Massen and Andrew Gallup reviewed the evidence in an article whose title states their conclusion: contagious yawning does not yet equate to empathy. They pointed to inconsistent results across studies of humans and animals and argued that the popular link had been drawn more firmly than the data allowed. The word yet is important.

@list N10 living_room_day | For and against the empathy link | For: closer people caught more yawns ;; For: some questionnaire links ;; Against: other studies found no link ;; Against: no clear link to helpful behaviour
So let's put it on two sides. In favour: closeness predicted catching in everyday observation, and some studies found higher empathy scores among yawn catchers. Against: other studies didn't, attention could explain part of the pattern, and a link to helpful behaviour didn't appear. A fair summary is that it's unsettled, and any effect is probably modest.

@char N01 autumn_overlook | Please don't test your partner | A kind request
This is a good moment for a kind request. Please don't yawn at your partner to measure their love. If they don't yawn back, you've learned that they didn't yawn. They may have been looking elsewhere, wide awake, older than the average, or simply among the many people who aren't very catchable. Affection is far too large to fit in a six second test.

@char A03 cabin_fireplace | Dami says goodnight | The friend on the screen
Dami says he has to go, yawns once more on his way to the end of the call, and the screen goes dark. Oskar finds that he's smiling. Whatever the final scientific answer turns out to be, there was something companionable about those yawns passing back and forth across all that distance, as though they were still sitting on the same old sofa.

@list A10 cafe_sunset | Where we are | Everyday catching rose with closeness ;; A 2018 study linked catching to empathy scores ;; It found no link to helpful behaviour ;; Reviewers call the empathy claim unproven
In short: in daily life, yawns passed most readily among family and partners, then friends, then acquaintances. A pair of student studies linked catching with higher empathy scores but not with helpful behaviour. And a review concluded that the case for yawning as a sign of empathy hasn't yet been made.

@char N27 street_day | Next stop | When someone is watching
There's one more social twist, and it runs in a surprising direction. Closeness may make a yawn easier to catch, but the sense of being watched can do almost the opposite. At the next stop, we'll slip on a virtual reality headset and discover that who is really in the room can matter more than who appears to be.
