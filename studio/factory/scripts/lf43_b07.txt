@chapter A05 living_room_day | Many years later
Since then, other researchers have looked at the same question in other people. One of the most careful pictures comes from a long-running study in Britain that has followed many sets of twins born in England and Wales in the mid-1990s. Twins are a gift to scientists, because they share a family, a home, and, in the case of identical twins, their genes. So when the twins differ, you can ask what makes the difference.

@study A18 rooftop_dusk | Matthews and colleagues | Psychological Medicine, published 2017 | Lonelier young adults reported poorer sleep quality, even when comparing identical twins
When the twins were eighteen, researchers asked them about their loneliness and about their sleep. They used a standard questionnaire for each. Those who felt lonelier reported poorer sleep quality overall. And the link held when the scientists compared one identical twin with the other. So when two people with the same genes and the same upbringing differed in loneliness, the lonelier twin tended to report worse sleep.

@stat A13 cabin_fireplace | Young adults in the study | 2,066 | twins assessed at age 18
The group assessed at that age numbered 2,066 young people, and they came from every kind of background across Great Britain. That's a large and varied group, which is part of why the finding is taken seriously. The title the authors chose is a lovely one, and a little eerie. They called it sleeping with one eye open. @char A20 living_room_day | What the twins add | A cleaner comparison
Why do twins matter so much here? Because they quietly remove several explanations.

@quote A13 rooftop_dusk | Not every explanation can be shared by twins. | Why twins help
It can't simply be that the lonelier twin grew up in a noisier house, or inherited a more anxious temperament from the family, since those things are shared. Of course, no study removes everything. But when a link survives a comparison like this, it's less likely to be a coincidence, and more likely to be something real, worth looking at.

@char A09 attic_rain | An honest limit | They asked once
There was an important limit, and the researchers said so themselves. The twins were asked about their loneliness and their sleep at the same time, and by self-report. So the study can't tell us which came first. It describes a link, and not a cause. It also found the connection was stronger among young people who had been through hard experiences in their past, such as violence or mistreatment. That's a reminder that loneliness never arrives in the same way for everyone.

@list A19 bedroom_night | What the two studies suggest | Lonelier people may sleep less restfully ;; The link showed up in students and in a large group of young adults ;; It held between identical twins ;; Cause and direction are still open
Let's put the two pieces together. In one study, lonelier students had more broken-up nights, despite similar hours. In another, lonelier young adults reported poorer sleep quality, even when compared with their identical twin. In both cases, the finding is a link. It would be too much to say loneliness is the single cause of poor sleep. But it would also be too little to say that it only touches our mood.
