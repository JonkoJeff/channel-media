It is evening again, and Tomas has the laptop open on the sofa. He is not looking for anything in particular. He moves from one feed to another, a photo here, a joke there, someone's holiday, someone's new job. An hour goes by quite quietly. When he finally looks up, the room is darker, and he has the feeling, as you may have had, that he has been with people without being with anybody.

@quote A10 cabin_fireplace | Social media is why we are lonely. | A very popular explanation
That is our next myth, and it is easy to see why it is so popular. It is simple. It has a villain. And it fits an experience many of us recognise, the hollow feeling after a long scroll. Whenever something simple fits a familiar feeling, we tend to accept it quickly. So let's look at it slowly, and see what the evidence says.

@char A13 rooftop_dusk | Why it feels so true | Highlights and hours
There are good reasons it feels true. We see other people's best moments, polished and cheerful, while we sit in our own ordinary evening. We lose hours without noticing. And a screen can sit in the place where a conversation might have been. None of those worries is silly. They are worth taking seriously, and many researchers have.

@char A18 autumn_overlook | The honest answer | It is tangled
Here is the honest answer, and it is less dramatic than the headlines. The evidence is mixed. Some studies find links between heavy use and feeling lonelier. Others find little. Others find that connecting online helps people. Researchers do not all agree, and a lot depends on the person, the platform and, above all, what someone actually does with it.

@list A19 cabin_fireplace | Two tidy stories, both too simple | Screens make us lonely ;; Screens connect us ;; Both can be true, for different people ;; It depends on how they are used
It may help to put the two tidy stories side by side. One says that screens make us lonely. The other says that screens connect us. Each has some truth in it, and each leaves out a great deal. Both can be true, for different people, on different evenings. In the end, the more useful question may be how a screen is used.

@chapter A12 cafe_sunset | A real experiment
Surveys cannot easily settle this, because they have a puzzle built in. If heavy users are lonelier, does the screen cause the loneliness, or does the loneliness send people to the screen? To untangle that, we need an experiment, where people are assigned to different habits and then compared.

@char A05 street_day | Why experiments help | Changing one thing
An experiment works by changing one thing on purpose. If two similar groups are treated differently, and only one of them changes, then differences afterward point toward that change. It is not perfect, and no single study is final. But it can tell us something a survey cannot, which is why this next study is worth going through carefully.

@study A12 living_room_day | Hunt, Marx, Lipson and Young | Journal of Social and Clinical Psychology, published 2018 | Students who limited social media for three weeks reported less loneliness and less depression
Melissa Hunt and her team at the University of Pennsylvania set up such an experiment. Some students kept using social media as usual. Others limited it for three weeks. When the three weeks ended, the students who had limited it reported significantly less loneliness and less depression than before. That is a neat, clear result, and it deserves a careful look.

@stat A15 cafe_sunset | Students in the experiment | 143 | undergraduates, assigned at random
A hundred and forty-three undergraduates joined the experiment. They were assigned at random to one of two groups. Random assignment is what gives an experiment its strength, because it makes the groups similar to begin with. One group carried on as before. The other had a gentle limit, and the researchers tracked how it went, using the phones themselves.

@list A10 cabin_fireplace | How the study worked | One week of normal use to start ;; Then three weeks of the experiment ;; Limit: 10 minutes a day on each of Facebook, Instagram and Snapchat ;; Control group: usual habits
Here is how it was set up. First, a week of normal use, to see where everybody began. Then three weeks of the experiment. The limited group was allowed ten minutes a day on each of three platforms, Facebook, Instagram and Snapchat, which is about thirty minutes in all. The other group kept their usual habits. Then everyone was measured again.

@char A19 attic_rain | What changed | Lighter, not cured
What did the researchers find? Among the students who had limited their use, loneliness and depression both went down significantly. It was not a miracle, and nobody claimed it was. But it was a real change, in the direction that the simple story would predict. And it came from an experiment, which makes it harder to put down to chance or to the order of events.

@char A03 living_room_day | Reading it carefully | What it does not say
It is good to be fair about what this does not say. The students were undergraduates, a particular group of young adults. The study looked at three platforms only. It did not find the perfect number of minutes. And the researchers did not suggest that anyone should quit. So it is a clear piece of evidence, but it is one piece, not the whole puzzle.

@chapter A18 rooftop_dusk | How we use it matters
So where does that leave the big question? Probably with a more interesting one. Not whether we use these tools, but how, and what happens in us while we do.

@char A12 street_day | Two ways to scroll | Watching and talking
There is a difference that many people feel without having a name for it. Some time online is watching, moving through other people's lives. Some time online is talking, with a real person, about something real. The first can leave us emptier. The second can leave us warmer. This is a plain observation, not a finding, but many of us will recognise it.

@char A15 cafe_sunset | Tomas's messages | Dozens, and still quiet
Think of Tomas at the very start, with dozens of messages sent and no real conversation. Nothing was wrong with sending them. They were small, friendly and kind. But they were quick, and nobody had to stay. A messaging app can carry a real conversation, or it can carry a hundred tiny ones that never become one. It is the same tool, used in two ways.

@char A05 autumn_overlook | An old habit | New tools, same wish
It also helps to remember that this is not a new wish. People have always used whatever tools they had to reach each other, letters, telephones, notes passed across a table. The wish to be in touch is very old. Only the tools change. So when we use a screen to reach for someone, we are doing something deeply human, in a very modern way.

@list A13 rooftop_dusk | Questions to ask yourself | After I scroll, do I feel closer or further away? ;; Was I talking with someone, or only watching? ;; Did it lead toward a real moment? ;; Do I feel a little better afterwards?
Here are four gentle questions you might ask yourself. They are not from the study, and they are not a test. After I scroll, do I feel closer or further away? Was I talking with someone, or only watching? Did it lead toward a real moment? Do I feel a little better afterwards? There are no wrong answers, only information, quietly offered.

@char A10 bedroom_night | When we are lonely | A natural reach
A kind thought is in order here. When we are lonely, it is natural to reach for the screen. It is right there, it is easy, and it offers company of a sort. Nobody should feel ashamed of that. Reaching out is the correct instinct. It may just be worth noticing which kind of reaching gets us the warmth we are really after.

@char A15 street_day | A kinder reading | A mixed picture is still useful
A mixed picture can feel unsatisfying, as if the science has shrugged. But it is actually quite useful. It tells us that the simple stories are too simple, and that we are free to pay attention to our own experience. If one person feels worse after a long scroll, and another feels warmer after a long chat, both are telling the truth.

@quote A03 cabin_fireplace | Not whether, but how. | A gentle way to remember it
This is only a small saying, and not from any study. But it is a comfortable thing to carry. Our phones are neither the villain nor the rescuer. They are a tool, and tools do what they are used for. And that gives us a little room to choose, which is a lovely thing to have on a slow evening.

@char A02 living_room_day | What it means for you | A small experiment
So what might this mean for you? Perhaps that you do not need to switch everything off. Perhaps just that you can be curious, and try small things, like a quiet hour, or one message that becomes a real conversation. This is not advice about your health. It is only an invitation to notice how different habits feel, and let that guide you.

@list A19 attic_rain | What to take from the screen question | The evidence on social media is mixed ;; In one experiment, limiting it eased loneliness ;; How we use it matters ;; Reaching out is never wrong
Let's gather four small thoughts. The evidence on social media is mixed. In one three-week experiment with students, limiting it eased loneliness and low mood. How we use it seems to matter more than whether we do. And reaching out, in any form, is never wrong. That is a fair and kind place to leave the question tonight.

Tomas closes the laptop, gently, the way you might close a book. The room is quiet, and for once the quiet does not feel like an accusation. He thinks about the students, and the three weeks, and the thirty minutes. He is not sure he will try it. But he is a little more curious about his evenings than he was an hour ago.

@char A03 cabin_fireplace | A bigger thought | Is this who I am
And then a bigger thought drifts in, the kind that arrives when a room is calm. If he feels this way, night after night, is it simply who he is? Is loneliness a piece of his character, like his height or his handwriting, or is it something that comes, and stays for a while, and one day passes? That is a very important question, and it deserves a quiet look.
