@chapter A13 living_room_day | Where did that detail come from?
Where did that detail come from? A detail can feel familiar while its route into your mind remains less clear. Did someone tell you? Did you read it somewhere? Or did you picture it yourself while thinking about what might happen?

@char A19 bedroom_night | A familiar detail | An uncertain beginning
Here is a harmless little puzzle. You are getting ready to meet a friend at a garden, and you have a yellow gate in mind. The gate seems like something you already know about. But now you wonder whether your friend mentioned its color, whether a message described it, or whether yellow was simply how you pictured the entrance.

@list A03 living_room_day | The same detail, different beginnings | Heard in conversation ;; Read in a message ;; Imagined while planning
Keep those possibilities separate for a moment. Each route could leave you thinking about a yellow gate. Yet they would support different answers to the question of where that detail came from. We will return to the gate shortly, because there is a small distinction here that is easy to glide past.

@list A10 living_room_day | Source-monitoring review | Johnson, Hashtroudi, and Lindsay ;; Psychological Bulletin, 1993 ;; Source-monitoring framework
In a review published in 1993, Marcia Johnson, Shahin Hashtroudi, and Stephen Lindsay described a framework for source monitoring: judgments about the origins of memories, knowledge, and beliefs. Their paper brought together theory and evidence. It was not one experiment in which everyone tried to remember the same yellow gate.

@char A05 living_room_day | Remembering what | Remembering where it came from
Let us give those questions their ordinary names. What is the detail? A yellow gate. Where did it come from? That remains open. You could answer the first question immediately and still hesitate over the second. Knowing what you are thinking about does not, by itself, answer how you came to think it.

Picture yourself sitting at a table with that little question. There is no urgency, and nothing serious depends on the answer. You are simply trying to follow a detail backward. Instead of asking whether yellow feels right for a gate, you ask what, if anything, you remember about receiving that particular information.

@list A18 living_room_day | Questions for the imagined situation | A voice? ;; A written message? ;; A picture you formed?
Perhaps you would look for the sound of your friend's voice. Perhaps you would try to picture the message on your screen. Or perhaps you would remember choosing yellow while imagining the garden. These are possible clues in our invented situation, rather than a test you need to perform successfully while listening.

@char A03 living_room_day | What might count as a clue?
The framework proposes that source judgments draw on features of experience, including perceptual details, context, and traces of thinking. It also allows for judgments that are relatively quick or more deliberate. That gives us a way to describe the gate puzzle without assuming that every familiar detail arrives with an unmistakable label.

@char A13 living_room_day | A voice in the example | What was actually said?
Let us try the conversation possibility first. You might remember your friend speaking while you were washing a cup. You can picture the kitchen and the phone resting nearby. Now ask a narrower question: in the scene you are imagining, do you remember the words about the gate, or only the conversation surrounding the visit?

@list A10 living_room_day | Keep the question narrow | The conversation ;; The garden ;; The gate's color
Those would be different answers. Remembering a conversation about the garden would establish less than remembering the sentence about its entrance. There is no need to fill the gap. You could leave the answer at, “I remember discussing the visit, but I am unsure whether yellow was mentioned.”

@char A01 living_room_day | A modest answer | Enough for this moment
Notice how undramatic that answer can be. You have not declared the whole conversation imaginary. You have not decided your friend never described the gate. You have simply kept one uncertain detail from becoming a more definite claim. That is all the question asks of you.

@chapter A19 bedroom_night | The written possibility
Now let the imagined scene shift from a conversation to a message. Perhaps you can picture your friend's name at the top of a screen and a few lines about meeting at the garden. Would remembering that a message existed settle whether it contained the color? We still need the more specific connection.

@char A18 living_room_day | A message about the visit | A message naming the color
You open the message and find directions to the entrance, but no color at all. That would answer something about this particular record. It would not tell you everything you had ever heard about the garden. The distinction is between the evidence in front of you and the rest of the story.

@list A05 living_room_day | What the imagined message could establish | Directions were written ;; The entrance was mentioned ;; Yellow may remain unresolved
You might discover that part of your recollection fits the message quite well. There really were directions. There really was an entrance to look for. Yet the yellow could remain unexplained. Our imaginary puzzle allows a mixed answer: some details have a visible source, while another detail still needs its own explanation.

@char A10 living_room_day | Let one detail stay open
There is room for that kind of unfinished answer. You could say, “The message explains why I expected a gate. I still do not know why I expected a yellow one.” Spoken slowly, it sounds less like a failure and more like an accurate description of what the available information does and does not establish.

@list A03 living_room_day | Source-monitoring review | Johnson and colleagues ;; Psychological Bulletin, 1993 ;; Features can overlap across possible sources
The framework treats similarities between possible sources, and the criteria used to judge them, as relevant to source attribution. A feature can be informative without being exclusive to one origin. Picturing the gate would not settle whether you had read about it or formed that picture while planning.

@char A13 living_room_day | A picture with an open question
Suppose the image in your mind has a curved top and pale yellow paint. Before giving that picture a history, pause at what the example actually provides: an image you can describe. Whether it matches something seen, something described, or something invented is another question. Describing the picture more carefully would not automatically settle its origin.

And now there is an actual illustration on the screen. It gives this telling of the gate a visible form. Within our story, however, that illustration cannot establish what your imaginary friend said earlier. It belongs to the explanation you are hearing now. Keeping that distinction clear is the very puzzle we are exploring.

@list A01 living_room_day | The example has layers | The original possibility ;; Your imagined scene ;; This illustration
You do not need to memorize those layers. Just notice that the question “Which gate?” differs from “Where did this version of the gate enter the story?” One asks about the object being described. The other asks about the path by which a particular description became available.

@list A19 bedroom_night | Content and origin | What detail is available? ;; How did it enter the situation?
We can now resolve the question we left open. Retaining the content would mean having the yellow gate available to think about. Identifying its source would mean deciding how that detail entered the situation. The example permits the first without giving us enough information to settle the second.

@char A03 living_room_day | Familiar does not finish the sentence
Try putting the distinction into an ordinary sentence: “Yellow feels familiar, but I cannot place where I got it.” That sentence is complete. It does not require an immediate guess at the missing source. You could carry that uncertainty with you until a message, a conversation, or the entrance itself supplied more information.

@list A18 living_room_day | Possible answers | I remember being told ;; I remember reading it ;; I cannot place it
The last answer deserves a place beside the others. Here, it would be more precise than selecting a source simply because the story sounds tidy. You could still meet your friend, enjoy the garden, and let this minor uncertainty remain minor. There is no need to turn a yellow gate into a grand verdict about memory.

@chapter A10 living_room_day | When the detail begins with you
There is another route we have not followed closely yet. Suppose nobody had mentioned a color at all. While planning the visit, you might have pictured an entrance and chosen yellow for the scene. In that version of the example, the detail would begin with an act of imagination, rather than a description received from someone else.

@char A05 living_room_day | Making a scene in your mind
Try building that scene deliberately. You place the gate beside a gravel path. You decide that it opens inward. Perhaps you add a shrub because the entrance looks a little bare without one. These are instructions for our fictional scene. They illustrate what it would mean to generate details, without claiming that you would later mistake them for something witnessed.

@list A13 living_room_day | Different questions about the scene | Can you picture it? ;; Do you think it happened? ;; Do you remember experiencing it?
Keep those questions apart as we approach the next piece of research. Being able to picture the gate would be one thing. Believing that a particular gate existed would be another. Saying you remembered encountering it would make a different claim again. Our illustration has not made those claims equivalent.

@char A19 bedroom_night | An imagined detail | Its origin still matters
If you remembered choosing the color while making up the scene, you would have a straightforward answer about its origin. “I supplied that myself.” The imagined gate could remain detailed and pleasant. There would be no contradiction between describing it clearly and knowing that you had constructed it for the exercise.

@list A03 living_room_day | Leave the possibilities distinct | Heard ;; Read ;; Imagined
So the gentle question we began with has become more precise. We are no longer asking only whether a detail is available or familiar. We are asking what supports an account of its beginning. For the yellow gate, the answer depends on which version of the story we actually have evidence for.

@char A18 living_room_day | Following imagination a little further
That brings us naturally to internally generated information. Our gate has been a hypothetical example, useful for separating questions. The next step is to look at an experiment that asked about imagination and later judgments. To understand it, we will need to watch carefully what participants rated, rather than assume every change in a rating meant a new remembered experience.
