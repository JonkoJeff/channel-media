@chapter A15 autumn_overlook | A sense of control
Let's stay with that idea for a while, because it sits underneath almost everything we've talked about tonight. Reactance is what happens when we lose a sense of control. So what happens when we're given a little more of it?

@char A10 living_room_day | The remote control | Small choices, big feelings
Think about how it feels when someone else holds the remote. You might not even mind what's on. But there's a faint restlessness, a sense that the evening isn't quite yours. Then someone hands it to you, and you end up watching the very same programme, but somehow it feels better.

@char A12 cafe_sunset | A question worth testing | Does control change us?
Is that just a passing feeling? Or can a sense of control actually change how people feel, how they act, maybe even how well they do over time? In the 1970s, two psychologists tested that question in a place where control is often quietly taken away.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Langer & Rodin | Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 1976 | The effects of choice and enhanced personal responsibility for the aged: a field experiment in an institutional setting
In 1976, Ellen Langer and Judith Rodin published a study from a nursing home in Connecticut. On one floor, residents heard a talk from the staff about how much responsibility they had for their own lives. They were invited to choose a plant and to look after it themselves, and to decide which evening they'd like to watch a film.

@char A03 attic_rain | The comparison floor | Same kindness, less choice
On another floor, residents heard a warm talk too, but this one stressed that the staff would take care of them. They were also given a plant, but the staff would water it. They were told which evening the film would be. Everything was kind. The only real difference was who was in charge of the small decisions.

@char A12 living_room_day | What changed | More alert, more active
Within a few weeks, the residents who'd been given responsibility were rated as happier, more alert and more active. They took part in more activities and spent more time talking with others. Watering a plant and choosing a film night are tiny things. But they seemed to carry a much bigger message. Your choices still count here.

@list A13 attic_rain | Eighteen months later | Still higher well-being ;; Fewer deaths in that time ;; Small groups, so held carefully
Eighteen months later, the researchers returned. In a follow-up published in 1977, they reported that the group given more responsibility still looked better on several measures of well-being. And fewer of them had died in that time, roughly half as many as in the comparison group. Remember, the main difference between the floors had been a small sense of being in charge of a few things.

@char A19 cabin_fireplace | Holding it carefully | A small study, a big claim
That last finding is often quoted, and it's worth holding carefully. The groups were small, and a result like that can't tell us exactly what caused it. Still, the overall pattern, that a little more control went along with more life and energy, has stayed with psychology ever since.

@study A04 bedroom_night | Richard Schulz | Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 1976 | Effects of control and predictability on the physical and psychological well-being of the institutionalized aged
Around the same time, Richard Schulz arranged for students to visit older residents of a retirement home. Some residents could decide when the visits happened and how long they lasted. Others were simply visited. Those who had control over the visits, or at least knew when they were coming, showed clear gains in well-being.

@char A10 rooftop_dusk | The sad part | When control is taken back
But a later follow-up, published in 1978 with Barbara Hanusa, found something sobering. After the visits ended, the residents who had enjoyed that control declined. Giving control matters. Taking it away again can hurt. It's reactance theory in its most tender form.

@list A10 living_room_day | The noise experiments | Loud, unpredictable bursts of noise ;; A button that could stop it ;; A request to press it only if needed
And sometimes, control doesn't even have to be used to help. In experiments described in their 1972 book Urban Stress, David Glass and Jerome Singer had people work on tasks while bursts of loud, unpredictable noise played, the kind of noise that makes it hard to think. Some were shown a button they could press to stop the noise, though they were encouraged not to press it unless they really needed to.

@char A18 cafe_sunset | The unpressed button | Knowing you could
Most of them never pressed it. Yet afterwards, they did better on puzzles and showed more patience than people who'd had no button at all. Simply knowing they could stop the noise seemed to soften its effect. Feeling free, it turns out, can matter almost as much as acting on that freedom.

@chapter A13 cabin_fireplace | Can there be too much choice?
So if a little control is good, is more choice always better? Here the story takes an interesting turn.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Iyengar & Lepper | Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 2000 | When choice is demotivating: can one desire too much of a good thing?
In 2000, Sheena Iyengar and Mark Lepper published a set of studies on choice. In one, university students were offered the chance to write an optional essay for extra credit. Some were given six topics to choose from. Others were given thirty.

@bars A12 living_room_day | Students who wrote the extra essay | % | 6 topics:74 ;; 30 topics:60
More students wrote the essay when they had six topics than when they had thirty. And the essays written from the shorter list were judged to be better, too. In another study, people who chose a chocolate from a small selection were more satisfied with their choice than people who picked from a large one.

@char A15 cafe_sunset | Choice overload | Too many doors
The researchers called this choice overload, and the idea quickly became famous far beyond psychology. Too many options, and the act of choosing becomes heavy. You worry about picking wrong. You wonder about the ones you didn't pick. Sometimes, you simply walk away.

@study A04 attic_rain | Scheibehenne, Greifeneder & Todd | Journal of Consumer Research, 2010 | Can there ever be too many options? A meta-analytic review of choice overload
But science likes to double-check its famous findings. In 2010, Benjamin Scheibehenne, Rainer Greifeneder and Peter Todd combined the results of dozens of choice overload experiments. On average, the effect of having more options was close to zero.

@char A03 autumn_overlook | It depends | Sometimes, not always
That doesn't mean choice overload never happens. Across the studies, the results varied widely. Sometimes more choice helped, sometimes it hurt, and often it made little difference. The effect seems to show up under particular conditions, for instance when people don't know what they prefer, or when options are hard to compare.

@list A18 living_room_day | What a sense of control really needs | A real say in what matters ;; A few meaningful options ;; Knowing what to expect ;; Not having it taken away again
So the lesson isn't more choice, always. It's a real say. A few meaningful options rather than an endless list. Knowing what's coming. And not having that control quietly taken back once it's been given.

@photo lf23_b13_01.jpg | A small thing to care for
Sometimes it really is as small as a plant on a windowsill. Something that's yours to look after, on your own schedule, in your own way. Not a grand freedom. Just a small, steady reminder that some part of the day still answers to you.

@char A01 cafe_sunset | Back to reactance | Two sides of one need
And this is where it all connects. Reactance and the good feeling of control are two sides of the same need. Take choice away, and people push back. Give a little of it back, and something in them relaxes and even grows.

@char A20 street_day | The everyday lesson | Offer, don't take
You can see it in tiny daily moments. Would you like to do the dishes now, or after this episode? It's the same dishes. But one sentence takes the evening away, and the other hands a small piece of it back.

@char A13 rooftop_dusk | Persuading without pushing | A different kind of influence
Which brings us to something rather hopeful. If people resist pressure so naturally, and relax when they feel free, then perhaps the best way to influence someone is to remind them, quite honestly, that they are free. It sounds too simple to work. But researchers have tested it.
