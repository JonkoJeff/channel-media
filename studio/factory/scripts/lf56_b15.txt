Halfway down her street, Nadia passes the house of her neighbours, a family who moved here from Spain a few years ago. Through the half-open window she can hear the end of a phone call, and then a burst of laughter. She doesn't understand the words. But she understands the laugh completely. It's the one part of the conversation that needs no translation.

@char N20 attic_rain | One sound, many languages | Laughter needs no dictionary
We've already met the research showing that laughter is recognised across very different cultures. But it's worth pausing on how remarkable that is. There are thousands of human languages, and most of us can understand only one or two. Yet a laugh from almost anywhere in the world is instantly recognisable to almost anyone, as if it came from a much older language we all still share.

@list A12 cafe_sunset | How people write a laugh | English: ha ha ;; Spanish: ja ja ;; Thai: 555, because five is said ha ;; Japanese: www, from the word for laugh ;; Korean: kkk
You can see this, in a playful way, in how people write their laughter in messages. English speakers type ha ha. Spanish speakers type ja ja, because the letter j makes that sound in Spanish. Thai speakers often type the number five, five, five, because the word for five is pronounced ha. Japanese speakers often type a row of w's, from the first letter of the word for laugh, and Korean speakers type a row of k's.

@char N29 living_room_day | Same laugh, different spelling | A shared sound
Each language spells it differently, but they're all trying to write down the same sound. The breathy, chopped-up out-breath we met at the very start of the evening. Nadia's group chat, tonight, was full of laughing faces, which is perhaps the most universal spelling of all. A little picture of a face doing what every human face knows how to do.

@study A03 rooftop_dusk | Michael Makagon, Sumie Funayama and Michael Owren | Journal of the Acoustical Society of America, 2008 | An acoustic analysis of laughter produced by congenitally deaf and normally hearing college students
Here's a question that tells us something about how deep laughter goes. Do we learn to laugh by hearing other people? In 2008, Michael Makagon, Sumie Funayama and Michael Owren recorded the laughter of college students who had been deaf from birth, and who had never heard a laugh, and compared it with the laughter of hearing students, while both groups watched funny videos.

@char A15 autumn_overlook | Laughing without ever hearing a laugh | It's built in
The deaf students laughed, and their laughter shared many of the basic features of the hearing students' laughter: the rhythmic bursts, the breathing pattern, the general shape. There were some differences in the details of the sound. But the core of laughter was clearly there. That strongly suggests that laughing isn't something we copy from others. It's something we're born ready to do.

@char A13 living_room_day | Why that matters | Old, deep and shared
That fits with everything else we've heard tonight. Babies laugh before they speak. Apes make laugh-like sounds in play. People around the world recognise each other's laughter. And people who have never heard a laugh still laugh. Laughter seems to be part of the basic equipment of being human, something we arrive with rather than something we're taught.

@study A10 autumn_overlook | Guillaume Dezecache and Robin Dunbar | Evolution and Human Behavior, 2012 | Sharing the joke: the size of natural laughter groups
Another study looked at a simple question: how many people laugh together at once? In 2012, Guillaume Dezecache and Robin Dunbar watched groups of people in natural settings, like bars and public spaces, and counted how many were laughing together at the same moment. They wanted to know whether laughter groups had a natural size.

@char N27 rooftop_dusk | Laughter groups are small | A handful at a time
They found that natural laughter groups tend to be small, usually just a handful of people, and rarely much larger than the size of an ordinary conversation group. Dunbar has suggested that laughter may work a bit like a long-distance version of grooming in other primates, a way of bonding with several people at once. But even so, it works best close up, with just a few.

@char N09 autumn_overlook | Like Nadia's table | Five was about right
Nadia's table tonight was right at that kind of size. Five old friends, close enough to see each other's faces and catch each other's eyes. Big enough for the laughter to bounce around the table. Small enough for everyone to be part of it. That may be one reason why so many of our best laughs happen around a dinner table, a kitchen counter or a car with friends.

@study N14 conspiracy_board | René Proyer, Willibald Ruch and colleagues | Humor: International Journal of Humor Research, 2009 | Breaking ground in cross-cultural research on the fear of being laughed at (gelotophobia): a multi-national study involving 73 countries
Of course, laughter can be painful too. Some people have a strong fear of being laughed at, which researchers call gelotophobia. In 2009, René Proyer, Willibald Ruch and a large team published a study using questionnaires in seventy-three countries, and found that this fear exists in every culture they looked at, though how common it is varies from place to place.

@char A19 rooftop_dusk | Laughed at, not with | The other side of laughter
That's a reminder of something we touched on earlier. The same sound that bonds a group together can also be used to shut someone out. Being laughed at, especially when we're young, can leave a lasting sting. People who fear being laughed at often hear mockery even in friendly laughter, and may avoid situations where laughter is likely.

@char N20 autumn_overlook | A kinder kind of laughter | Bringing people in
It's one more reason why the difference between laughing with and laughing at matters so much. The laughter at Nadia's table brought everyone in, including the couple at the next table. That's the kind of laughter that seems to do us good. If we can, it's worth trying to make sure our laughter is the kind that brings people in, rather than leaving them out.

@list N01 cafe_sunset | Laughter around the world | Recognised across cultures ;; Written in many ways ;; Present even without hearing ;; Best in small groups ;; Can include or exclude
So, putting it together. Laughter is recognised across cultures. It's written in many playful ways, but it's the same sound underneath. It appears even in people who have never heard it. It tends to happen in small groups, a few people at a time. And it can bring people in or push them out, depending on how we use it.
