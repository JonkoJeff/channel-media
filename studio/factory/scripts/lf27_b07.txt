@chapter A12 living_room_day | Helping around the world
So far, most of our experiments have been run in a few countries, and a good deal of them with students. It's natural to ask whether the findings hold elsewhere. Do people in different parts of the world help strangers equally often? Or does culture change the answer?

@char A04 attic_rain | The project | Twenty-three cities
In the late 1990s, a social psychologist, Robert Levine, and his colleagues set out to find out. They sent trained research assistants into the central districts of twenty-three large cities, in twenty-three countries, to stage three small scenes in which a stranger needed help. In each case the researchers recorded whether passers-by stepped in.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Levine, Norenzayan & Philbrick | Journal of Cross-Cultural Psychology, 2001 | Cross-cultural differences in helping strangers
The three scenes were simple. In one, a person walking along the street dropped a pen without noticing. In another, a person with a visible leg injury and a brace dropped a pile of magazines and struggled to pick them up. In the third, a person with dark glasses and a cane stood at a crossing, apparently waiting for help to cross the road.

@list A03 living_room_day | The three scenes | A dropped pen the owner did not notice ;; A hurt person struggling with magazines ;; A blind person waiting at a crossing ;; The same procedure in every city
Using the same procedure in every city allowed a fair comparison. The research assistants were trained to behave in the same way, and the situations were chosen to be understandable anywhere. The researchers then counted how many of the passers-by helped in each case.

@char A12 living_room_day | The results | Big differences between cities
The differences between cities were large. In the highest-ranking city, Rio de Janeiro, people helped most often across the three situations. In the lowest-ranking city, Kuala Lumpur, they helped least often. Most of the cities fell somewhere between, and the order of the rankings was less important than the size of the gaps.

@chapter A13 rooftop_dusk | What explained the differences?
The researchers looked for patterns. They noted that helping seemed to be higher in places with a strong tradition of what Spanish speakers call simpatía, a social ideal of being friendly, agreeable and considerate in everyday interactions.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Triandis, Marín, Lisansky & Betancourt | Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 1984 | Simpatía as a cultural script of Hispanics
Harry Triandis and his colleagues described simpatía as a cultural script that values pleasant, respectful and positive relations, and that discourages conflict. They argued that this script shapes how people behave towards others in many everyday settings. It offered one way to understand why helping would be common in some places.

@char A01 cafe_sunset | A caution | Cities are not countries
It's important to be careful here. The study sampled one large city per country, and the cities do not stand for whole nations. The results show how people in a central district responded on a particular day. They do not show that one country is kinder than another, and nobody should treat the rankings as national character.

@list A12 living_room_day | Why we should be careful with rankings | One city represents one country ;; Small samples in each city ;; Different local norms about strangers ;; Helping a stranger is only one kind of kindness
The last point is important. A culture in which strangers are not expected to help may be one in which family and neighbours help intensely. The experiments measured a particular kind of generosity, towards unknown people in public, and that is only one part of a community's caring.

@chapter A06 cafe_sunset | Local norms
Psychologists have emphasised that norms, the unwritten rules about what is expected, differ across places. In some communities it is natural to ask a passer-by for directions, or to help carry a pram upstairs. In others, strangers keep their distance, and offering help might feel intrusive.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Cialdini, Reno & Kallgren | Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 1990 | A focus theory of normative conduct: recycling the concept of norms to reduce littering in public places
Robert Cialdini and his colleagues showed in a series of field experiments that people's behaviour in public places is shaped by what they see others doing. In places where there was already litter, people littered more, and drawing attention to the norm made the effect stronger. The research suggests that visible behaviour sets the tone for what others do.

@char A04 attic_rain | The link to helping | Visible kindness spreads
If visible behaviour sets the tone, then visible helping may do the same. When people see someone else helping, they may be more likely to help themselves. There is experimental evidence of this kind, including a classic field study in which passers-by were more likely to stop after seeing someone else help a stranded motorist.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Bryan & Test | Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 1967 | Models and helping: naturalistic studies in aiding behavior
James Bryan and Mary Ann Test arranged for a woman with a car and a flat tyre to wait by the roadside in Los Angeles. On some occasions, drivers first passed another car on which a man was seen helping a woman with her tyre. Those drivers were more likely to stop to help the second woman than drivers who had not seen the model. Seeing kindness made kindness more likely.

@list A03 living_room_day | Words to keep from this chapter | Cross-cultural research ;; Simpatía ;; Social norm ;; Modelling
Four terms from this chapter. Cross-cultural research compares people in different societies. Simpatía is a cultural ideal of warmth and agreeableness. A social norm is an unwritten rule about how to behave. And modelling is learning from watching what others do.

@list A12 living_room_day | Small ways to make kindness visible | Say thank you out loud to people who help ;; Hold the door and let others see it ;; Offer help openly rather than quietly ;; Tell a child about a time someone helped you
Here are some ways to make kindness visible. Say thank you out loud to people who help. Hold the door where others can see it. Offer help openly, and not always quietly. Tell a child about a time someone helped you. Picture this scene: a bus driver waits for a woman running to the stop and says, no rush. The passengers watch. A few minutes later, a teenager gives up a seat to a man with a stick. Nobody planned it. A small visible kindness had changed what felt normal on that bus, and the research says that this is exactly how norms work.

@list A12 living_room_day | Pause and reflect | What are the unwritten rules of kindness in my community? ;; Who sets the tone? ;; What visible kindness have I seen lately? ;; What could I make visible?
Take a pause to think about the unwritten rules of kindness where you live. In some places, greeting a stranger is normal. In others, it is odd. Every neighbourhood, workplace and family has norms of this kind, and few of them are written anywhere. They are set by what people see others do. Who sets the tone in your community? It might be a shopkeeper who always asks after people, a teacher, or a particular neighbour. Think about the last time you saw a small act of kindness in public. How did it make you feel? The studies suggest it probably made you slightly more likely to do something kind yourself. Is there something you could make visible? Not for praise, but because visible kindness is how norms are built. A greeting, a held door, or a thank you said out loud costs almost nothing, and it can travel further than you will ever know.

@photo lf27_b07_01.jpg | Pins on a map
A map with pins in many cities. The project that sent researchers to twenty-three places was an effort to see helping on a global scale, and it showed that the answer to whether a stranger will help depends on where, and also on who else is watching. The map of twenty-three cities tells us that kindness has local accents. It also tells us that those accents are not destiny, because norms are made of what we do and what we are seen doing.

@char A15 cabin_fireplace | A gentle thought | Norms can be changed
If your own community feels distant or guarded, take heart. Norms aren't fixed. They are built from many small visible acts, and any one of us can add to them. Every time kindness is seen, it becomes a little more normal. You do not need to change a whole neighbourhood. A greeting said clearly, or a small kindness done where it can be seen, adds to the tone of the place, and the tone of a place is built out of exactly such small things.

@char A08 cabin_fireplace | Looking back | What the cities taught us
So what did we learn? That helping varied greatly across twenty-three cities. That cultural ideals like simpatía may play a part, though rankings must be read with care. That norms are shaped by what people see others doing. And that visible kindness makes more kindness likely.
