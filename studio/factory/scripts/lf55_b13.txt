Hugo slows down outside the old community hall. Through the open door he can hear a small amateur orchestra working through the same few bars again and again, stopping, laughing, starting over. He stands in the doorway's warm light for a minute, listening. Even this clumsy rehearsal stirs something in his chest, a faint echo of the feeling from earlier.

@char A15 living_room_day | The belief | We only cry when we're sad
Our ninth belief is the simplest of all, and perhaps the most clearly wrong: that tears are a sign of sadness. If someone is crying, they must be unhappy. Yet nearly everyone has cried at a wedding, a reunion, a birth, a piece of music or the end of a film where everything turns out well. Hugo's tears in the school hall are a perfect example.

@list A13 bedroom_night | Two explanations for happy tears | Joy carries a thread of sadness ;; The feeling is simply too big
Researchers have offered two main explanations for happy tears. The first is that joyful moments often carry a thread of sadness inside them. The second is that strong positive emotion can simply overwhelm us, and tears are one way the body copes when a feeling is too big to hold. Let's look at both, because Hugo's evening may contain a little of each.

@char A10 rooftop_dusk | Hidden sadness | Joy with a shadow
Vingerhoets has pointed out that positive events can also stir up negative feelings. A reunion reminds you of the time spent apart. A wedding reminds you of the people who aren't there to see it. A child's first concert reminds you that they're growing up, and that time is passing for you as well. The joy is real, and so is the quiet ache that travels with it.

@char N28 attic_rain | Hugo's thread | Who wasn't in the hall
Hugo thinks about that. In the hall, watching his granddaughter, did he think of his wife, who would have loved to hear her sing? Perhaps, somewhere underneath, he did. Perhaps the song about the sea reached a place where pride and missing someone sit very close together. Tears don't always need a single, simple cause.

@study N07 cabin_fireplace | Oriana Aragón, Margaret Clark, Rebecca Dyer and John Bargh | Psychological Science, published 2015 | Mixed reactions to strong positive feelings
The second explanation was explored by a team at Yale University. In 2015, Oriana Aragón, Margaret Clark, Rebecca Dyer and John Bargh published a paper in Psychological Science about what they called dimorphous expressions: two very different expressions that come from the same strong feeling. Crying at happy moments is one example. Another is the urge to pinch the cheeks of a very cute baby.

@list N20 autumn_overlook | What the Yale team found | Very cute babies drew both care and playful aggression ;; Feeling overwhelmed drove the mixed reactions ;; The same people showed this in other happy moments ;; 679 people took part in one online study
In their experiments, people viewing pictures of especially cute babies reported both caring feelings and playfully aggressive ones, such as wanting to squeeze or pinch. The mixed reactions were driven by feeling overwhelmed by positive emotion. People who showed these reactions with babies tended to show them in other positive situations too. One of the online studies alone involved six hundred and seventy-nine people.

@char A04 bedroom_night | A way to rebalance | Coming back down
The researchers proposed that these opposite-seeming expressions might help people bring overwhelming positive feelings back into balance. People who showed more of the aggressive kind of reaction had a larger drop in their positive emotion five minutes later. Aragón suggested that people may be restoring emotional equilibrium with these expressions. Happy tears, on this view, are a kind of release valve for joy that's too big to hold.

Wooden music stands and empty chairs in a warmly lit hall, sheet music left open, a violin case resting against a chair leg. Music is one of the most reliable sources of happy tears. People are moved by a melody, by a performance, or by the sight of others giving everything to make something beautiful together.

@study A03 living_room_day | Janis Zickfeld, Alan Page Fiske and many colleagues | Published 2019 | Being moved, across 19 nations and 15 languages
There's even a name for one particular kind of tearful feeling. Researchers led by Janis Zickfeld and Alan Page Fiske call it kama muta. In a study published in 2019, they surveyed three thousand five hundred and forty-two people in nineteen countries, across five continents and fifteen languages, about the experience most of us would simply call being moved.

@list A05 cafe_sunset | Signs of being moved, in their research | A warm feeling in the chest ;; Moist eyes or tears ;; Chills or goosebumps ;; A lump in the throat ;; A sense of buoyancy
They found a consistent set of signs, which appeared together across cultures: a warm feeling in the chest, moist eyes or tears, chills or goosebumps, a lump in the throat, and a feeling of buoyancy or lightness. If you've ever felt all of these at once, watching a reunion at an airport or the end of a moving film, you've felt kama muta.

@char A03 rooftop_dusk | What sets it off | Closeness suddenly growing
According to these researchers, kama muta is triggered when people experience or witness a sudden intensification of communal sharing. In plainer words, it's when closeness or belonging between people suddenly grows stronger. A long-lost friend returns. A crowd comes together. A family gathers round a small child singing her heart out on a stage.

@char A10 cabin_fireplace | Hugo's tears, explained | Pride, belonging, and a little ache
Now Hugo's tears in the school hall begin to make sense. A room full of families, a group of children singing together, his own granddaughter among them, the sense of being part of something larger. That's almost a textbook moment for being moved. Add a thread of missing his wife, and the old habit of holding things in, and the tears were close to inevitable.

@list A20 autumn_overlook | What was in that school hall | Children singing together ;; Families side by side ;; A granddaughter growing up ;; Someone missing
Let's list what was in that school hall. Children singing together, in one voice. Families sitting side by side in the warm. A granddaughter growing up, faster than anyone expected. And someone missing, who would have loved to be there. Almost every ingredient for tears of joy, and one small ingredient of sorrow, all in the same few minutes.

@list A13 cafe_sunset | Four kinds of tear-proneness, as Vingerhoets describes | Attachment tears ;; Societal tears ;; Sentimental and moral tears ;; Compassionate tears
Vingerhoets has described four dimensions of how prone people are to tears. There are attachment tears, linked to loss and separation. Societal tears, linked to events in the wider world. Sentimental and moral tears, linked to beauty, kindness and goodness. And compassionate tears, shed for other people's suffering. Hugo's were mostly the sentimental kind, perhaps with a little attachment mixed in.

@char A15 rooftop_dusk | Tears with age | Meaning, more than pain
Vingerhoets has also described how older adults cry relatively often over experiences that give their lives depth and meaning. A child's tears tend to come from pain, fear or frustration. With the years, more tears seem to come from beauty, connection and the sense of time passing. If you find yourself crying more easily at happy things as you get older, you're in good company.

@char N20 cabin_fireplace | The verdict | Clearly a myth
So the ninth belief is clearly a myth. Tears don't only mean sadness. They can come from joy, pride, relief, beauty, and the warm swell of belonging. Sometimes a thread of sadness runs through the happiness. Sometimes the feeling is simply too big. Either way, a tear at a school concert is one of the most human things a grandfather can do.
