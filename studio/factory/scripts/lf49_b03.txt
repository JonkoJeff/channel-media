@chapter N10 bedroom_night | The brain at rest
Anton is back in his armchair with his tea, and the book is closed on his knee. He's not doing anything in particular. And, as it turns out, neither is his brain, at least in the way that scientists once understood it.

@char N20 cafe_sunset | An old assumption | Rest means quiet
For a long time, the common assumption was simple. When you do a task, parts of the brain switch on. When you stop, they switch off. Rest was thought of as a blank baseline, a sort of idling engine, and the interesting science was whatever happened when the engine was asked to do something.

@char N02 living_room_day | A strange observation | Something that wouldn't go away
But in the 1990s, researchers using brain imaging began to notice something odd. When people were asked to do a focused task, such as looking at words or solving a puzzle, some regions of the brain did switch on. But a different set of regions switched off, or at least became less active, compared with when people were simply lying quietly in the scanner.

@char N19 autumn_overlook | A pattern emerges | The same regions, again and again
What made it striking was that the same regions kept showing up, in study after study, regardless of the task. A group of areas along the middle of the brain, towards the front and the back, always seemed more active when nothing was being asked of the person, and quieter when attention was needed elsewhere. The pattern was so consistent that it couldn't be an accident.

@study N07 cafe_sunset | Marcus Raichle and colleagues | PNAS, 2001 | Brain imaging showed a consistent set of regions that were more active at rest than during focused tasks
In 2001, the neurologist Marcus Raichle and his colleagues at Washington University in St. Louis published a paper that gave this pattern a name. They called it a default mode of brain function. Their idea was that when you're not engaged in an outside task, the brain doesn't go quiet. It falls back to a default way of operating, which is active and organized, and which seems to matter.

@char N10 rooftop_dusk | A quiet surprise | The brain is never idle
One striking point that came out of that line of research concerns energy. Raichle and others noted that the brain uses a great deal of energy even at rest, and that the extra energy used when we take on a demanding task is quite small by comparison, usually just a few percent. The brain isn't switching on when you give it something to do. It's already running, all the time.

@char N23 street_day | A name for the network | The default mode network
The set of regions came to be called the default mode network. It includes parts of the medial prefrontal cortex, towards the front of the brain, and the posterior cingulate cortex and precuneus, towards the back, along with regions in the parietal lobes and the inner temporal lobes, including the hippocampus. It's a team, and it works together.

@char N09 cabin_fireplace | The question that followed | What is it for?
And then came the obvious question. If the brain spends so much effort on this network when we're apparently doing nothing, what is it doing? One of the first suggestions was that it might be the home of mind wandering itself. When we're not focused on the outside world, we turn our attention inward, and this network seems to be where we do that.

@study N07 autumn_overlook | Malia Mason and colleagues | Science, 2007 | People practiced a task until it became automatic, then did it in the scanner while their reports of wandering thoughts were recorded
In 2007, the psychologist Malia Mason and her colleagues ran an experiment that made the link directly. Volunteers practiced a simple task until it was automatic, and then did it, or a new version of it, in the scanner. When the task was easy and well practiced, people reported more wandering thoughts, and the default network was more active. The title of their paper said it plainly: wandering minds and the default network.

@char N02 street_day | Why that was interesting | A task that lets you drift
The clever part of the design was that the difficulty of the task was changed, while everything else stayed similar. An easy, practiced task left spare mental room, and the mind used it to wander. A new, unfamiliar one demanded full attention, and the wandering faded. And the brain activity followed.

@quote N27 cabin_fireplace | When the world asks for less, the inner world begins to speak. | a way of putting the finding
That's one way of putting what they found. When the outside world asks for less, the mind turns inward, and the default network lights up.

@char N19 living_room_day | A refinement | It isn't only the default network
But the story got more complicated, in an interesting way. In 2009, Kalina Christoff and her colleagues at the University of British Columbia published a study in PNAS that used experience sampling during brain scanning. They interrupted people mid-task, at random, and asked whether their minds had been wandering. Then they looked at the brain activity just before each interruption.

@study N07 rooftop_dusk | Kalina Christoff and colleagues | PNAS, 2009 | Experience sampling during fMRI compared brain activity before reported mind wandering with brain activity during focused reading
They found that the default network was indeed more active before people reported wandering. But they also found something more surprising. A set of regions linked with executive control, the parts that help us plan and hold goals in mind, were active too. And they were most active when people hadn't noticed that their minds had wandered.

@char N10 cafe_sunset | What that suggests | A busy, working mind
That finding changed the picture. It suggested that mind wandering isn't simply the brain going slack. In some cases, the control regions of the brain are quietly working alongside the default network, which suggests that a wandering mind may be busy rather than lazy. It's a different kind of busyness, directed inwards, and not always supervised.

@photo lf49_b03_01.jpg | A city at night, lights still on
Seen from a hill at night, a city never really turns off. Windows glow in a thousand small squares, and traffic moves like slow threads of light. From the outside, it looks quiet. Up close, it's full of activity, only none of it is aimed at you.

@char N20 autumn_overlook | An image to hold | A city that never switches off
Many scientists use a similar image for the resting brain. From a distance it looks calm, but it's full of hidden activity, much of it quietly organizing memories, rehearsing futures and keeping a running sense of who you are. It's a city at night. Not asleep, only turned inward.

@char N02 street_day | Buckner's review | The network's many jobs
In 2008, the neuroscientists Randy Buckner, Jessica Andrews-Hanna and Daniel Schacter wrote a major review of the network for the Annals of the New York Academy of Sciences. They described how the same regions seem to be involved in remembering the past, imagining the future, thinking about other people's minds, and wandering through the many possible things we could be doing. In other words, it's the network of the inner life.

@char N10 bedroom_night | Back to Anton | A quiet armchair
In the armchair, Anton lets his eyes rest on the lamp. If the research is right, his default network is quite busy right now, quietly turning over the afternoon, the week ahead, and the small things he's forgotten to do. He isn't wasting time. He's doing what a brain does when it has nothing else to do, which is to keep thinking.
