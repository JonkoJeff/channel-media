@chapter A12 cabin_fireplace | The body's own clock
In the early 1930s, the American physiologist Hudson Hoagland was looking after his wife, who was ill with a fever. He noticed something odd. To her, time seemed to be passing very slowly. Minutes felt much longer than they should. Instead of brushing it off, Hoagland did what curious scientists do. He turned it into an experiment.

@char A10 bedroom_night | Counting to sixty | Again and again
He asked his wife to count to sixty, at what she felt was one count per second. And he timed her with a stopwatch. He asked her to do this again and again over the course of her illness, as her temperature rose and fell. It was a simple, homely test, carried out at a sickbed. But the results would become one of the most famous early clues in the study of time perception.

@study A13 living_room_day | Hudson Hoagland | Published in the 1930s | With a fever, his wife counted to sixty faster
Here's what he found. When her temperature was higher, she counted faster. Her sixty seconds were over sooner than sixty real seconds. And the higher the fever, the faster she counted. It was as if something inside her that kept time had sped up with the heat, so that the outside world seemed slow by comparison. Hoagland wondered whether a kind of chemical clock in the body was involved.

@char A03 attic_rain | A warm clock | Later experiments
Of course, one person counting at a bedside isn't enough to build a theory on. But later researchers followed up the idea with more careful experiments, warming or cooling volunteers and asking them to judge short intervals. In 1995, John Wearden and Ian Penton-Voak reviewed this kind of research, and concluded that body temperature can indeed influence how we judge time, in roughly the direction Hoagland had noticed.

Picture a quiet bedroom at night. An old brass clock on the shelf. A glass of water. A folded wool blanket at the end of the bed. Somewhere in rooms like this, Hoagland sat with his stopwatch and noticed that his wife's sense of time had drifted away from the clock. A small observation, at a sickbed, that opened a door into how the body keeps time.

@char A20 cafe_sunset | The pacemaker idea | Ticks and a counter
Inspired by clues like these, psychologists built a simple model of how we might time short intervals. In 1963, Michel Treisman described a version of it. Imagine a pacemaker inside us, sending out a steady stream of ticks. And imagine a counter that adds them up. To judge how long something lasted, the brain checks how many ticks have been counted. More ticks, longer time.

@list A13 cafe_sunset | A simple model of timing | A pacemaker sends out ticks ;; A counter adds them up ;; More ticks feels like more time
Let's set out the model simply. A pacemaker sends out ticks. A counter adds them up. And more ticks feels like more time. If the pacemaker speeds up, as it might with a fever, more ticks pile up in each real second. So an outside interval feels longer than it should, and sixty counted seconds come around too soon. That's how the model would explain Mrs. Hoagland's counting.

@char A12 living_room_day | An attention gate | Ticks can be missed
Later researchers added an important twist. Dan Zakay and Richard Block suggested that attention acts like a gate between the pacemaker and the counter. When we pay attention to time, the gate is open and more ticks get through. When we're absorbed in something else, the gate closes a little, ticks are missed, and time seems to pass more quickly. It's a neat way of explaining why a watched pot never boils.

@char A15 street_day | A watched pot | And a good book
Think about that. Waiting for a kettle, staring at it, the gate is wide open. Every tick is counted, and the minutes feel long. But lost in a good book, your attention is somewhere else. Many ticks slip by uncounted. You look up, and an hour has gone. The model is a simplification, of course. But it captures a lot of everyday experience in a single, memorable picture.

@quote A19 rooftop_dusk | Is there one clock in the head? | The sixth popular idea
And that brings us to a sixth popular idea. That somewhere in the brain, there's a single clock, ticking away like a watch, keeping all of our time. The pacemaker model can make it sound that way. But researchers today generally think timing is more spread out than that, involving several brain systems, and perhaps different mechanisms for different lengths of time.

@char A10 attic_rain | Many timekeepers | Different jobs
There's timing for split seconds, which helps us catch a ball or understand speech. There's timing over seconds and minutes, the kind used in waiting or counting. There's the daily rhythm of sleep and waking, which follows the cycle of day and night. And there's the much longer sense of months and years, which leans heavily on memory. These seem to involve different processes, working together.

@quote A19 cabin_fireplace | Not one clock, but a quiet orchestra. | One way to picture it
So here's one way to picture it. Not one clock, but a quiet orchestra. Different instruments keep time in different ways, and together they give us our sense of time. The pacemaker model remains useful as a simple sketch, especially for short intervals. But the real system seems to be richer, and still not fully understood. That's part of what makes this field so fascinating.

@char A12 cabin_fireplace | Verdict on the sixth myth | More than one clock
So what's the verdict on our sixth popular idea? There's probably no single clock in the head. Instead, our sense of time seems to be built from several systems, each suited to different time scales. And that matters for Hanna's question, because the feeling that a year has flown by isn't really about the ticking of a pacemaker. It's about how the mind remembers and summarizes a long stretch of life.

@char A20 cabin_fireplace | Back to Hanna | Feeling the warmth
Hanna pulls the blanket a little closer. The fire has made the room warm, and she feels pleasantly drowsy. She thinks of times she'd been ill as a child, lying in bed with a fever while the afternoon seemed to last forever. She'd always thought it was boredom. Perhaps it was partly that. But perhaps, as Hoagland suspected, the heat had also quietly sped up something inside her.
