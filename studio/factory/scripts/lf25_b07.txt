@chapter A04 attic_rain | How fights begin
In the 1980s, in a laboratory at the University of Washington in Seattle, a psychologist named John Gottman built what the press called the Love Lab. It was an apartment, fitted with cameras and sensors, where couples came to spend a day, and to discuss the issues in their relationship while being recorded. Over several decades, thousands of couples took part.

@char A12 living_room_day | The method | Watch, measure, follow up
Gottman and his colleagues coded every facial expression, every word, every pause. They measured heart rate, sweat and movement. And then they followed the couples for years, to see who stayed together and who didn't. It was one of the most detailed studies of human conflict ever done, and it led to a few clear observations.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Carrère & Gottman | Family Process, 1999 | Predicting divorce among newlyweds from the first three minutes of a marital conflict discussion
The researchers observed newlyweds as they discussed a disagreement, and found that the first three minutes carried an unusual amount of information. Couples whose discussions started with criticism, sarcasm or blame tended to end the way they began, while those that started softly tended to stay constructive. The authors reported that they could predict the later course of the marriage with high accuracy.

@char A04 attic_rain | The harsh start-up | How you begin is how it ends
Gottman called it harsh start-up. It's the habit of opening a difficult topic with an accusation: you never help, you always forget, why can't you ever. These openings put the other person on the defensive from the very first sentence, and the rest of the conversation then struggles to recover.

@list A03 living_room_day | Harsh and soft start-ups | Harsh: you never help me with anything ;; Soft: I'm feeling overwhelmed and could use help ;; Harsh: you always forget what matters ;; Soft: I'd like to talk about the plans, because it matters to me
The contrast is easy to hear. The soft version describes a feeling and states a need, without blaming. It doesn't give up the point. It simply makes it easier to hear, because it doesn't ask the listener to defend their character.

@chapter A13 rooftop_dusk | The four horsemen
Gottman also identified four patterns that he thought were especially corrosive. He borrowed a phrase from the Bible and called them the four horsemen. They are criticism, contempt, defensiveness and stonewalling, and he argued that they tend to appear in that order, each making the next more likely.

@list A12 living_room_day | The four horsemen | Criticism: attacking the person, not the behaviour ;; Contempt: mockery, sneering, a sense of superiority ;; Defensiveness: counter-attacking or playing the victim ;; Stonewalling: shutting down and withdrawing
Of the four, Gottman considered contempt the most destructive. It carries a message of disgust, as if the other person were beneath you, and it's the opposite of respect. In his work, signs of contempt were among the strongest predictors of relationship breakdown.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Gottman & Levenson | Journal of Marriage and Family, 2000 | The timing of divorce: predicting when a couple will divorce over a 14-year period
In a follow-up of couples over fourteen years, Gottman and Levenson found that different patterns predicted divorce at different times. Negative patterns, such as the horsemen, were associated with earlier divorce, while a lack of positive emotion in everyday and conflict interactions predicted later divorce. The fuller picture was that both too much negativity and too little warmth did damage.

@char A01 cafe_sunset | A fair caution | Predictions are not destiny
It's important to treat this work with care. Some critics have pointed out that the reported accuracy rates came from analysing the same couples that were used to build the model, which tends to flatter any prediction. When a prediction is tested on a new group, accuracy usually falls. The work is influential, but the numbers should be held loosely.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Heyman & Slep | Journal of Marriage and Family, 2001 | The hazards of predicting divorce without cross-validation
Richard Heyman and Amy Slep made this critique carefully. They noted that models built on the same sample that they predict will always look better than they are, and called for validation in fresh samples. It's a good example of science doing what it should: a striking claim, a careful challenge, and a clearer sense of what has been shown.

@list A03 living_room_day | What survives the critique | Soft start-ups are kinder and tend to go better ;; Contempt is corrosive in many studies ;; Warmth in everyday moments matters ;; The exact prediction numbers are uncertain
That's a fair way to hold the evidence. The broad lessons have held up in later studies. The precise percentages, which made the headlines, should be treated with caution.

@chapter A06 cafe_sunset | The ratio and the repair
One more idea is worth keeping. Gottman suggested that stable couples had a ratio of about five positive interactions to every negative one during conflict, and that they also made frequent repair attempts, small gestures to cool things down: a joke, a touch, an apology, a pause. The ratio is a rough rule, and has been debated, but the idea of repair is widely supported.

@char A19 cabin_fireplace | A repair attempt | Small moves that cool things
Repair attempts can be tiny. I'm sorry, that came out wrongly. Can we start again? Let me try to say that differently. What matters, according to the research, is that the other person accepts the attempt. Couples who could accept each other's repairs, even clumsy ones, recovered from arguments more easily.

@list A12 living_room_day | Words to keep from this chapter | Harsh start-up ;; Four horsemen ;; Contempt ;; Repair attempt
Four terms to remember. A harsh start-up is opening a hard topic with blame. The four horsemen are criticism, contempt, defensiveness and stonewalling. Contempt is expressing disrespect or superiority. And a repair attempt is any gesture that eases tension and invites a fresh start.

@list A03 living_room_day | Rewriting your first sentence | Describe the situation, not the person ;; Say how you feel ;; State what you'd like ;; Leave out always and never
Here's a practical exercise. Before your next hard conversation, write the first sentence. Describe what happened, not what kind of person the other is. Say how it affects you. State what you'd like. And remove the words always and never, which almost no one hears as true. Gottman's research suggests that this one sentence can change the whole direction of the talk, and it takes a minute to write.

@photo lf25_b07_01.jpg | A quiet room for hard talks
A quiet room, two armchairs and a small lamp. Gottman's apartment-laboratory was meant to look like a home, because the aim was to see what really happens when people talk about what matters. The most important finding was how much depended on the first thirty seconds.

@list A03 living_room_day | Picture this scene | A couple, a messy kitchen, a tired evening ;; One opens with you never help ;; The other defends ;; The first sentence decided the rest
Picture a couple in their kitchen after a long day. One of them opens with, you never help with anything in this house. The other, who spent the afternoon at the hospital with a parent, bristles and replies, that's not fair. Within a minute, they're arguing about fairness, not about the kitchen. Now picture the same moment with a different first sentence: I'm exhausted and this mess is getting to me. Could we tackle it together? The facts haven't changed, and neither has the mess. But the second opening gives the other person something to respond to that isn't an attack. It's an invented example, and it shows the pattern that Gottman observed over many years.

@list A19 living_room_day | Phrases that raise the temperature | You always... ;; You never... ;; What's wrong with you? ;; Calm down
Here are four phrases that almost always raise the temperature. You always and you never are accusations about character, and they invite a list of exceptions. What's wrong with you is an attack. And calm down, though it's meant kindly, usually has the opposite effect, because it tells people that their feelings are a problem. The research on harsh start-ups, and on criticism and contempt, suggests that avoiding these phrases is one of the simplest changes a person can make. You can replace each with something that describes the situation or the feeling. Instead of, you never help, try, I've been carrying a lot this week and I'd like to share it. It's longer, but it works much better.

@char A15 cabin_fireplace | A gentle thought | Begin softly
If you take only one habit from this chapter, take this one. Begin softly. It isn't weakness, and it doesn't mean hiding what you feel. It means opening in a way that lets the other person listen. A gentle first sentence is a gift to the rest of the conversation, and to the person you love.

@char A08 cabin_fireplace | Looking back | What fights taught us
So what did we learn? That the start of a conversation tends to set its course. That criticism, contempt, defensiveness and stonewalling corrode. That reported prediction rates should be held loosely. And that repair attempts, accepted warmly, help couples recover.
