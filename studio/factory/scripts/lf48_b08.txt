@chapter N10 attic_rain | The chemistry of a shiver
Ines has put the last plate on the rack and sat down at the kitchen table with a cup of tea, still listening. She's wondering about the little rush she felt. Is it a chemical, and if so, which one, and could anyone ever prove it?

@char N02 living_room_day | Liking and wanting | Two different systems
To follow the chemistry, it helps to know one idea from the neuroscientist Kent Berridge, at the University of Michigan. He and his colleagues have argued for years that the brain has separate systems for wanting something and for liking it. Wanting is the pull, the drive to seek. Liking is the pleasure itself, the warm feeling when you get it.

@char N19 cafe_sunset | Dopamine and opioids | Different jobs
In that picture, dopamine is closely tied to wanting, and the body's own opioid chemistry is more closely tied to liking. It's a simplification, and neuroscientists are still refining it, but it's a helpful map. And it matches what we've seen so far. Dopamine rose as people anticipated their favorite moments. Opioid blockers, in the small studies we mentioned, dulled the pleasure of the music itself.

@study N07 street_day | Laura Ferreri, Ernest Mas-Herrero and colleagues | PNAS, 2019 | Twenty-seven people took a dopamine booster, a dopamine blocker or a dummy pill before listening; pleasure rose or fell
In 2019, a team in Barcelona and Montreal tested dopamine directly. Twenty-seven volunteers came to the lab on three separate days. On one, they took a medicine that boosts dopamine, levodopa. On another, a medicine that blocks it, risperidone. On the third, a dummy pill. Each time they listened to music and rated how much they enjoyed it and how much they wanted to hear more.

@char N18 autumn_overlook | What they found | A dial for the pleasure
The results were clear and, in a way, remarkable. With the dopamine booster, people reported more pleasure and more wanting. With the dopamine blocker, both dropped. Nothing about the music had changed, and nothing about their hearing. The only difference was the chemistry, and it turned the dial up or down.

@stat N11 cabin_fireplace | Ferreri and colleagues, 2019 | 3 | conditions: dopamine up, dopamine down, and a dummy pill
It's a neat piece of experimental design, because every volunteer took part in all three conditions. That means each person acted as their own comparison, which makes the result harder to explain away by differences between people.

Think about what that says. Something as personal as the feeling of being moved by a piece of music, which can feel like it comes straight from the soul, can be nudged up or down by a few milligrams of a chemical. It doesn't make the feeling any less real. It simply shows that the feeling has a physical home.

@char N23 rooftop_dusk | A review of the field | Chanda and Levitin
In 2013, Mona Lisa Chanda and Daniel Levitin pulled the available evidence together in a review called The Neurochemistry of Music. They looked at studies on dopamine, opioids, and on other substances such as serotonin and oxytocin. Their conclusion was that music engages several chemical systems at once, tied to reward, to mood, to stress and to social bonding.

@char N20 bedroom_night | Stress and calm | Cortisol, too
Some studies in that review looked at stress. Listening to relaxing music before a medical procedure, for example, has been linked in some studies with lower levels of the stress hormone cortisol. The evidence there is mixed, and results depend on the music and the person, but it points to the same general idea. Music reaches into the body's chemistry in more than one way.

@char N10 living_room_day | A caution about shortcuts | Chemistry isn't the whole story
There's a risk in any story about brain chemistry, and it's the idea that a feeling is just a chemical. It's more accurate to say that a chemical is part of how the feeling is made. The music, your memories, your expectations, your mood, the room you're in, and the people you're with, all of that matters too, and it's all channelled through the chemistry.

@char N29 cafe_sunset | Why one dose isn't a cure | No music pill
It's also why nobody has found, or is likely to find, a drug that makes music beautiful. A medicine might raise the pleasure of music a little for someone who already has an ear for it. It couldn't supply the meaning, the memories or the surprise that make a piece worth loving.

@char N18 cafe_sunset | Does the feeling fade? | Familiarity and chills
Some people worry that if they play a favorite song too often, the chills will disappear. For many people, there does seem to be a limit, and some listeners deliberately save their most powerful pieces for special occasions. But researchers haven't pinned down a general rule, and plenty of people report chills from the same piece for decades.

@char N02 autumn_overlook | Ines and her tea | A small experiment
Ines decides to run a very small experiment of her own. She won't play the song again tonight. She'll leave it until tomorrow, when she's walking home. Without knowing it, she's using something researchers have seen in many settings, that a little distance from a pleasure can make it feel fresh again.
