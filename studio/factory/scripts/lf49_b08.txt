@chapter N10 cabin_fireplace | The gifts of wandering
Anton is stuck on something. For three days, he's been trying to decide how to lay out a map of a long valley trail, with a steep section in the middle that never seems to fit on the page. He's tried every arrangement he can think of, and none of them work.

@char N02 living_room_day | A familiar situation | Stuck on a problem
We've all been there. You work on something hard, and you push, and you get nowhere. And then you go for a walk, or take a shower, or wash the dishes, and in the middle of doing something else entirely, the answer simply arrives. It feels almost like cheating. But it's one of the oldest observations in the psychology of creativity.

@char N19 cafe_sunset | An old model | Four stages of creative thought
In 1926, the British psychologist Graham Wallas wrote a book called The Art of Thought, in which he described creative problem solving in four stages. Preparation, in which you work on the problem consciously. Incubation, in which you put it aside. Illumination, the sudden insight. And verification, in which you check that the idea works.

@char N10 autumn_overlook | An old story | Poincare on a bus
The mathematician Henri Poincare gave a famous account of this in the early twentieth century. He described struggling with a problem for days, then setting off on a trip, and, as he put his foot on the step of a bus, suddenly seeing the answer, with no sign that he'd been thinking about it. Stories like this are vivid, but they're only stories, and psychologists wanted to test the idea properly.

@study N07 cafe_sunset | Ut Na Sio and Thomas Ormerod | Psychological Bulletin, 2009 | A meta-analysis of studies asking whether taking a break from a problem helps people solve it
In 2009, Ut Na Sio and Thomas Ormerod published a meta-analysis in Psychological Bulletin, combining dozens of experiments on incubation. Their conclusion was that taking a break from a problem does tend to help. And the effect appeared to be strongest in studies where the break involved a low-demand activity, one that left the mind free to wander, rather than a very demanding task.

@char N23 rooftop_dusk | A sharper test | The Alternate Uses Task
A cleaner experiment came from Benjamin Baird, Jonathan Smallwood and colleagues. In their 2012 paper in Psychological Science, the title said it directly: Inspired by distraction. Their volunteers worked on a classic creativity test, the Alternate Uses Task, in which you list as many unusual uses as you can for an everyday object, such as a brick or a paperclip.

@study N07 living_room_day | Benjamin Baird and colleagues | Psychological Science, 2012 | After starting creativity problems, people did a demanding task, an undemanding task, a rest, or no break, and then returned to the same problems
After working on those problems for a while, the volunteers were given a break. Some did a demanding task that took up all their attention. Some did an undemanding task, which left room to think about other things. Some simply rested, and some had no break. Then everyone returned to the same problems, and also to some new ones.

@char N02 cabin_fireplace | What they found | The easy task won
The group that did the undemanding task during the break improved the most on the problems they'd already started. And they reported more mind wandering during the break, too. The people who did the demanding task, and those who rested without any task, didn't show the same improvement. And on new problems, the break made no difference.

@char N19 street_day | Why that detail matters | Only the old problems improved
That last detail is important. The benefit wasn't a general boost to creativity. It only showed up on problems the people had already begun. That fits the idea of incubation. The mind, while wandering, seems to keep turning over unfinished business in the background, and may stumble on a fresh angle.

@quote N27 rooftop_dusk | Wandering seems to help most on the problems you've already begun. | a summary of Baird and colleagues, 2012
That's a careful way to say it. Not that wandering makes anyone a genius, but that it can keep an unfinished problem warm.

@char N10 living_room_day | A cautious note | Not every study agrees
A cautious note belongs here. Incubation effects are not always easy to reproduce, and some studies have found nothing. The benefits seem to depend on the kind of problem, on how long the break is, and on what's done during it. So the honest summary is that wandering sometimes helps, in the right conditions, and that it isn't a guaranteed trick.

@char N09 autumn_overlook | A second route | Walking
Another well-known line of research looks at walking. In 2014, Marily Oppezzo and Daniel Schwartz, at Stanford, published a paper in the Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition with the title Give your ideas some legs. They ran four experiments comparing people who sat with people who walked, indoors on a treadmill or outdoors, while they did tests of creative thinking.

@study N07 cafe_sunset | Marily Oppezzo and Daniel Schwartz | Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition, 2014 | People did creativity tests while sitting or while walking, indoors or outdoors
The results were encouraging. On a test of generating new ideas, people produced more creative responses while walking than while sitting, and the benefit lasted a little while after they sat down again. The effect was seen both on a treadmill and outdoors, though the outdoor walkers did best on one of the tasks. Walking didn't help every kind of thinking equally, but for open-ended ideas, it did seem to help.

@char N02 living_room_day | Why walking might help | A mind with room to roam
The researchers weren't certain why. One idea is that walking is a gentle, automatic activity that leaves attention free to drift. Another is that movement loosens up the way we think. Whatever the explanation, it fits the broader pattern, which is that a mind with a little room to roam often does better than one held on a tight leash.

@char N10 cabin_fireplace | Anton's valley | An idea arrives
Anton, as it happens, doesn't need to be told. He gets up from the armchair, puts on his coat, and walks to the end of the street and back, with no particular thoughts. Halfway along, his mind drifts to the steep section of the trail, and he sees that it doesn't need to be drawn in a straight line at all. It can be folded into a small inset. He laughs out loud, alone, on a quiet pavement.

@char N20 attic_rain | A fair balance | A hope, not a promise
It's worth keeping that in proportion. Anton's insight might have come anyway, after a night's sleep. And the research can't promise that a walk will solve your problem. But it does suggest that a mind allowed to wander, in the right conditions, is not wasting its time.
