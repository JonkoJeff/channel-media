@chapter N20 cabin_fireplace | A shiver without the cold
So let's begin with the shiver itself, and with the simple question of what it is.

@char N18 street_day | What scientists call it | Frisson, or "chills"
Researchers have several names for the experience. Some call it frisson, a French word for a shiver. Some say musical chills, or thrills, or in everyday speech, goosebumps. In the scientific papers, the word you'll most often see is chills, and they define it loosely as a pleasant tingling sensation, often on the back of the neck or the arms, that comes in waves and lasts a few seconds.

@char N29 autumn_overlook | The skin has a part in it | Tiny muscles at the base of each hair
The goosebumps are the visible part. At the base of each body hair there's a tiny muscle, and when it contracts, it pulls the hair upright and makes the skin around it bump. In animals with thick fur, the same reflex puffs up the coat to trap warmth or to look bigger. In people, with so little fur left, the reflex is mostly a leftover. It still fires, though, and it fires for more reasons than cold.

@study N07 street_day | Benedek and Kaernbach | Biological Psychology, 2011 | Measured goosebumps with a small camera and found they went with moments of being moved
In 2011, two Austrian psychologists, Mathias Benedek and Christian Kaernbach, found a way to measure goosebumps objectively. They pointed a small camera at the skin of listeners' forearms and tracked tiny changes in the surface of the skin. People listened to music and watched emotional clips, and the goosebumps they recorded lined up with moments when listeners said they felt moved, and not just with moments when they felt chilly.

@char N20 living_room_day | Not just the skin | The whole body joins in
Chills are rarely just a skin event. Researchers who attach sensors to listeners during strong musical moments often see a short burst of arousal. The heart rate shifts. Breathing changes. The skin's electrical conductance, a sign of tiny changes in sweat, jumps. In other words, the body shows the same sort of excitement it might show in a thrilling moment, even though the listener is sitting perfectly still.

@char N10 cafe_sunset | A pleasant kind of arousal | Excited, but happy
That's worth noticing. Arousal usually has a bad reputation, as though it always meant stress. But arousal is simply the body getting ready. In a frightening moment it points toward fear, and in a musical moment it points toward delight. The same physical system can carry either message, and the music seems to steer it toward the second.

@char N23 rooftop_dusk | How common is it? | Most people, but not everyone
How common are chills? It depends on how the question is asked, which is a good habit to keep in mind with any statistic. In surveys, a majority of people say they've felt them, and some reports put the share well above half. But not everyone does, and the people who don't are not missing anything about hearing. @char N11 cabin_fireplace | A feeling people chase | A favorite piece, replayed
Many people who do get chills describe going back to the same piece again and again, to feel it. They know exactly which moment it will arrive. Some can point to the very bar. And for a long time, that was simply something people said about themselves. In the second half of the twentieth century, researchers began to ask whether it could be studied.

@char N02 autumn_overlook | How to study a shiver | Quiet rooms, favorite pieces
How do you study a feeling that lasts four seconds and can't be forced? Early researchers used a simple trick. They asked people to bring their own favorite music, the pieces that reliably gave them chills, and then they recorded what happened in the body and the brain while those pieces played. Starting from a person's own favorite turns out to work far better than choosing the music for them.

@char N19 living_room_day | Why that matters | Taste is personal
It matters because taste is personal. A piece that sends a wave through one listener leaves another untouched, and a researcher who plays the same recording to everyone will mostly measure the average of many different tastes. By letting people choose, scientists could look at what the brain does when the pleasure is real, rather than what it does when music is merely played.
