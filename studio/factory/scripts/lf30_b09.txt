@chapter A12 cafe_sunset | The map on your tongue
If seeing and hearing mix so freely, it's worth asking about a sense that feels even more private. Taste. Surely, when you put something in your mouth, you simply taste what's there.

@char A10 living_room_day | Another belief | Taste happens on the tongue
Here is the next belief we'll test tonight. Taste happens on the tongue, and the tongue does the work. Many of us even learned a picture of it at school, a little map with sweet at the tip, bitter at the back, and salty and sour along the sides.

#ai lf30_b09_01.jpg | Editorial still-life of an old printed school textbook lying open on a wooden desk beside a small bowl of lemons and a sugar bowl, soft afternoon window light, calm warm mood, muted palette, no people, no text, no logos

@photo lf30_b09_01.jpg | A picture many of us learned
You may remember seeing that map in a textbook, with neat coloured zones. It looked so official. And it turns out to be one of the most famous mistakes in the history of the senses.

@study A13 cabin_fireplace | David Hänig | Leipzig, 1901 | Measured how sensitive different edges of the tongue were to each taste, and found only small differences
The story starts in 1901, with a German researcher named David Hänig. He dabbed different tastes along the edges of people's tongues and measured how strong a taste had to be before they noticed it. He found that some areas were slightly more sensitive to some tastes than others. Slightly. The differences were small.

@study A03 attic_rain | Edwin Boring | Harvard, 1942 | Retold Hänig's results in a history of psychology, and the small differences were later read as separate zones
Four decades later, in 1942, the Harvard psychologist Edwin Boring described Hänig's results in a big book on the history of sensation and perception. Over the years, as the idea was copied from book to book, small differences in sensitivity turned into separate regions. And the tongue map was born.

@char A19 cabin_fireplace | Try it yourself | A tiny experiment
You can test this one at home. Put a few grains of salt on the very tip of your tongue, the place that's supposed to be only for sweet. You'll taste salt straight away. Every part of the tongue that has taste buds can taste every basic taste.

@study A18 living_room_day | Chandrashekar, Hoon, Ryba & Zuker | Nature, 2006 | A review of taste receptors: cells for each basic taste are found in taste buds across the whole tongue
Modern biology confirms it. In 2006, a review in the journal Nature by Jayaram Chandrashekar, Charles Zuker and their colleagues described how taste receptor cells work. Cells that respond to sweet, bitter, salty, sour and savoury, which is often called umami, are found in taste buds all over the tongue, not grouped into zones.

@list A15 cafe_sunset | The basic tastes | Sweet ;; Salty ;; Sour ;; Bitter ;; Umami, or savoury
So the basic tastes are sweet, salty, sour, bitter and umami, the rich savoury taste of broth, ripe tomatoes or aged cheese. Five simple signals. And here is the surprising part. Five signals are nowhere near enough to explain everything you experience when you eat.

@study A18 cabin_fireplace | Kikunae Ikeda | Tokyo, 1908 | Identified glutamate in kombu seaweed broth as the source of a distinct savoury taste he named umami
That fifth taste has its own small story. In 1908, the Japanese chemist Kikunae Ikeda was wondering about the deep, savoury taste of a traditional broth made with kombu seaweed. He traced it to a substance called glutamate, and he gave the taste a name, umami, from a Japanese word for delicious. For many decades, Western science treated it as a curiosity. Today it's widely accepted as a basic taste, with its own receptors on the tongue.

@chapter A05 autumn_overlook | Where flavour really comes from
Because most of what we call taste isn't coming from the tongue at all.

@char A13 attic_rain | A blocked nose | Food goes flat
Think of the last time you had a heavy cold. Your favourite meal suddenly tasted of almost nothing. Your tongue was working perfectly well. What had stopped was your nose.

@list A12 living_room_day | What makes a flavour | Taste from the tongue ;; Smell from inside the mouth ;; Texture and temperature ;; Sound, sight and expectation
Flavour is a team effort. The tongue gives you those five basic tastes. But as you chew and breathe out, scent from the food rises up the back of your throat into your nose. This is sometimes called retronasal smell, smell from the inside. Add texture, temperature, the crunch you hear and the colour you see, and your brain blends all of it into one experience, which it then places, rather cleverly, in your mouth.

@char A20 cafe_sunset | The nose-pinch test | Strawberry or nothing
There's a simple way to feel this. Pinch your nose closed and put a jelly bean or a sweet in your mouth. You'll notice that it's sweet, but you won't easily tell which flavour it is. Let go of your nose, and suddenly, there it is. Strawberry. Lemon. The flavour arrives with the breath.

@quote A10 cabin_fireplace | Flavour is something the brain puts together, and then hands to the mouth. | A way to think about it
So a useful way to picture it is this. Flavour isn't sitting in the food, waiting to be picked up. It's something the brain assembles from several senses at once, and then presents to you as if it were all coming from your tongue.

@chapter A18 rooftop_dusk | When the eyes join in
And if the brain is building flavour out of several senses, a mischievous question follows. What happens if one of those senses tells it something false?

#ai lf30_b09_02.jpg | Editorial still-life of two plain glass tumblers on a wooden laboratory bench, one holding pale golden liquid and one holding deep red liquid, small glass droppers beside them, soft daylight, calm neutral mood, no people, no text, no logos

@photo lf30_b09_02.jpg | Two glasses, one wine
That is exactly what a team at the University of Bordeaux, in the heart of French wine country, decided to find out.

@study A03 living_room_day | Morrot, Brochet & Dubourdieu | University of Bordeaux, 2001 | Wine students described a white wine with red-wine words once it was coloured red with a tasteless dye
In 2001, Gil Morrot, Frédéric Brochet and Denis Dubourdieu published a study called The Color of Odors, in the journal Brain and Language. Their volunteers were students of wine tasting, people who were training to describe wine for a living. In one part of the study, the students tasted a white wine, and the same white wine coloured red with a dye that had no taste or smell of its own.

@list A13 cafe_sunset | What they reported | White wine: words like honey, lemon, pear ;; Same wine, dyed red: words like cherry, plum, spice ;; Nobody spotted that it was white
When the wine looked white, they described it with the kind of words people use for white wine, light fruits and floral notes. When the very same wine looked red, they reached for red-wine words, dark berries and spice. The colour had quietly changed what they reported smelling.

@char A19 cabin_fireplace | Not foolish | Very human
It's tempting to laugh at this, but that would miss the point. These weren't careless people. They were trained tasters doing their best. Their brains were simply doing what all brains do, using every clue available, including colour, to guess what was coming.

@char A10 living_room_day | Why it makes sense | A useful shortcut
And most of the time, that's a good strategy. In the real world, red wines and white wines really do smell different, and colour really is a reliable clue. The brain learns these patterns from experience. It only looks like a mistake when a researcher deliberately breaks the pattern.

@study A03 living_room_day | Massimiliano Zampini & Charles Spence | Journal of Sensory Studies, 2004 | Crisps seemed crisper and fresher when the crunching sound people heard was made louder or brighter
And it isn't only the eyes. In 2004, Massimiliano Zampini and Charles Spence, at the University of Oxford, asked volunteers to bite into crisps while wearing headphones. The sound of each bite was played back to them, sometimes louder, sometimes softer, sometimes with the high, bright frequencies turned up or down. When the crunch sounded louder and brighter, people rated the very same crisps as crisper and fresher. The crisps hadn't changed. Only the sound had.

@list A12 autumn_overlook | Expectation flavours everything | Colour of the food ;; Weight of the plate ;; Sounds while eating ;; What we were told it is
Researchers who study food and the senses have found many small effects like this, from the colour of a drink to the sounds we hear while we eat. The details vary from study to study, and some findings are stronger than others. But the general lesson keeps coming back. What we taste is shaped by what we expect to taste.

@char A15 cafe_sunset | Something to enjoy | Slow down
There's a kind and practical side to this. If flavour is built by the brain from many senses, then paying attention can make food richer. Look at it, smell it, chew slowly, notice the texture. You're giving your brain more to work with, and it rewards you.

@chapter A13 attic_rain | The sense you never think about
So far we've seen that the eyes guess, the ears borrow from the eyes, and the tongue gets most of its credit from the nose. But there's one sense that feels even more certain than all of these.

@char A03 bedroom_night | Close your eyes | Where is your hand?
Close your eyes for a moment, if you like, and ask yourself where your left hand is. You know, don't you? You don't need to look. You can feel exactly where it is, resting on a blanket or a knee. That feels like the most direct knowledge there is. Your own body, from the inside. And as we'll see next, even that can be fooled, with nothing more than a rubber glove and two paintbrushes.
