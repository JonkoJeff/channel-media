@chapter A04 attic_rain | The brain as a prediction machine
Take a look at a picture made of black and white blotches, rather like a spilled ink pattern. At first, it looks like nothing at all. Then someone tells you it's a dalmatian dog, sniffing the ground under a tree. And suddenly, you see it, instantly, and from that moment on, you can't stop seeing it. You couldn't go back to the blotches if you tried.

@char A12 living_room_day | The dalmatian | A picture that turns on
The dalmatian picture is a famous image, often credited to the photographer R. C. James and popularised by the psychologist Richard Gregory in the 1970s. It isn't a trick of the eyes. The picture itself doesn't change when you see the dog. What changes is the brain's interpretation, and with it, your experience.

@char A10 attic_rain | A bigger idea | Seeing is inferring
This simple experience points to one of the biggest ideas in the science of perception. In the nineteenth century, the German physicist Hermann von Helmholtz proposed that perception is a kind of unconscious inference. The brain doesn't read the senses directly. It works out the most likely cause of the signals it receives, and that conclusion is what you see.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Hermann von Helmholtz | Handbook of Physiological Optics, 1867 | Perception as unconscious inference
Helmholtz saw that the signals arriving at the eye are ambiguous. A small image on the retina could be a small object nearby, or a large one far away. To decide, the brain uses prior knowledge about how the world usually is. He called the process unconscious, because we never feel ourselves reasoning, and we simply see.

@list A03 living_room_day | What the brain combines | Signals from the senses ;; Memories of similar scenes ;; Knowledge about how things usually are ;; Expectations about what comes next
Put together, these make a perception. And the more experience you have, the stronger your expectations. Which is wonderful for speed and efficiency, and occasionally leads to a confident mistake.

@chapter A06 cafe_sunset | Prediction, not reception
In recent decades, this idea has been developed into a bigger theory, often called predictive processing or the predictive brain. It says that the brain is constantly generating predictions about what its senses will report next, and that what flows up from the senses is mostly the error, the difference between what was expected and what arrived.

@char A01 cafe_sunset | A cleaner way to say it | Guess, compare, update
Put plainly, the brain makes a guess, compares it with the incoming signal, and updates. If the guess was good, there's little to report, and the signal is quiet. If it was bad, the surprise travels upward, and the guess is revised. It's a thrifty way to work, because it spends effort only where the world isn't as expected.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Rao & Ballard | Nature Neuroscience, 1999 | Predictive coding in the visual cortex
In 1999, Rajesh Rao and Dana Ballard at the University of Rochester described a computer model of the visual cortex built on this principle. It reproduced some of the behaviour of real visual neurons, and helped to bring the idea into mainstream neuroscience. It was, and remains, a model, but a very influential one.

@char A04 attic_rain | Why it appeals | It explains a lot
One reason the idea appeals to many researchers is that it ties together so many findings. The dress, the filled-in blind spot, the McGurk effect and the dalmatian can all be seen as cases where the brain's predictions shape what we experience. Others think it's too broad to be tested properly, and that debate is still going on.

@list A12 living_room_day | What the theory says | The brain makes predictions all the time ;; Perception is the best current guess ;; Surprises are used to update the guess ;; Attention decides which surprises matter
Whether or not the details turn out to be right, this picture helps to explain a feeling that many of us have. The world feels immediate, but it is built, moment by moment, from a mixture of what's out there and what we expect.

@chapter A12 living_room_day | Seeing it again
Here's a way to catch your own predictions at work. Look at a Mooney image, a high-contrast photograph reduced to black and white blobs, named after the psychologist Craig Mooney who created them in 1957 to study how children recognise faces. At first, many appear meaningless. Once you've seen the original, the blobs jump into focus.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Hsieh, Vul & Kanwisher | Journal of Neurophysiology, 2010 | Recognition alters the spatial pattern of fMRI activation in early retinotopic cortex
In a brain-scanning study in 2010, Pei-Ling Hsieh and colleagues showed people Mooney images before and after they'd seen the matching photographs. After they knew what the image was, the pattern of activity in the early visual cortex, an area that handles basic features, changed. Knowing what was there altered the very first stages of seeing.

@char A10 attic_rain | The notable thing | Knowledge reaches down
This was a notable result because it suggests that knowledge reaches all the way down to early vision. It's not only that you think about the image differently. The way the brain responds, at an early stage, shifts. The idea of a one-way pipeline, from eye to brain to understanding, doesn't capture what's going on.

@char A19 cabin_fireplace | The hollow mask | A face that turns inside out
Another famous case is the hollow mask illusion. When you look at the inside of a mask, a hollow face, with the nose and cheeks curving away from you, it appears to bulge outward like a normal face. Even when you know that it's hollow and you can see it rotate, the brain refuses to accept the concave shape.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Gregory | The Intelligent Eye, 1970 | The hollow-face illusion and the power of the brain's expectation of faces
Richard Gregory used it as a showpiece for the idea that our expectations about faces are so strong that they override depth cues. Faces are almost never hollow, so the brain bets on a convex face, and wins the argument every time. Later research found that some people with certain conditions, such as schizophrenia, may be less susceptible to the illusion.

@char A04 attic_rain | A careful note | Interesting, not diagnostic
It's worth being careful about that last point. Some studies have found that people with schizophrenia are less fooled by the hollow mask, which has been taken as a sign that the weighting of expectations may differ. But these results are group averages, not a test for any individual, and the interpretation is still being worked out.

@chapter A10 attic_rain | When the guess goes too far
The predictive idea also offers a way of thinking about hallucinations. If perception is a controlled guess, then an excess of confidence in the guess can produce something that feels like a real experience without matching the world. In 2017, a study by Albert Powers, Philip Corlett and colleagues at Yale tested this.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Powers, Mathys & Corlett | Science, 2017 | Pavlovian conditioning-induced hallucinations result from overweighting of perceptual priors
The researchers trained people to expect a faint sound whenever a light flashed. Later, some people reported hearing the sound when there was none. And those people who did so, including people who regularly heard voices that others do not, seemed to give more weight to their expectations than did a comparison group.

@char A12 living_room_day | A cautious summary | A clue, not a cause
This is an exciting line of research, but it's early, and it needs the usual caution. The study involved a modest number of people, and a lab task is far from the daily experience of someone who hears voices. It suggests a way to think about the balance between expectation and evidence, and that is all it should be taken to say.

@list A03 living_room_day | The balance | Too much weight on expectation: seeing what you expect ;; Too much weight on the senses: being overwhelmed by detail ;; A good balance: stable, flexible perception ;; The balance can shift with tiredness, stress and context
We all slide along that scale from day to day. When you're tired, you mishear your name. When you're anxious, a shadow looks like a figure. Those aren't signs of anything wrong. They're ordinary samples of a prediction machine at work.

@char A01 cafe_sunset | Everyday examples | Mishearing and misreading
You've probably read a typo and not noticed, or heard a word in the noise of a fan, or seen a familiar face in a crowd who turned out to be a stranger. All of these are ordinary moments where prediction beat the evidence. It's usually harmless, and sometimes funny.

@char A08 cabin_fireplace | The wider view | Anil Seth
The neuroscientist Anil Seth has summed up this view with a memorable phrase, that perception is a controlled hallucination. He means that the brain's guess is continually tied to the world by sensory evidence, so it's not a free-floating fantasy. It's a hallucination that is reined in. That's a vivid way of putting the point, though it's only one way of describing it.

An abstract pattern and a magnifying glass. It's a reminder that the sense of meaning often comes from us. We bring the dog, the face, or the story, and the world supplies a few blotches. And, once it clicks, the world will never again look quite so blank.

@char A15 cabin_fireplace | A gentle thought | You are doing a lot of work
There's something quietly impressive about all this. Every moment of your waking life, your brain is doing a huge amount of silent work: guessing, checking and correcting, to give you a calm, stable world. The least we can do is be a little gentle with it when it makes a mistake.

@char A08 cabin_fireplace | Looking back | What the prediction machine taught us
So what did we learn about the prediction machine? That perception is inference, not a recording. That expectation reaches down into early vision. That some illusions are the brain's best bet winning over the evidence. And that a flexible balance between expectation and evidence is what keeps the world steady.
