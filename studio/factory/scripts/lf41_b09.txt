@study A10 cafe_sunset | Michael Wohl, Timothy Pychyl and Shannon Bennett | Carleton University, published 2010 | First-year students, two midterm exams and self-forgiveness
A study published in 2010 by Michael Wohl, Timothy Pychyl and Shannon Bennett, at Carleton University in Canada, looked at exactly this. They followed one hundred and nineteen first-year students through two midterm exams. Before the first exam, they asked how much the students had procrastinated on studying. And they asked something more unusual. How much had the students forgiven themselves for it?

@stat A19 rooftop_dusk | First-year students followed through two exams | 119 | Wohl, Pychyl and Bennett, 2010
Then they waited for the second exam, and asked about procrastination again. If the tough approach worked, you'd expect the students who were hardest on themselves to procrastinate least the second time. But the result went the other way. The students who had forgiven themselves for procrastinating before the first exam tended to procrastinate less before the second one.

@quote A13 cafe_sunset | Forgiveness lets us move past the last mistake. | The idea behind the result
The researchers suggested a reason. When we forgive ourselves for a slip, we can let it go and move on. When we don't, the bad feeling about the last time comes along with us to the next task. And remember what procrastination often is. A way of escaping a bad feeling. So adding more guilt to a task may only give us one more feeling we'd like to escape from.

@chapter A03 cabin_fireplace | Self-compassion
Fuschia Sirois, whom we met earlier, has also studied self-compassion and procrastination. Self-compassion means treating yourself with the kind of understanding you'd offer a friend who was struggling. In a study published in 2014, she found that people who procrastinated more also tended to have lower self-compassion, and that this seemed to be part of why they felt more stressed.

Here's a simple test. Imagine a good friend told you that they'd been putting off a speech for their sister's wedding. Would you call them hopeless? Probably not. You might say, that sounds like a big thing to write, no wonder it feels hard. Want to talk through a few stories together? That second voice is kinder. And according to this research, it may also be more useful.

@list A13 cafe_sunset | A kinder inner voice | That was hard, and that's okay ;; Everyone does this sometimes ;; What's one small next step?
So a kinder inner voice might sound something like this. That was hard, and that's okay. Everyone does this sometimes, I'm not the only one. And then, the most important part. What's one small next step I could take? It isn't about letting yourself off the hook forever. It's about clearing away the extra weight, so the next step feels possible.
