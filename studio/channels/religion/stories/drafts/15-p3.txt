CHAPTER: The Sign Above the Cross
Mark tells us that they brought him to Golgotha, offered him wine mixed with myrrh, which he did not take, and crucified him. Then, as often in the Gospels, the story pauses over a small detail: the soldiers divide his clothes by casting lots. || Mark 15:22–24 || text-card
The Gospels do not describe the act of crucifixion. They simply say, and they crucified him. This is the most reticent moment in the whole narrative. The first listeners already knew what it meant, and the writers did not dwell on suffering. || Codex Vaticanus, Mark 15 || wikimedia
What they do record is the written charge. In Mark it reads simply, the King of the Jews. Matthew and Luke give slightly different wording, and John says that it was written in Hebrew, in Latin and in Greek, and that it read, Jesus of Nazareth, the King of the Jews. || Mark 15:26; John 19:19–20 || text-card
Roman practice was to display the reason for the execution, either on a board carried before the condemned or fixed to the cross. The variations in wording across the Gospels are what we might expect from independent memories of a short text read once in a crowd. || Roman writing tablet, Vindolanda || wikimedia
John adds a small exchange. The chief priests ask Pilate to change the wording to, this man said, I am King of the Jews. Pilate refuses and says, what I have written, I have written. It is a brief moment of defiance from a governor who has otherwise given way. || John 19:21–22 || text-card
Two other men are crucified with Jesus, one on each side. Mark calls them bandits, a word that Josephus uses for rebels against Rome. So the three were probably executed together as part of a response to unrest in the festival city. || Mark 15:27 || text-card
Passers-by mock him, shaking their heads. The chief priests and scribes join in, saying that he saved others but cannot save himself. In Luke, one of the two men joins the mockers, while the other says, remember me when you come into your kingdom. || Luke 23:39–43 || text-card
Notice that each mockery echoes the charges that were made at the hearings. The Temple, the claim to be Messiah, the title of king. The Gospel writers wanted their readers to see that the events of that day were of a piece with everything that had led up to them. || Temple Mount model, Second Temple Jerusalem, Israel Museum || wikimedia
Mark also says that the soldiers divided his clothes, throwing dice to decide who should take what. John adds that the tunic was seamless, woven in one piece, so the soldiers cast lots for it instead of tearing it. John links this to a verse in the Psalms. || John 19:23–24; Psalm 22:18 || text-card
This is the first of several places in the passion story where a line from the Psalms shapes the telling. The early Christians read these scenes through the Scriptures they knew best, and used the language of the Psalms to find words for what had happened. || Psalm 22, Great Isaiah Scroll tradition, Dead Sea Scrolls || wikimedia
Women watch from a distance. In Mark they include Mary Magdalene, Mary the mother of James and Joses, and Salome. In John, the mother of Jesus and the beloved disciple stand near the cross, and Jesus speaks to them, giving them into one another's care. || John 19:25–27 || text-card
Here is another difference between the Gospels. In Mark, the disciples have fled and the women watch from afar. In John, some of the people closest to Jesus are at the foot of the cross. Historians usually treat the nearer scene as a theological and literary choice by John. || Stabat Mater, Giotto, Scrovegni Chapel || wikimedia
As the morning passes, something changes in the sky. All three Synoptic Gospels have a note about the hour, and then about light, and for each of them the day turns at noon. || Jerusalem under clouds, 19th century engraving || loc
CHAPTER: Hours and Words
All four Gospels give a timeline, and they do not quite agree. In Mark, Jesus is crucified at the third hour, around nine in the morning, and darkness falls from the sixth hour until the ninth. He dies at the ninth hour, about three in the afternoon. || Mark 15:25, 33–34 || text-card
In John, the trial before Pilate is still in progress at about the sixth hour, around noon, on the day of Preparation for the Passover. So the two outlines place the crucifixion at different times of the day. This is the same difference in dating that we noticed in the last video. || John 19:14 || text-card
Scholars have proposed several explanations: different ways of counting hours, scribal changes in the manuscripts, or different theological emphases. John's outline places Jesus's death at the time when the Passover lambs were slaughtered in the Temple, and that may be the point. || Temple sacrifice, Second Temple model || wikimedia
This is a good example of how the Gospels preserve different memories of the same day, and how historians weigh them. The general shape is the same: a morning execution, a few hours on the cross, and death in the afternoon, before the evening that began a Sabbath. || Sundial from ancient Jerusalem || wikimedia
Roman crucifixion could take many hours, even days. Part of the Gospel tradition, Jesus's death after only a few hours, surprised even the Roman governor. Mark says that when Joseph of Arimathea asked for the body, Pilate wondered whether he was already dead. || Mark 15:44 || text-card
What did Jesus say from the cross? Here the Gospels differ markedly, and the differences are worth noticing. Mark and Matthew record one cry only, in Aramaic: Eloi, Eloi, lema sabachthani, which they translate as, my God, my God, why have you forsaken me. || Mark 15:34 || text-card
That is the opening line of Psalm twenty-two, a psalm that begins in anguish and ends in trust. Some readers hear the whole psalm in the cry, others hear only its first line. The Gospels do not say, and scholars have debated it for generations. || Psalm 22:1 || text-card
Luke gives other words. Father, forgive them, for they do not know what they are doing. Today you will be with me in Paradise. Father, into your hands I commend my spirit. The first of these is missing in some of the oldest manuscripts, and textual critics are divided about it. || Luke 23:34, 43, 46 || text-card
John gives still another set. He entrusts his mother to the beloved disciple. He says, I thirst. And at the end he says, it is finished. Each Gospel, then, offers its own picture of the last hours, shaped by what the author most wanted readers to hear. || John 19:26–30 || text-card
Some Christian traditions arrange these sayings into a single list of seven last words, a way of meditating on the whole passion across the four accounts. It is a devotional pattern, and not a historical reconstruction. The differences between the Gospels are preserved, not erased. || Seven Last Words, Haydn, manuscript || wikimedia
For historians, the cry of abandonment is often regarded as the saying most likely to go back to the event, because it is hard to explain as a later invention. A leader's last words, in the earliest Gospel, express anguish and not triumph. Later Gospels soften that. || John P. Meier, A Marginal Jew || text-card
It is worth pausing on how much weight the tradition places on these few lines. They have inspired music, poetry and prayer across centuries and across cultures. And they are all that survives of what was said on the hill. || Passion music, Bach, St Matthew Passion manuscript || wikimedia
Then, in the middle of the day, the Synoptic Gospels record a change that nobody could have missed. || Judean hills at midday || wikimedia
CHAPTER: Darkness at Noon
Mark says that at noon, darkness came over the whole land until three in the afternoon. Matthew and Luke follow him. Luke adds that the sun's light failed. The Gospels do not explain how, and they do not say how far the darkness spread. || Mark 15:33; Luke 23:44–45 || text-card
Ancient writers often described darkness at the death of great figures, as a sign that the world itself was shaken. Roman authors said so of Julius Caesar's death, and the Hebrew prophets spoke of the day of the Lord as a day of darkness. The Gospel images belong to that language. || Amos 8:9 || text-card
Historians have noticed that a solar eclipse cannot occur at Passover, which falls at the full moon. Some ancient Christian writers, such as Julius Africanus in the third century, discussed this, so the problem is not a modern one. The Gospels themselves do not call it an eclipse. || Julius Africanus, Chronographiai || text-card
The most sensible conclusion is that the darkness belongs to the Gospel writers' way of marking the weight of the moment. It might preserve a memory of an unusual day, such as dust from a desert wind, but we cannot say, and the sources do not allow us to. || Khamsin dust over Jerusalem || wikimedia
Then Mark records that Jesus gave a loud cry and breathed his last. The curtain of the Temple was torn in two from top to bottom. And the Roman centurion standing opposite, seeing how he died, said, truly this man was God's Son. || Mark 15:37–39 || text-card
The torn curtain is a rich symbol. Mark does not explain it, but his first readers would have known that curtains in the Temple marked off the holiest places. The image suggests that something had been opened, or ended, in the very centre of Jewish worship. || Temple curtain, reconstruction, Tabernacle Museum || wikimedia
Matthew adds an earthquake, split rocks, and the opening of tombs, with holy people raised from sleep. That scene is found only in Matthew, and historians read it as symbolic language of the end of the age, in the tradition of Jewish apocalyptic writing. || Matthew 27:51–53 || text-card
Notice the first person to speak after the death is the Roman centurion. In Mark, a pagan soldier recognises what the disciples had failed to see. Luke gives his words as, certainly this man was innocent. In both, a Roman voice speaks for the dead man. || Luke 23:47 || text-card
John tells the ending differently. After Jesus says, it is finished, he bows his head and gives up his spirit. Because the next day is a Sabbath, the Jewish authorities ask Pilate to have the legs broken so that the bodies can be taken down. || John 19:30–31 || text-card
The soldiers break the legs of the other two but, finding Jesus already dead, they do not break his. One of them pierces his side with a spear, and blood and water come out. John says that this happened to fulfil Scripture. || John 19:32–37 || text-card
The detail of broken legs has some support in practice. Crucifixion victims could be hastened to death by breaking the legs, which stopped them from lifting themselves to breathe. Yet the Yehohanan find shows only that the heel bone was fractured, and scholars do not agree about the cause. || Zias and Sekeles, Israel Exploration Journal, 1985 || text-card
Whatever the details, all four Gospels agree that Jesus died on the cross, and that his death was witnessed by a Roman soldier and by women who had followed him. Then, as evening approaches, they turn to what happens to the body. || Evening over Jerusalem, 19th century engraving || loc
CHAPTER: A Borrowed Tomb
The Gospels agree that a man named Joseph of Arimathea went to Pilate and asked for the body. Mark calls him a respected member of the council who was also waiting for the kingdom of God. John says that he was a secret disciple. || Mark 15:42–43; John 19:38 || text-card
Burial was a matter of great importance in Jewish tradition. The Torah says that a person who has been executed and hung on a tree must not remain overnight, but must be buried the same day, so that the land is not defiled. This law may help to explain why Joseph acted quickly. || Deuteronomy 21:22–23 || text-card
Roman custom was often different. Bodies of the crucified might be left on the cross, or thrown into a common grave. Yet, as Josephus notes, Jews in Judea took down the crucified before sunset. The Gospel account fits what we know of Jewish practice. || Josephus, Jewish War 4.317 || text-card
Pilate, surprised that Jesus is already dead, checks with the centurion and then grants the body. Joseph buys a linen cloth, takes the body down, wraps it, and lays it in a tomb cut out of rock. Then he rolls a stone against the door. || Mark 15:44–46 || text-card
Tombs of this kind are well known in Jerusalem from the first century. Wealthy families cut chambers into the rock, with benches or niches for the bodies, and sealed the entrance with a stone, sometimes a large round one rolled in a groove. || Rolling-stone tomb, Tomb of the Herods, Jerusalem || wikimedia
After a year or so, when the flesh had decayed, the bones were often gathered into a stone box called an ossuary, and the bench was used again. The ossuaries found in Jerusalem, sometimes inscribed with names, give us a vivid picture of burial practice in the period. || Ossuary, Israel Museum || wikimedia
John adds Nicodemus, the Pharisee who once came to Jesus at night. He brings a large quantity of myrrh and aloes, and the two men wrap the body in linen with spices, according to Jewish burial custom. The tomb was in a garden near the place of crucifixion. || John 19:39–42 || text-card
Mark closes the scene with the women. Mary Magdalene and Mary the mother of Joses see where the body is laid. Their presence matters, because they are the link between the burial and the story that follows, when they come back to the tomb. || Mark 15:47 || text-card
Matthew adds a guard at the tomb, posted at the request of the chief priests, who feared that the body might be stolen. That detail appears only in Matthew, and is usually read in light of the arguments between Christians and others in his time. || Matthew 27:62–66 || text-card
You may wonder about relics and cloths. In 1988, laboratories tested a sample from the Shroud of Turin, a linen cloth that bears a faint image of a man, and dated it to the thirteenth or fourteenth century. Some researchers have since questioned the sampling, but the study is widely accepted. || Damon and colleagues, Nature, 1989 || text-card
The Shroud, then, is not evidence a historian can use. What matters for our story is the burial, which is among the best-supported parts of the tradition, since it was in Jewish custom and a named man, Joseph, was remembered as responsible. || Burial preparations, Giotto, Scrovegni Chapel || wikimedia
So ends the day. The sun sets, the Sabbath begins, and the women return to their homes. What happened after that belongs to another story. First, we must ask how the earliest believers understood what they had seen. || Jerusalem at sunset, Mount of Olives || wikimedia
