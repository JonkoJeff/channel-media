@chapter A10 cabin_fireplace | Myth four: wavelength is just a figure of speech
We say it all the time. We're on the same wavelength. It's just a figure of speech, of course. A way of saying we understand each other. Brains don't really have wavelengths that match. Or do they? Over the past twenty years, neuroscientists have found ways to watch more than one brain at a time, and what they've seen is rather remarkable.

@char A13 rooftop_dusk | Watching together | Shared experiences, shared patterns
It started with a simple question. If several people watch the same film, do their brains respond in similar ways? You might expect everyone's brain to do its own thing. After all, each of us has different memories, different tastes and different thoughts.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Hasson, Nir, Levy, Fuhrmann & Malach | Science, 2004 | Intersubject synchronization of cortical activity during natural vision
In 2004, Uri Hasson and his colleagues scanned people's brains while they watched a section of a classic western film. When they compared different viewers, they found that large parts of their brains rose and fell in activity together, moment by moment, as the film unfolded. A good story seemed to pull different brains into a shared rhythm.

@char A12 living_room_day | The power of a story | Many minds, one rhythm
That's a beautiful idea. When you sit in a cinema, or listen to someone tell a gripping story, your brain may be moving in step with everyone else who is paying attention. For a while, many separate minds are following the same path.

@chapter A15 cafe_sunset | Speaker and listener
That raised a natural next question. What happens between two people, when one is speaking and the other is listening?

@study A04 attic_rain | Stephens, Silbert & Hasson | PNAS, 2010 | Speaker-listener neural coupling underlies successful communication
In 2010, Greg Stephens, Lauren Silbert and Uri Hasson recorded a speaker's brain activity while they told a long, unrehearsed story about something from their own life. Then they played the recording to listeners while scanning their brains, and compared the two.

@char A03 cafe_sunset | Coupled brains | The listener followed the speaker
They found that the listeners' brain activity echoed the speaker's, usually with a short delay, as if the listener's brain was following just behind. The researchers called this neural coupling. And in some areas, the listener's brain activity even ran slightly ahead of the speaker's, as if the listener was anticipating what would come next.

@char A12 living_room_day | Understanding | More coupling, more understanding
Here's the key result. The more strongly a listener's brain was coupled with the speaker's, the better that listener understood the story, as measured by a test afterwards. When the story was told in a language the listeners didn't understand, the coupling largely disappeared. Understanding someone seemed to involve, quite literally, getting in step with their brain.

@char A10 attic_rain | A gentle image | Two minds moving together
I find that a moving image. When someone tells you something important and you really listen, your mind isn't just receiving their words. It's moving alongside theirs, a fraction of a second behind, sometimes even a fraction ahead. Being understood might feel so good partly because, in some measurable way, someone is right there with you.

@chapter A13 rooftop_dusk | Brains of friends
So brains can fall into step with each other in the moment. But what about friends? Are the brains of people who choose each other more alike to begin with?

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Parkinson, Kleinbaum & Wheatley | Nature Communications, 2018 | Similar neural responses predict friendship
In 2018, Carolyn Parkinson, Adam Kleinbaum and Thalia Wheatley studied a group of graduate students who all knew each other to some degree. First, they mapped the social network of the whole class, who was friends with whom, and who was a friend of a friend. Then they scanned some of the students while they watched a series of varied video clips.

@char A03 cafe_sunset | The pattern | Friends responded alike
The clips ranged from comedy to science to emotional scenes, the kind of material where people's reactions might differ. When the researchers compared the brain responses, they found that friends had the most similar patterns of activity. People who were further apart in the social network tended to have less similar responses.

@char A12 living_room_day | What it might mean | Seeing the world in a similar way
The researchers suggested that friends may tend to see and interpret the world in similar ways, attending to the same things, reacting in similar ways to what they see. It's a study that can't show which comes first. Perhaps we choose friends who think like us. Perhaps friends grow more alike over time. Probably a bit of both.

@char A10 cabin_fireplace | A familiar feeling | That's exactly what I thought
Either way, it gives scientific weight to a very familiar feeling. You're watching something with a close friend, and at exactly the same moment you both turn to each other, because you both noticed the same tiny detail. You didn't need to say anything. You were already seeing it the same way.

@chapter A15 cafe_sunset | A classroom of brains
Scanning machines are big and noisy, so most of these studies happen in a lab. But one study took a lighter kind of brain recording out into the real world.

@study A04 attic_rain | Dikker and colleagues | Current Biology, 2017 | Brain-to-brain synchrony tracks real-world dynamic group interactions in the classroom
In 2017, Suzanne Dikker and her colleagues fitted a group of high school students in New York with portable headsets that recorded the electrical activity of their brains. They recorded the students during their normal biology lessons, across a whole semester.

@char A03 cafe_sunset | In sync in class | Engagement and closeness
When students were more engaged in a lesson, their brain activity tended to be more in sync with each other. And there was a lovely detail. Pairs of students who had spent a little face-to-face time together just before class tended to show more synchrony with each other during the lesson. A few minutes of connection seemed to carry over into how their brains moved through the class together.

@list A12 living_room_day | What the brain studies suggest | Good stories pull minds into a shared rhythm ;; Listeners couple with speakers they understand ;; Friends respond to the world in similar ways ;; Engagement and closeness go with synchrony
So, being on the same wavelength. It's not quite a figure of speech after all. Good stories pull brains into a shared rhythm. Listeners couple with speakers they understand. Friends tend to respond to the world in similar ways. And engagement and closeness both seem to go hand in hand with brains that move together.

@photo lf22_b04_01.jpg | Shared attention
It's worth adding a careful note. These are fascinating findings, but they're early. Brain synchrony is not a magic measure of love or friendship, and many things affect it. Still, it's a striking thought, that when we feel deeply connected to someone, something in our brains may really be moving in time with theirs.

@char A10 attic_rain | Being listened to | Why it feels so good
Perhaps that's part of why being truly listened to feels so powerful. When someone gives you their full attention, their mind follows yours. You can feel it, even if you can't see it. And that sense of someone moving with you, step by step, may be one of the deepest kinds of connection there is.

Brains moving together is invisible. But there's a much more visible way people fall into step with each other, and we all do it without noticing. We copy each other. Our postures, our gestures, even our way of speaking. Is that a sign of connection, or of something fake? That's next.
