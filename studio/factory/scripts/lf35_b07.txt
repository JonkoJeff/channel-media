@chapter A15 cafe_sunset | The myth of the big highs
Think about the happiest people you can imagine. What do their lives look like? For many of us, the picture is full of fireworks. Big trips, big parties, big moments of joy that light up the sky. The happiest life, we assume, must be the one with the most intense highs. It's a very natural idea. And it's our next myth.

@char A02 living_room_day | Nora's first night | The brightest moment
Nora's brightest moment with the window came on her very first night. She sat on the floor among the boxes, a sandwich in her lap, and watched the sun go down over the water. It was almost too beautiful. She remembers thinking, this is it, this is what happiness feels like. And for a while afterwards, she measured every evening against that one.

@study A18 cafe_sunset | Ed Diener, Ed Sandvik and William Pavot | University of Illinois, published 1991 | Happiness is the frequency, not the intensity, of positive feelings
In 1991, the psychologist Ed Diener and his colleagues Ed Sandvik and William Pavot published a chapter with a title that says it all. Happiness is the frequency, not the intensity, of positive versus negative feelings. It appeared in a book on subjective well-being, and it gathered evidence from studies in which people reported on their feelings, often again and again over many days.

@list A12 living_room_day | What they found | How often you feel good predicts well-being best ;; How intensely you feel good adds much less ;; Small, frequent pleasures beat rare, dazzling ones
What they found was quietly radical. How often people felt good was a much better guide to their overall well-being than how intensely they felt good. People who were happy most of the time, in a mild and steady way, were the ones who described themselves as happy overall. The dazzling peaks added surprisingly little.

@char A13 cabin_fireplace | Why intensity is tricky | Highs cast shadows
Diener and his colleagues also pointed to a reason why intense highs may not help as much as we'd think. Intense positive moments are rare. And when you've just had one, the ordinary days that follow can look a little grey beside it. A very bright light makes everything around it seem darker. The high doesn't just fade. It can quietly lower the shine of what comes next.

@char A19 attic_rain | Nora's grey evenings | Measured against the best
That was exactly Nora's problem. After that first glowing night, the ordinary evenings, with cloud over the water or rain on the glass, felt like a disappointment. Not because they were bad, but because she kept holding them up against the best evening of all. Her happiness hadn't vanished. It had been outshone by a memory.

And yet, look at an evening like this one. A grey sea, a cup of tea, a little rain on the window. Nothing glittering. Nothing to post or to tell anyone about. But if the research is right, it's evenings like this, many of them, gently pleasant, that make up most of a happy life.

@chapter A05 autumn_overlook | How we remember happiness
There's a second half to this myth, and it's about memory. Because the way we remember good and bad experiences is not the way we lived them. Our memories keep some moments and quietly drop others. And one of the most famous demonstrations of this involves a bowl of very cold water.

@study A18 autumn_overlook | Kahneman, Fredrickson, Schreiber and Redelmeier | Published 1993, Psychological Science | When more pain is preferred to less
In 1993, Daniel Kahneman, Barbara Fredrickson, Charles Schreiber and Donald Redelmeier published a study in Psychological Science. Volunteers held one hand in cold water, which is uncomfortable but harmless. It became known as the cold hand study, and it showed something about memory that almost nobody expected.

@stat A13 cabin_fireplace | Cold water trial | 14°C | for 60 seconds, then an extra 30 seconds slightly warmer
In one trial, a hand went into water at fourteen degrees Celsius for sixty seconds. In another trial, the hand stayed in the same cold water for sixty seconds, and then for an extra thirty seconds while the water was warmed by about one degree. Still cold, just a little less so. So the second trial contained all of the first one's discomfort, plus more.

@char A10 living_room_day | Which would you repeat? | The surprising answer
Then the volunteers were asked which of the two trials they would rather repeat. Logically, the shorter one should win. It had less discomfort in total. But most of the volunteers chose to repeat the longer trial. The one with more cold, but a gentler ending. Their memory of the experience had been shaped not by its total, but by how it ended.

@list A12 cafe_sunset | The peak-end rule | Memory leans on the most intense moment ;; Memory leans on the final moment ;; Length matters surprisingly little
This pattern is called the peak end rule. When we look back on an experience, our memory tends to lean on two moments in particular. The most intense moment, the peak. And the final moment, the end. How long the experience lasted matters surprisingly little. Kahneman called that last part duration neglect.

@list A20 living_room_day | You may know this feeling | A holiday remembered by its last day ;; A film judged by its final scene ;; An evening coloured by how it ended
You may recognise this from your own life. A holiday that's remembered mostly by its last day, good or bad. A film that's judged almost entirely by its final scene. A lovely evening that feels a little spoiled, in memory, because of one sharp word just before everyone went home. The middle was fine. But the ending wrote the story.

@study A18 cafe_sunset | Donald Redelmeier and Daniel Kahneman | Published 1996, Pain | Patients' memories of medical procedures
The same idea was later studied with real medical procedures. In 1996, Donald Redelmeier and Daniel Kahneman published a study in the journal Pain, in which patients rated their discomfort moment by moment during a colonoscopy. Afterwards, their overall memory of the procedure was linked to the worst moment and to the last few minutes, far more than to how long it had lasted.

@chapter A20 living_room_day | The James Dean effect
And this strange way of remembering doesn't only apply to short experiences. It can even shape how we judge a whole life.

@study A13 cabin_fireplace | Ed Diener, Derrick Wirtz and Shigehiro Oishi | Published 2001, Psychological Science | End effects of rated life quality
In 2001, Ed Diener, Derrick Wirtz and Shigehiro Oishi published a study they called the James Dean effect, after the young film star whose life ended suddenly at its height. People read short descriptions of imaginary lives. Some lives were wonderful, and ended suddenly. Others were the same wonderful life, with a few extra years that were pleasant, but a little less wonderful, added at the end.

@char A19 attic_rain | A strange judgement | Less can seem like more
And people tended to rate the shorter, purely wonderful life as more desirable than the same life with some extra, mildly pleasant years. Logically, those extra years were still good years. Adding them should have made the life better, not worse. But the ending coloured the whole. It's as if we judge a life the way we judge a story, by its final chapter, rather than adding up every good day along the way. And the same quiet bias seems to shape how we look back on a summer, a friendship, or a year in a new home.

@char A10 cabin_fireplace | What it means for Nora | And for us
So here's how these two ideas fit together. In daily life, it's the frequent, gentle pleasures that seem to matter most. But in memory, it's the peaks and the endings. Nora's first night by the window became a peak, and her mind kept comparing everything to it. That's a natural trick of memory. It just isn't a fair picture of her life.

@list A05 autumn_overlook | Gentle lessons | Look for frequent small pleasures ;; Don't let one peak outshine every ordinary day ;; Give good experiences a good ending
There are some gentle lessons here. Look for small pleasures that can happen often, rather than waiting for rare spectacular ones. Try not to let one bright peak outshine every ordinary day. And when you can, give good experiences a good ending. A slow last walk at the end of a holiday, a warm goodbye, a quiet cup of tea at the end of an evening.

@char A13 cabin_fireplace | Nora's new ending | A few minutes by the glass
So Nora began to give her own evenings a better ending. Nothing grand. Just a few quiet minutes by the window before bed, with the lamp turned low, looking out at whatever the sea happened to be doing. Sometimes it was silver. Sometimes it was black, with a single light far out on the water. She stopped asking whether it was as beautiful as that first night. She simply let each evening end gently, and found that the days began to feel kinder in memory.

@chapter A15 cafe_sunset | Breaks and pauses
But if frequent, gentle pleasures are what matter, there's still a problem. Frequent pleasures fade fastest of all. The more often we do something nice, the more ordinary it becomes. So is there any way to keep a good thing feeling fresh? Surprisingly, the answer may involve doing something most of us would never choose. Interrupting it.
