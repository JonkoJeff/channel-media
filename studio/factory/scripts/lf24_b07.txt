@chapter A01 cafe_sunset | What happens when people quit
If surveys can't tell us what causes what, what can? The answer is an experiment. And for social media, the most direct one is to take it away from a group of people, leave it with another group, and compare. Over the last few years, several teams have done exactly that. The results are some of the most informative evidence we have.

@char A12 living_room_day | The experimental logic | Random assignment
The key is random assignment. If you ask people who choose to quit how they feel, you've learned very little, because people who quit may differ from people who don't. If you randomly choose who quits and who stays, any consistent difference afterwards is likely to be caused by the break itself.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Tromholt | Cyberpsychology, Behavior, and Social Networking, 2016 | The Facebook experiment: quitting Facebook leads to higher levels of well-being
Morten Tromholt, a Danish researcher, recruited one thousand and ninety-five people and randomly asked half of them to stop using Facebook for a week. The other half carried on as usual. At the end, those who had quit reported higher life satisfaction and more positive emotions than the control group. The effect was larger for people who were heavy users, who often felt envy, and who used the site passively.

@stat A14 conspiracy_board | Participants in the Danish Facebook experiment | 1,095 | randomly assigned to one week with or without Facebook
This was an influential study, and it matched the passive use story from the previous chapters. But it had weaknesses. People knew whether they were in the group that quit, and they knew what the study was about. That raises the possibility that some of the improvement came from expecting to feel better. Better-controlled studies were needed.

@chapter A06 cafe_sunset | A longer, larger test
The most ambitious experiment so far came in 2018, in the weeks before the American midterm elections. A team of economists, led by Hunt Allcott, Luca Braghieri, Sarah Eichmeyer and Matthew Gentzkow, recruited a large group of Facebook users and paid some of them to deactivate their accounts for four weeks. They also checked that those people really did stay off.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Allcott, Braghieri, Eichmeyer & Gentzkow | American Economic Review, 2020 | The welfare effects of social media
They enrolled two thousand seven hundred and forty-three users and found that those who deactivated spent more time on other activities, such as watching television and spending time with family and friends, and had less knowledge of the news. They also reported small improvements in subjective wellbeing, about nine hundredths of a standard deviation, which the authors put at roughly a quarter to two-fifths of the effect that other studies find for psychological interventions such as therapy.

@list A03 living_room_day | What the deactivation study found | More time with family and friends ;; Less time spent on news ;; Small gain in subjective wellbeing ;; Less political polarisation on some measures ;; Less Facebook use after the experiment ended
The last point is intriguing. After the four weeks were over, people who had been asked to deactivate used the site less than before, which suggests that the break helped them see how they wanted to use it. Many of them said afterwards that they valued the experience.

@char A04 attic_rain | A careful reading | Real, but small
Notice how modest the wellbeing effect was. It was statistically clear, which with so many participants is easy to achieve. But it wasn't large in everyday terms. It's best to think of it as a measurable shift, not a transformation. And it applied to Facebook users in a particular American election period, so we should be careful about generalising.

@chapter A13 rooftop_dusk | Limiting rather than quitting
Most people don't want to quit entirely, and it would be unrealistic to expect them to. So another team asked whether limiting use might help. In 2018, Melissa Hunt and her colleagues at the University of Pennsylvania designed an experiment around a very practical rule: ten minutes per platform per day.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Hunt, Marx, Lipson & Young | Journal of Social and Clinical Psychology, 2018 | No more FOMO: limiting social media decreases loneliness and depression
They recruited one hundred and forty-three undergraduates, and randomly asked half of them to limit Facebook, Instagram and Snapchat to ten minutes each per day for three weeks, while the others carried on as usual. The group that limited their use showed significant reductions in loneliness and depression over those weeks, compared with the control group, particularly those who were more depressed at the start.

@stat A14 conspiracy_board | Undergraduates in the Penn experiment | 143 | 10 minutes per platform per day for three weeks
This study attracted a lot of attention, and it also drew criticism. It was small, it relied on self-reports, and the control group's wellbeing also improved a little, possibly because everyone became more aware of their use. As often happens, the sensible conclusion is cautious: limited use seems to help, at least for some people, but the size of the effect is uncertain.

@list A12 living_room_day | The shape of the evidence | Breaks and limits often help a little ;; Effects are bigger for heavy or struggling users ;; Many studies are short and self-reported ;; Few study what happens over years
That's an honest description of where the experimental work stands. It is more encouraging than the survey data, in that it finds some effect. And it's more modest than the headlines, in that the effect is small.

@chapter A12 living_room_day | The role of expectation
There's another reason for caution. In the quitting experiments, people know what they're being asked to do. They may feel virtuous, hopeful or proud. And expectation can improve how people feel on its own, which psychologists call a placebo effect. Some researchers have tested how much of the result might come from that.

@char A01 cafe_sunset | An idea to test | Does believing matter?
In several studies, people were told that reducing social media would make them feel better, or worse, or nothing. The results suggest that what people believe about the effects can shape what they report, at least a little. This doesn't make the findings meaningless. It means that part of what's measured as an effect might be what people expected to feel.

@char A04 attic_rain | A reasonable conclusion | A small, real gain
Putting it all together, a fair conclusion is that taking a break from social media gives many people a small, real improvement in mood and time use, part of which may come from expectation, and that there's more benefit for people who use it heavily and passively. That's no reason to stop for ever. It's a reason to give yourself an occasional honest test.

@list A03 living_room_day | A two-week test you could run | Note how you feel at the end of each day for a week ;; Cut your use to a fixed limit for two weeks ;; Keep a note of mood, sleep and time ;; Compare, and decide for yourself
You are, in a sense, the best scientist of your own life. A personal experiment doesn't have the controls of a randomised trial. But it asks the right question, which is how this particular habit changes your particular days.

A calendar with some days crossed out, and a phone lying quietly on its face. The experiments suggest that stepping away, even briefly, can change how a day feels, not dramatically, but in a way that many people notice.

@list A03 living_room_day | Running a fair personal experiment | Pick one change, not five ;; Keep a short daily note of mood and sleep ;; Run it for two weeks ;; Review it before deciding what to keep
If you try a break or a limit, treat it like a real experiment. Change one thing at a time, so that you know what caused any difference. Write down a few words each evening, about mood, sleep and energy, so that you aren't relying on memory, which smooths out the details. And decide in advance how long you'll try it. Scientists call this preregistering a plan, and it protects against the temptation to judge the result by how you hoped it would turn out. A fair experiment, even a tiny one, teaches more than a hundred opinions.

@list A12 living_room_day | Words to keep from this chapter | Randomised trial ;; Control group ;; Placebo effect ;; Replication
Four terms from the quitting studies. A randomised trial assigns people to groups by chance. A control group carries on as normal, for comparison. The placebo effect is an improvement that comes from expecting one. And replication means repeating a study to see whether the result holds.

@char A15 cabin_fireplace | A gentle thought | A break is not a punishment
If you try a break, try it kindly. It isn't a test of willpower and it isn't a punishment. It's an experiment about what you do with your attention when it's free. You may be surprised by how much room there is, and by what moves into it.

@char A08 cabin_fireplace | Looking back | What the breaks taught us
So what did we learn? That randomised experiments are the best way to test cause and effect. That one week and four weeks off Facebook gave small improvements. That limiting use to thirty minutes a day helped some students. And that expectation, and the small size of the effects, call for modesty.
