@chapter A13 cabin_fireplace | Cats and X-rays
Let's travel back more than a hundred years, to Harvard Medical School in Boston. A young researcher called Walter Cannon is doing something that sounds almost like science fiction for the time. He's using the brand new technology of X-rays to watch digestion happen, live, inside the bodies of animals.

@char A10 living_room_day | A moving stomach | Watched on a screen
Cannon gave animals a meal mixed with a substance that showed up on an X-ray screen. Then he could watch the stomach work, squeezing and churning in slow, steady waves. It was beautiful, careful, rather patient research. And then he noticed something he hadn't planned to study at all.

@char A03 cafe_sunset | The waves stop | When the cat was upset
When an animal became frightened or distressed, the waves in its stomach simply stopped. Digestion paused, as if someone had flicked a switch. Calm the animal down, and after a while the waves returned. Cannon realised that emotion wasn't just something in the mind. It reached all the way down into the gut.

@study A01 cafe_sunset | Walter Cannon | Harvard Medical School, 1915 | Bodily Changes in Pain, Hunger, Fear and Rage
Cannon followed that clue for years. In 1915, he published a book with a wonderfully direct title. Bodily Changes in Pain, Hunger, Fear and Rage. In it, he described what happens to the body in an emergency, and he gave us a phrase that you still hear every day. Fight or flight.

@list A12 living_room_day | The emergency response | Adrenaline from the adrenal glands ;; More sugar in the blood ;; Blood sent to the muscles ;; Digestion put on hold
Cannon showed that in an emergency, the adrenal glands, two small glands sitting on top of the kidneys, release adrenaline into the blood. Sugar pours into the bloodstream as quick fuel. Blood is sent towards the muscles. And anything that can wait, like digesting breakfast, is put on hold. The whole body is reorganised, in moments, for one purpose. To fight, or to run.

@char A15 autumn_overlook | A sensible design | For a dangerous world
When you think about it, it's a sensible design. If a predator is coming towards you, digesting your lunch is the least of your worries. What you need is fuel, strength and speed, right now. Everything else can be sorted out later, if there is a later.

@chapter A05 cabin_fireplace | The wisdom of the body
Cannon had a second big idea, and it's just as important for our story. He was fascinated by how steady the inside of the body stays, while the world outside keeps changing. Hot days, cold nights, big meals, long fasts. And yet, inside, things stay remarkably constant.

@stat A20 cafe_sunset | Kept remarkably steady | 37 °C | Typical core body temperature
Take your body temperature. Whether you're in a snowy street or a hot kitchen, the core of your body usually stays close to thirty seven degrees Celsius. Your blood sugar, the salt in your blood, the acidity of your blood, they all stay within narrow ranges too. The body works hard, all the time, to keep them there.

@quote A18 living_room_day | The Wisdom of the Body | Walter Cannon, 1932
In 1932, Cannon wrote a book called The Wisdom of the Body, and in it he made a word famous. Homeostasis. It means staying the same, or keeping a steady state. In his view, the fight or flight response was part of this wisdom. A short, sharp change, so that the body could get back to its steady state as soon as the danger was over.

@char A13 rooftop_dusk | Meanwhile | On the platform
Back on the platform, Jonah's stomach is doing exactly what Cannon's cats did. It has tightened and gone quiet. He had planned to grab a coffee and a pastry at the station. Now the idea of eating anything feels impossible. His body has decided, without asking him, that breakfast can wait.

@chapter A19 cabin_fireplace | Rats in Montreal
Now let's move a few hundred miles north, and a couple of decades on, to McGill University in Montreal. A young doctor called Hans Selye, born in Vienna, is hoping to discover a new hormone. He's injecting laboratory rats with an extract made from ovaries, and then carefully examining their bodies to see what changed.

@list A10 living_room_day | Three changes | Swollen adrenal glands ;; A shrunken thymus and lymph glands ;; Ulcers in the stomach
He found three striking changes. The adrenal glands, those same small glands that Cannon studied, had become swollen. The thymus and the lymph glands, part of the immune system, had shrunk. And there were bleeding ulcers in the lining of the stomach. Selye was thrilled. He thought he had found the effects of a new hormone.

@char A03 cafe_sunset | A disappointment | Or a discovery
But then he tried other injections. Extracts from other organs. Even substances that were simply irritating or poisonous. And he got the same three changes again. Then he tried cold, and injury, and forced exercise. The same three changes. Whatever he did to the rats, as long as it was harmful enough, their bodies answered in the same way.

@study A12 rooftop_dusk | Hans Selye | McGill University, Montreal, 1936 | A syndrome produced by diverse nocuous agents
For a moment, it looked like a failure. There was no new hormone. But Selye realised he had found something bigger. In 1936, he published a short letter in the journal Nature, with the title A Syndrome Produced by Diverse Nocuous Agents. Nocuous simply means harmful. Many different harms, one shared response.

@list A05 autumn_overlook | The general adaptation syndrome | Alarm ;; Resistance ;; Exhaustion
Selye later called this response the general adaptation syndrome, and he described it in three stages. First, alarm, the body's immediate reaction to the threat. Then resistance, a longer stage in which the body adapts and holds out. And finally, if the pressure goes on for too long, exhaustion, when the body's resources start to run down.

It's worth pausing on that third stage. Selye was suggesting that the very response that protects us in the short run could wear us down if it never stopped. That idea would grow and grow over the following decades. And we'll come back to it later tonight, when we look at what happens when the alarm never switches off.

@char A10 cabin_fireplace | Built for a sprint | Not a marathon
Think of it like a sprinter. A sprinter can run flat out for a few seconds, and that burst is a wonderful thing. But nobody can sprint for an hour. If you tried, your body would start to give way long before the finish. Selye's rats were, in a sense, being asked to sprint without ever being allowed to stop. And their bodies showed the cost.

@chapter A13 living_room_day | Borrowing a word
For his new idea, Selye eventually reached for a word from physics and engineering. Stress. In engineering, stress is the force that presses on a material, like the weight on a bridge or the pull on a cable. Push hard enough, for long enough, and even steel begins to bend.

@char A20 cafe_sunset | A word goes global | From labs to Mondays
Over the following decades, Selye wrote about stress again and again, in scientific papers and in popular books, like The Stress of Life in 1956. And slowly, the word escaped from the laboratory. Today, it's one of the most common words we use to describe our lives. Jonah's word, on that platform, comes from Selye's rats.

@study A18 attic_rain | John Mason | Walter Reed Army Institute of Research, 1971 | A re-evaluation of the idea that the stress response is non-specific
But Selye's picture wasn't quite complete. He believed the stress response was the same, whatever caused it. Later researchers, like the American scientist John Mason, who published a careful re-evaluation of the idea in 1971, argued that this wasn't quite right. In Mason's view, much of the response depended on how a situation felt to the animal or person. Was it new? Was it uncertain? Did they have any control over it?

@char A12 rooftop_dusk | What it feels like | Matters a lot
That small correction turns out to be enormous. It means stress isn't only about what happens to you. It's also about how your brain reads what happens to you. A late train is just a late train. It only becomes stressful when your brain links it to something that matters. A talk at nine. A boss waiting. A room full of faces.

Back in our story, the train finally pulls in, eighteen minutes late. Jonah squeezes on and finds a place to stand, one hand on the cold rail, his bag pressed against his legs. And then his phone buzzes. A message from his manager. Can we start a little earlier? Eight forty five?

In the tiny gap between reading that message and feeling his heart jump, something remarkable happens in Jonah's brain.
