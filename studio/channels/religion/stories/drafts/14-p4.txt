CHAPTER: A Meal at Corinth
We return now to where we began: Paul's letter to the Corinthians. Paul does not quote the Last Supper because he is writing a history. He quotes it because the church at Corinth has a problem with its shared meals. || 1 Corinthians 11:17–22 || text-card
The Corinthian Christians gathered to eat together, and Paul says that when they do, some go hungry while others are drunk. It sounds like the wealthy members were eating their fill first, while poorer members arrived later and found nothing left. || Corinth ancient agora ruins || wikimedia
Remember that early Christian groups met in private homes. A host with a large house could seat only a few in the dining room, and the rest would gather in the atrium. Social class shaped the seating, even in a community that preached equality. || Roman house atrium, Pompeii House of the Faun || wikimedia
Paul is angry about this. He says that when they come together, it is not really the Lord's supper that they eat. Then he reminds them of the tradition he passed on: the night Jesus was handed over, the bread, the cup, and the command to remember. || 1 Corinthians 11:20, 23–25 || text-card
The point of the story, in Paul's hands, is moral and communal. If this meal proclaims the death of Jesus, then eating it in a way that humiliates the poor contradicts its meaning. Paul asks them to wait for one another and to examine themselves. || 1 Corinthians 11:27–34 || text-card
Dennis Smith, in his book From Symposium to Eucharist, shows how much the early Christian meal resembled other banquets of the Greco-Roman world, with a formal meal followed by a drinking party and talk. The Christians gave the familiar shape a new meaning. || Dennis E. Smith, From Symposium to Eucharist || text-card
Paul Bradshaw, a historian of early liturgy, warns against reading later, tidy ritual back into the first century. In his book Eucharistic Origins he argues that early practice was varied, and that we should be careful about assuming a single original form. || Paul F. Bradshaw, Eucharistic Origins, 2004 || text-card
One valuable witness to that variety is a short early Christian manual called the Didache, probably written in the late first or early second century. It contains prayers for a communal meal, with thanksgivings over the cup and the broken bread. || Didache, Codex Hierosolymitanus || wikimedia
The Didache's prayers are striking for what they leave out. They speak of the vine of David, of life and knowledge, and of the gathering of the church from the ends of the earth, like grain gathered from scattered hills into one loaf. || Didache 9:1–4 || text-card
The Didache does not mention the words of institution, the sayings about body and blood. This has puzzled scholars. Perhaps the text reflects a community with a somewhat different tradition, or perhaps it records only some of the prayers. We simply do not know. || Early Christian meal scene, Catacomb of Priscilla, Rome || wikimedia
About the middle of the second century, a teacher named Justin Martyr wrote a defence of Christianity addressed to the Roman emperor. In it he describes a Sunday gathering with readings, a sermon, prayers, bread and wine mixed with water, and a distribution to those present. || Justin Martyr, First Apology 65–67 || text-card
Justin explains that those who share this food believe it is not ordinary bread and drink. He then recounts the words of Jesus over the bread and cup, as the apostles passed them down in the memoirs they wrote, which are called Gospels. || Justin Martyr, First Apology 66 || text-card
So within about a century and a half of the supper, we can see the story and the practice woven together. The meal at the table in Jerusalem is remembered in the Gospels, and re-lived in the weekly gathering. Each supports the other. || Early church fresco, Dura-Europos || wikimedia
Notice the change of setting as well. The meal that began as a full dinner slowly became a shorter ritual, set within a service of prayer and teaching. By the time of Justin, the sharing of bread and wine was already separate from a larger meal. || Early Christian basilica, Santa Sabina, Rome || wikimedia
That is a large transformation, and it happened gradually and unevenly across different regions. Historians trace it through scraps of evidence, a letter here, a prayer there, an image in a catacomb. The picture is still being refined. || Catacomb of Callixtus fresco, Rome || wikimedia
CHAPTER: One Meal, Many Understandings
As Christianity spread and grew, its communities developed names and understandings for this shared meal. Some called it the Eucharist, from the Greek word for thanksgiving. Others called it Communion, the Lord's Supper, or the Divine Liturgy. || Eucharist, Greek word eucharistia || text-card
In the Eastern churches, the Divine Liturgy developed rich forms of chant and prayer. In the West, the Mass took shape in Latin, with its own structure and music. Both traditions treat the central rite as the heart of Christian worship. || Hagia Sophia interior, Istanbul || wikimedia
Over the centuries, theologians asked: how are we to understand the words this is my body? The question was not new in the Middle Ages, but it became sharper. Various answers were given, and some became official teaching of particular churches. || Thomas Aquinas, Summa Theologiae, manuscript || wikimedia
In 1215, the Fourth Lateran Council used the word transubstantiation, expressing the belief that the bread and wine are changed into the body and blood of Christ while their appearance remains. This became the standard Catholic understanding. || Fourth Lateran Council, 1215, Rome || wikimedia
The Eastern Orthodox churches affirm a real change as well, but traditionally avoid defining the how, preferring to speak of mystery. The language differs, even where the conviction about a real presence is shared. || Orthodox Divine Liturgy, Byzantine icon of the Communion of the Apostles || wikimedia
In the sixteenth century, the Reformation reopened the question. Martin Luther rejected transubstantiation but held that Christ is truly present in, with and under the bread and wine. Ulrich Zwingli, in Zurich, taught that the meal is a memorial. || Martin Luther, Lucas Cranach the Elder || wikimedia
In 1529, Luther and Zwingli met at Marburg to try to settle their disagreement. They agreed on many points but could not agree on this one, and the Reformation divided over a meal that was meant to express unity. It is a painful part of the story. || Marburg Colloquy, 1529, Landgrave Philip of Hesse || wikimedia
John Calvin proposed a middle path, speaking of a real spiritual presence through the Holy Spirit. Other Protestant traditions, including many Baptist and evangelical churches, emphasised the memorial aspect, in keeping with the command to remember. || John Calvin, portrait, Geneva || wikimedia
Today, Christians still differ about how often to celebrate, who may receive, and what exactly happens. Some celebrate daily, some weekly, some a few times a year. Some use wine, others grape juice. Some use a common cup, others small individual cups. || Communion service, Methodist church, 20th century || loc
What remains shared is the gesture. Bread is taken, thanks is given, the bread is broken and shared, and a cup is passed. The words differ in detail, but the shape of the act, which the Gospels and Paul describe, runs through almost all of these traditions. || Communion of the Apostles, Fra Angelico || wikimedia
Modern ecumenical work has tried to find common ground. The document known as Baptism, Eucharist and Ministry, produced in 1982 by the World Council of Churches, was a landmark attempt to describe areas of convergence among churches. || World Council of Churches, Baptism, Eucharist and Ministry, 1982 || text-card
Convergence does not mean agreement on everything. But it does reflect a growing willingness to listen. Many scholars see the study of the earliest sources, as we have done here, as one of the places where different traditions can meet. || Ecumenical service, Taizé community || wikimedia
Meanwhile, Jewish and Christian scholars have also reflected together on the Jewish roots of the meal. Recognising that the supper belongs to a Jewish setting has helped modern Christians to appreciate their shared heritage with Judaism. || Nostra Aetate, Vatican II, 1965 || text-card
Notice how a single evening, remembered by a small group, has become a global ritual with many forms. The ritual has outlived empires and languages, and been carried to every continent, taking local shapes while preserving a core gesture. || Map of Christianity's spread, medieval Europe || map
Every tradition has its own way of understanding that gesture. Our task here is not to adjudicate between them, but to see how they grew from the same early memory. That memory, as we have seen, is already complex, layered and richly Jewish. || Old manuscript Gospel lectionary, Byzantine || wikimedia
CHAPTER: The Painting and the Memory
In the 1490s, in the refectory of the convent of Santa Maria delle Grazie in Milan, Leonardo da Vinci painted what would become the most famous image of the Last Supper. He worked on a long wall, where the monks ate their own meals under it. || Santa Maria delle Grazie refectory, Milan || wikimedia
Leonardo chose a particular moment: the instant after Jesus says one of you will betray me. The disciples react in groups of three, with gestures of shock, protest and question rippling outward from the centre. || The Last Supper, Leonardo da Vinci, 1495–1498 || wikimedia
Notice what he does not paint. There is no lamb on the table, no Passover setting, no Hebrew prayer. The scene is shaped by Renaissance ideas of drama and perspective, and the table is long and Italian, not a first-century Jewish couch arrangement. || Study for Last Supper, Leonardo, Royal Collection || wikimedia
This is not a criticism. Every age paints the Last Supper in its own image. Byzantine artists, medieval mosaicists and Baroque painters each placed the table in their own world, and each reveals how the story was imagined and felt. || Last Supper, Byzantine mosaic, Sant'Apollinare Nuovo, Ravenna || wikimedia
Leonardo's painting has also had a troubled physical life. He experimented with a new technique, painting on dry plaster, rather than traditional fresco, and the paint began to flake within his own lifetime. Later damage, including bombing in 1943, added to the problem. || The Last Supper, Leonardo, before restoration || wikimedia
A long restoration, completed in 1999, removed layers of earlier repainting and revealed more of the original surface. Yet much of what we see is reconstructed. The painting is, in a sense, a memory of a memory, carefully tended. || Last Supper restoration, Milan, 1999 || wikimedia
That seems an apt image for our story. The Last Supper has reached us as a set of remembered words and gestures, reshaped by teachers, copyists, painters and communities. We can study its layers, but we cannot step back into the room. || Detail of Last Supper, Leonardo, hands || wikimedia
What can we say with reasonable confidence? That Jesus shared a final meal with his followers in Jerusalem around Passover time. That the meal was linked, in the earliest tradition, with bread and a cup and a reference to a covenant. That one close follower handed him over. || Jerusalem from Mount of Olives, 19th century photograph || loc
What remains uncertain? The exact date, the precise wording, the shape of the room and the full guest list. These are the questions on which the sources leave us wondering, and on which scholars continue to work, with care and humility. || Jerusalem Old City aerial view || wikimedia
There is also what lies beyond history: the meaning that millions of people find in the meal. That is not a question the historian can settle, and this video has not tried to. It has tried only to listen to the oldest voices, and to hear how they differ. || Candle in a church, soft light || wikimedia
Picture again the room we began with. The lamps are low, the bread has been broken, and the cup has passed from hand to hand. The singing starts, and the group steps into the street, down toward the valley and the olive trees. || Kidron Valley at dusk, Jerusalem || wikimedia
The meal was over. The night was only beginning. And the story of what came next, the arrest, the trials and the cross, is where we will go in the next episode of Belief Through Time. || Old olive tree, Garden of Gethsemane || wikimedia
