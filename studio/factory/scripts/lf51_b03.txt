@chapter N27 cabin_fireplace | Alone in a room
Now, let's visit that plain little room, because what happened inside it became one of the most talked-about psychology experiments of the last decade.

@char N20 living_room_day | A simple setup | Just think
The study was published in the journal Science in July 2014. Its lead author was Timothy Wilson of the University of Virginia, and its title was a gentle instruction, Just Think: The Challenges of the Disengaged Mind. The idea was beautifully simple. Take ordinary people, put them in an empty room, and ask them to do one thing, which was to entertain themselves with their own thoughts.

@study N10 cafe_sunset | Wilson et al., 2014 | University of Virginia and Harvard, published in Science | Participants sat alone, with no phone, book or pen, for six to fifteen minutes, and rated how much they enjoyed it
Here is how it went in the main experiments. Participants were asked to sit alone in a plain room, with no phone, no book, no pen and no music, for somewhere between six and fifteen minutes. They had been asked to think about whatever they liked, and then to say how enjoyable and how hard it had been.

@char N09 living_room_day | The results | Not much fun
The average verdict was not flattering. Many people said it was hard to concentrate, that their minds wandered, and that they didn't enjoy it much. This held across different age groups and across different settings, including a university lab and a group of people from the community. Thinking alone for a few minutes was, for most, a mild trial.

@char N19 street_day | The twist | A shock button
Then came the twist, and the reason the study became famous. In one experiment, the researchers first gave participants a mild electric shock, and asked how much they'd pay to avoid being shocked again. Many said they would pay to avoid it. Then each person was left alone in the room for fifteen minutes, with a button they could press to give themselves the shock, if they felt like it.

@char N10 rooftop_dusk | What happened | They pressed it
Many pressed it. Among the participants who had said they'd pay to avoid the shock, a substantial share chose to deliver one to themselves, simply because the quiet had become hard to bear. Some of them pressed the button more than once.

@bars N02 cabin_fireplace | Gave themselves a shock | % of participants | Men:67 ;; Women:25
The numbers were striking. About two thirds of the men, twelve of eighteen, pressed the button at least once, and about a quarter of the women, six of twenty-four, did the same. One man pressed it one hundred and ninety times. The study was not claiming that men are more prone to this in general, since the groups were small, but the overall pattern got a great deal of attention.

@char N09 attic_rain | A careful reading | What it does and doesn't show
It's worth being careful about what this does and doesn't show. The shock was mild, chosen by each participant to be unpleasant but not painful. The participants knew exactly what it was. And the researchers themselves did not conclude that people hate their own thoughts. Their point was narrower, that most of us find it surprisingly hard to be content with only our thoughts, when we are told to sit still and do nothing.

@quote N27 rooftop_dusk | Many people prefer to do something rather than nothing, even if the something is negative. | Wilson and colleagues, summarising the results in Science, 2014
Their own summary is fairly modest. Many people, they wrote, seem to prefer doing something rather than nothing, even when the something is negative. That is the finding in a sentence. And it fits a great many everyday experiences, from tapping at a phone in a lift to picking at a loose thread while we wait.

@char N10 cafe_sunset | At home | A cheating test
The team also asked people to try the same thing at home, for about fifteen minutes, with no phone, no tablet and no distraction. Roughly a third admitted that they cheated, by getting up, checking a phone or listening to music. That is probably an underestimate, since some who cheated might not have confessed. And remember, nobody was forcing them. It was a quiet game of sitting still, and a third couldn't quite do it.

@char N19 living_room_day | Why it matters | A window into the mind
Why did this small, odd experiment cause so much interest? Because it hints at something about how our minds are built. It suggests that attention wants a task, and that when none is available, the mind can feel more uncomfortable than we'd expect. The silence itself isn't the problem. The wanting to engage, with nothing to engage with, is.

@char N10 cabin_fireplace | The pleasant thought test | Another finding
There were other findings too. People who were told to think about something pleasant, or to plan something enjoyable, didn't find it much easier. And participants who were given a chance to do something even mildly engaging, like reading or listening to something, enjoyed their time more. The mind, it seems, would rather have a handhold.

@char N09 rooftop_dusk | Back to Mira | The fourth reading
So the fourth reading of the hand-washing poster begins to make a little more sense. Mira isn't being silly, and she isn't uniquely restless. She is doing what many of the people in that room did, reaching for any little thing that would give her attention something to hold.

@char N09 cabin_fireplace | A wider echo | Everyday versions
You can find small versions of the same pattern all around you. People tap their phones in lifts, pick at labels, tidy things that were already tidy, or wander into the kitchen without being hungry. None of this is as dramatic as pressing a shock button, but it comes from the same place, a mind that would rather do something than nothing.

@char N10 living_room_day | A gentle thought | Why this isn't a flaw
It would be easy to read this as a criticism of people, as though we were all simply unable to relax. But a mind that seeks something to do has served our species well. Curiosity, exploration and invention all grow from a mind that doesn't like to sit still. The same trait that makes a quiet room uncomfortable also makes us restless explorers.

@char N02 living_room_day | A question | Is that really all it is?
But a sceptical listener might ask whether the shock button was really about boredom. Maybe the participants were just curious about what the shock would feel like. Maybe they wanted to feel something. Those are fair questions, and later researchers tested them.

@char N19 cafe_sunset | Good science | Replication matters
That's how good science works. A striking result isn't the end of the conversation, it's the start. Other teams wanted to know if the finding would hold up, whether it was really boredom, and what exactly people were trying to escape. The next chapter of the story has some surprising answers.

@char N10 living_room_day | A thought | The mind wants something
For now, hold on to one image. A person, a plain chair, a quiet room and a small black button. The question the experiment asks isn't really about the button. It's about what it feels like to be left alone with a mind that wants to do something, and has nothing to do.
