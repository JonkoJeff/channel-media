CHAPTER: Visions, Hope and Other Explanations
If we set aside the question of whether the resurrection happened, there remains a historical question: what led the first followers to say that it did? Scholars have proposed several answers, and it is worth laying them out fairly. They are not all compatible, and none has convinced everyone. || Appearance of Christ to Mary Magdalene, Alexander Ivanov || wikimedia
One explanation is that the disciples had real experiences, visions in which they believed they saw Jesus, perhaps shaped by grief and by their expectation of vindication. Psychologists have noted that bereaved people sometimes report vivid experiences of a lost loved one. The scholar Gerd Lüdemann argued this view, and Dale Allison has discussed it carefully. || Dale Allison, Resurrecting Jesus, 2005 || text-card
A second explanation is that the story grew over time, from a belief that Jesus was vindicated by God into a belief that he had been raised bodily. On this view, the empty tomb stories developed later, as the community expressed its faith in narrative form. John Dominic Crossan has argued in this direction. || John Dominic Crossan, Jesus: A Revolutionary Biography, 1994 || text-card
A third explanation is that the body was moved or lost, by the authorities, by Joseph of Arimathea, or by someone else, and that the disciples mistook an empty tomb for a sign. This is essentially the old claim that Matthew mentions, though modern writers state it more cautiously. It has few defenders among historians today, but it is part of the history of the debate. || Matthew 28:13, Ottheinrich Bible || wikimedia
SHORT: What Convinced the First Christians?
A fourth view, held by N. T. Wright and many other Christian scholars, is that nothing less than a real event can explain the way the movement started: the empty tomb together with appearances of the risen Jesus. They do not claim that history alone can prove it, but that it is the best explanation of the evidence. || The Resurrection, Raphael, Vatican || wikimedia
Notice that these are not simply disagreements between believers and unbelievers. Some Christian scholars have been cautious about reading the stories as plain reports. Some non-Christian scholars, including the Jewish writer Pinchas Lapide, have said that they accept the disciples had a real experience of the risen Jesus, even though they do not draw the Christian conclusion. || Pinchas Lapide, The Resurrection of Jesus: A Jewish Perspective, 1983 || text-card
What historians can all agree on is the result. A small group of frightened followers, whose leader had been executed, became within weeks a movement that proclaimed him alive. They were willing to face hostility, and some of them, according to the early traditions, died for that claim. That change is the data any explanation has to meet. || Pentecost, El Greco, Prado || wikimedia
CHAPTER: Forty Days and the Ascension
Luke is the only writer to tell us that the appearances went on for a period. In the book of Acts he says that Jesus presented himself alive to the apostles by many proofs over forty days, speaking to them about the kingdom of God. The number forty has deep echoes in scripture: forty years in the wilderness, forty days on Sinai. || Acts 1:1-3 || text-card
At the end of that time, Acts says that he led them to the Mount of Olives, and as they watched, he was lifted up, and a cloud took him out of their sight. Two men in white stood beside them and asked why they stood looking into heaven, and promised that he would come again in the same way. || Ascension, Mount of Olives, Chapel of the Ascension || wikimedia
The ascension is celebrated by Christians forty days after Easter, and it points to a conviction that runs through the whole New Testament: that Jesus is now with God, and that his followers wait for his return. Paul's earliest letters already speak of waiting for the Son from heaven. || Ascension of Christ, John Singleton Copley || wikimedia
Notice that this story is told only by Luke. Matthew ends on the mountain in Galilee, and John with the shore of the lake. Mark, in its earliest form, stops at the tomb. The authors seem to have felt free to close the story where it served their own message. || Gospel writers, Four Evangelists, mosaic || wikimedia
In the creeds, the ascension is a statement about the exaltation of Jesus rather than a report about the sky. Many theologians have said that the ancient picture of going up is a way to speak about presence rather than distance, and that the point is that Christ is no longer limited to one place. || Christ Pantocrator, Saint Catherine's Monastery, Sinai || wikimedia
CHAPTER: Pentecost and a Movement That Would Not Die
The book of Acts continues the story where Luke's Gospel ends. After the ascension, the apostles wait in Jerusalem. On the day of Pentecost, fifty days after Passover, they are gathered when a sound like a rushing wind fills the house, and tongues like flames rest on each of them. || Pentecost, Jean Restout, Louvre || wikimedia
Acts says that they begin to speak in other languages, and that the Jews from many nations who had come to Jerusalem for the festival hear them in their own tongues. Peter stands up and addresses the crowd, saying that God has raised this Jesus to life, and that they are witnesses of it. || Acts 2:1-24 || text-card
Luke says that about three thousand people were baptized that day. Historians doubt that such numbers can be taken literally, but they agree that Jerusalem was the center of the new movement, and that it began to spread quickly. Within twenty years there were communities in Antioch, Corinth, Philippi and Rome. || Map: Spread of early Christianity, first century || map
Some of the earliest evidence that the movement continued comes from outside it. Around the year one hundred and twelve, Pliny the Younger, governor of Bithynia, wrote to the emperor Trajan about Christians in his province. They gathered before dawn on a fixed day, he reported, and sang a hymn to Christ as to a god. || Pliny the Younger, Letter to Trajan 10.96 || text-card
Tacitus, whom we met in our last episode, says that the superstition was checked for the moment, but broke out again, not only in Judea but even in Rome. The very fact that a Roman senator thought it worth mentioning shows how quickly the movement had grown from a small band in Galilee. || Tacitus, Annals 15.44 || text-card
Early Christians called the first day of the week the Lord's day, and gathered on it to remember Jesus, which is one reason why Sunday became the Christian day of worship. In that sense, every week in the Christian calendar is a small Easter, and the year itself has a great one at its center. || Early Christian worship, Dura-Europos house church || wikimedia
CHAPTER: Witnesses Who Paid
The early sources also report that belief in the resurrection was costly. Acts says that Stephen was stoned in Jerusalem after speaking of seeing Jesus at the right hand of God, and that the apostle James, the brother of John, was executed by Herod Agrippa in the early forties. || Martyrdom of Saint Stephen, Rembrandt || wikimedia
Josephus reports the death of James, the brother of Jesus, in the year sixty-two, at the hands of the high priest Ananus. Tacitus says that Nero blamed the Christians of Rome for the great fire in the year sixty-four, and that a great multitude was condemned, many put to death in cruel ways. || Tacitus, Annals 15.44 || text-card
The letter known as First Clement, written from Rome around the year ninety-six, mentions the sufferings of Peter and Paul. Later tradition says that both died in Rome under Nero, Peter on a cross, and Paul by the sword. Historians regard the outline as likely, but the details come from later sources. || First Clement 5, Rome, about 96 || text-card
What does this prove? It does not prove that the resurrection happened, since people have died for many beliefs, true and false. What it does show is that the first Christians were sincere. They were not telling a story for gain, and whatever they experienced, they were convinced it was real. || Martyrdom of Peter, Caravaggio, Cerasi Chapel || wikimedia
That sincerity is one reason why the theory of deliberate fraud has been abandoned by almost all modern historians. The serious debate is about what the disciples experienced and how to explain it, not about whether they were lying. || Early Christian catacomb, Rome || wikimedia
CHAPTER: Other Voices: Jewish and Muslim Traditions
We have been following the Christian story, but it has always been told alongside other voices. In the Jewish tradition, the resurrection of Jesus has not been accepted. Jews believe in a future resurrection of the dead, but not in the idea that Jesus is the Messiah or divine, and that disagreement is as old as the Gospel of Matthew. || Synagogue scroll, Torah, Jewish Museum || wikimedia
Over the centuries, there have been painful episodes in which Christians used the story to blame Jews, which the Catholic Church formally repudiated in the declaration Nostra Aetate in 1965. Today many Jewish and Christian scholars work together on the first-century evidence, each tradition reading it according to its own faith. || Nostra Aetate, Vatican II, 1965 || text-card
For Muslims, Jesus, known as Isa, is one of the great prophets and the Messiah, born of the Virgin Mary. The Quran says that he spoke from the cradle, healed the sick, and was strengthened by the Holy Spirit. He is mentioned by name in many chapters, and Mary has a chapter named after her. || Isa and Maryam, Persian miniature, Rashid al-Din || wikimedia
SHORT: What the Quran Says About the End of Jesus's Life
On the end of his life, the Quran speaks briefly. In the fourth chapter it says that they did not kill him, nor did they crucify him, though it was made to appear so to them, and that God raised him up to himself. Another verse has God saying: I will take you and raise you to myself. || Quran 4:157-158, 3:55 || text-card
Muslim commentators have understood these verses in different ways. The majority tradition holds that Jesus was not crucified, but that God raised him bodily to heaven, and that he will return before the end of time. Others have proposed different readings, and the debate over the details continues among scholars. || Quran manuscript, Surah an-Nisa || wikimedia
Here, then, is a notable meeting point and a notable parting of the ways. Christians and Muslims agree that God raised Jesus to himself, and that he lives. They disagree on whether he was crucified, on what happened at the tomb, and on who he is. A respectful study can state both without trying to settle them. || Sant'Egidio, interfaith meeting || wikimedia
CHAPTER: Easter Through the Centuries
How did the Christians remember the resurrection? Very early, they began to keep an annual celebration around the time of the Jewish Passover. In the second century there was a dispute over the correct date, since some churches in Asia Minor kept it on the fourteenth of the Jewish month, and Rome kept it on a Sunday. || Eusebius, Church History 5.23-25 || text-card
The Council of Nicaea, in the year three hundred and twenty-five, set rules to bring the churches to a common practice. Easter would be kept on a Sunday, after the spring equinox and the full moon. The calendar systems used by the Eastern and Western churches have since produced different dates in most years. || Council of Nicaea, icon, Church of Saint Nicholas || wikimedia
Eastern Christians call the festival Pascha, and the icon of the resurrection shows not the empty tomb, but the Anastasis, Christ descending to the realm of the dead, taking Adam and Eve by the hand and pulling them up from their graves. In the West, painters preferred the moment of rising, with a banner of victory. || Anastasis, Chora Church, Istanbul || wikimedia
In Jerusalem, the tomb became a site of pilgrimage almost as soon as Constantine's builders had cleared it. The Church of the Holy Sepulchre was consecrated in the year three hundred and thirty-five. It has been destroyed, rebuilt and divided among several communities, which still share it in an uneasy peace to this day. || Church of the Holy Sepulchre, Jerusalem || wikimedia
In 2016, restorers removed the marble slab that covered the traditional burial bench inside the small shrine, called the Edicule, and found a limestone surface beneath it. Scientists dated mortar from the structure to around the fourth century, which fits the story that Constantine's workers enclosed the tomb at that time. || Edicule, Church of the Holy Sepulchre, restoration 2016 || wikimedia
Easter also entered music and art. Bach wrote an oratorio for it, and Handel's Messiah ends with the words worthy is the Lamb. Cathedral bells ring at dawn in many countries, and the greeting Christ is risen, answered with he is risen indeed, is still exchanged in many languages around the world. || Easter Oratorio, Bach, manuscript || wikimedia
CHAPTER: How Churches Understand Easter
Christians do not all speak about the resurrection in the same way. In Catholic and Orthodox teaching, the resurrection of Jesus is a bodily event, a real transformation of the crucified body, which the church confesses in the creed. Easter is the heart of the liturgical year. || Resurrection, Master of the Brunswick Diptych || wikimedia
Orthodox worship expresses this with great joy. At midnight on Easter, the church is dark, and then a single flame is passed from candle to candle until the whole building is filled with light. The priest proclaims, Christ is risen, and the people answer, truly he is risen. || Holy Fire, Church of the Holy Sepulchre || wikimedia
In the Protestant tradition, Easter is often tied to Paul's teaching that Christ was raised for our justification, and the day is marked with sunrise services and with hymns such as Christ the Lord is risen today, by Charles Wesley. The emphasis falls on the promise of forgiveness and of new life. || Charles Wesley, Christ the Lord Is Risen Today || text-card
Some modern theologians have approached the question differently. Rudolf Bultmann, in the twentieth century, argued that the resurrection should be understood as the rise of faith in the disciples, an event in their lives rather than in the tomb. Others, such as Marcus Borg, held that the stories are true in a metaphorical sense, while leaving open what happened to the body. || Rudolf Bultmann, Marburg || wikimedia
Against that view, theologians such as Wolfhart Pannenberg and Wright have insisted that Christianity makes a claim about history, and that without an actual event, the proclamation loses its meaning. This debate continues, and it runs through Christian communities as much as between them and outsiders. || Wolfhart Pannenberg, Jesus God and Man, 1968 || text-card
Despite these differences, almost all Christians share a few convictions. That death is not the last word. That God's justice and mercy are stronger than violence. And that the world is meant for renewal rather than destruction. That is why Easter has been called the feast of feasts, the day on which everything else depends. || Easter vigil, Orthodox church || wikimedia
CHAPTER: What History Can Say, and What Faith Says
So what can we say, at the end of this long look? Historians can say that Jesus of Nazareth was crucified under Pilate. They can say that within a few years, his followers were proclaiming that God had raised him, and that this belief reshaped the ancient world. These are well supported. || Pontius Pilate, Pilate Stone, Israel Museum || wikimedia
They can say that the earliest written testimony, in Paul's letter, lists appearances to named people, and that the Gospels, written later, add the empty tomb and further stories that differ in their details. They can show how the accounts developed and how they were read. All of that is open to study. || Gospel of Luke, Papyrus 75 || wikimedia
What the historian's tools cannot do is to decide whether a man was raised from the dead. That is a question about what is possible, about God, and about trust, and it is the point at which different people reach different conclusions. Christians say that it happened, and that it was the beginning of a new creation. || Resurrection, Titian, Averoldi Polyptych || wikimedia
Others say that it was a profound experience of hope that took the form of a story, or that it is a mystery which history cannot touch. Others still hold a different faith altogether. A fair account does not hide those differences, and it does not mock any of them. People have found meaning here for two thousand years. || Easter Sunrise, Mount of Olives || wikimedia
What no one disputes is that for millions of people, the claim that death is not the end changed how they lived, how they grieved, and how they hoped. It inspired hospitals and schools, music and art, courage and sometimes, sadly, cruelty. It is a story whose effects are part of world history. || Chartres Cathedral, west rose window || wikimedia
In our next episode, we meet a man who may have changed that history more than anyone after Jesus himself. He had been an opponent of the movement, and then an apostle. He had never met Jesus before the cross, and yet he wrote most of the earliest Christian letters that survive. We follow Paul on the road to Damascus. || Conversion of Saint Paul, Michelangelo, Pauline Chapel || wikimedia
For now, we leave the garden at dawn. The stone has been moved, the women have run to tell the others, and the story that follows is still being told and still being argued over. Thank you for watching, and for listening with an open mind. || Dawn over Jerusalem, Old City || wikimedia
