@chapter A06 cafe_sunset | The power of testing yourself
In 2006, two psychologists at Washington University in St Louis, Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke, ran an experiment that is now one of the most cited in the science of learning. It was simple. They gave students short prose passages about topics such as the sun and sea otters, and asked them to learn them. The twist was in how they were allowed to study.

@char A12 living_room_day | The setup | Study or be tested
Some students read the passage four times in a row. Others read it once, and then took a test, writing down as much as they could remember, with no feedback. Then they did another test, and another. The students who were tested spent less time reading, and more time trying to recall, which many people would think is a poor use of time.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Roediger & Karpicke | Psychological Science, 2006 | Test-enhanced learning: taking memory tests improves long-term retention
There were two follow-ups. Some students were tested after five minutes, and others after one week. The results were revealing. After five minutes, the students who had reread the passage did better. That's what most people would expect, and it's also what makes rereading feel so effective: it does work, in the short run.

@stat A14 conspiracy_board | Recall after one week | 61% vs 40% | students who practised recall remembered more than students who reread four times
But after a week, the picture reversed. Students who had been tested repeatedly remembered about sixty-one percent of the material, compared with about forty percent for those who had reread. The students who reread had also predicted that they'd do better. They were wrong in the direction of confidence, which is what the fluency illusion does.

@char A04 attic_rain | The pattern | Short-term gain, long-term loss
This is the pattern that keeps reappearing in the science of learning. Methods that give you an edge on tomorrow's test may leave you with little by next month. Methods that feel clumsy on the day can leave you with much more. If you only look at the short term, you'll pick the wrong method every time.

@list A03 living_room_day | What the 2006 study showed | Testing beat rereading at one week ;; Rereading won at five minutes ;; Students predicted that rereading would work better ;; Recall practice produced durable memory
It's a result that has been repeated in laboratories and classrooms around the world. And it's reassuring to know that it isn't a one-off. Over the next two decades, hundreds of studies followed.

@chapter A13 rooftop_dusk | Is it just a gimmick?
Skeptics asked a fair question. Perhaps testing helps with simple facts, but not with deeper understanding. In 2011, Jeffrey Karpicke and Janell Blunt set out to test that. They compared retrieval practice with a method that educators liked, called concept mapping, in which students draw diagrams of how ideas connect.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Karpicke & Blunt | Science, 2011 | Retrieval practice produces more learning than elaborative studying with concept mapping
Eighty students read a science text, then either did retrieval practice, which meant writing down all they could remember, or drew concept maps while looking at the text. A week later, they took tests that asked for facts, and tests that asked for inferences. Retrieval practice won on both, including on the concept-mapping test itself.

@char A12 living_room_day | The surprise | It helped understanding too
That result mattered. It suggested that retrieval practice doesn't only help with memorising facts. It also helps people to understand and apply what they've read. Recalling how ideas fit together seems to strengthen those connections, in a way that looking at a diagram doesn't.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Rowland | Psychological Bulletin, 2014 | The effect of testing versus restudy on retention: a meta-analytic review of the testing effect
Christopher Rowland combined results from many studies in a meta-analysis. He found a consistent benefit for testing over restudying, with a medium-sized effect, and that the benefit was larger when people got feedback on their answers. In other words, testing works, and it works better when you check what you got right and wrong.

@list A12 living_room_day | How to use retrieval practice | Close the book and write what you remember ;; Use flashcards, and say the answer before flipping ;; Answer practice questions without looking ;; Check your answers afterwards
These are all versions of the same thing. Try to produce the answer before you look at it, then check. It doesn't need special equipment. A blank sheet, a set of flashcards or a friend with a list of questions is enough.

@chapter A10 attic_rain | Even failing helps
Here's a finding that students tend to find reassuring. You don't even need to get the answer right for testing to help. In 2009, Nate Kornell, Matthew Hays and Robert Bjork asked people to study word pairs, and for some of them, to guess the answer before seeing it. Most of the guesses were wrong.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Kornell, Hays & Bjork | Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition, 2009 | Unsuccessful retrieval attempts enhance subsequent learning
But the wrong guesses still helped. People who tried and failed to recall the answer, and then saw the right one, remembered it better later than people who simply studied the pair. The attempt seemed to prepare the mind to receive the answer. It's sometimes called pre-testing, and it suggests that a wrong guess isn't wasted.

@char A04 attic_rain | What this means | Struggle first, then check
It turns the usual picture upside down. Many of us avoid questions that we can't answer, because failing feels bad. But trying and failing, followed by the correct answer, is an effective way to learn. It makes sense to try a problem before you've been taught the solution, and to guess a word before you look it up.

@list A03 living_room_day | Using the pretesting effect | Try a practice question before reading the chapter ;; Guess the meaning of a new word before checking ;; Predict the result before seeing an experiment ;; Note your errors and see what they teach you
It also helps with something that everyone fears: being wrong. When errors are part of the method, they stop being embarrassing. They become information, and they point to exactly where more attention is needed.

@chapter A12 living_room_day | The role of feedback
Testing works best when it's followed by feedback. If you practise an error without noticing, you may make it stronger. That's why checking your answers matters. In general, the research suggests that feedback soon after a test, and ideally with the correct answer shown, makes retrieval practice more effective.

@char A01 cafe_sunset | The high-confidence error | A special case
There's a particularly interesting finding about errors that people make with high confidence. In 2012, Andrew Butler and Henry Roediger found that when students were confident but wrong, and then received feedback, they were more likely to correct the mistake than when they were unsure. A surprise seems to make the correction stick.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Butler, Karpicke & Roediger | Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition, 2008 | Correcting a metacognitive error: feedback increases retention of low-confidence correct responses
That fits with a general idea in psychology, that learning is driven by surprise. When the world doesn't match what you expected, the brain pays attention and updates. When it matches, there's little to learn. That's why a confident mistake, kindly corrected, can be one of the best learning moments there is.

@char A04 attic_rain | A caution | Don't test yourself into misery
It's worth saying that retrieval practice should be kind. It works best when the questions are challenging but achievable, and when you're gentle with yourself about the mistakes. A test that is far too hard just produces frustration. The sweet spot is a task where you get about two-thirds right, and learn from the rest.

@list A12 living_room_day | A simple weekly routine | After each lesson, write down what you remember ;; At the end of the week, quiz yourself on it ;; Review the gaps ;; Repeat a few weeks later
Nothing complicated. A few minutes of recall after each session, a longer quiz at the end of the week and another a few weeks later. This is practice testing and spacing combined, and it's the closest the science of learning comes to a standard recipe.

Flashcards are among the oldest tools in learning, and the research gives them a fresh seal of approval. Not because the cards are special, but because they make you recall. The card is a question, and the answer is yours to produce.

@char A15 cabin_fireplace | A gentle thought | Trying is the learning
If you remember one thing from this chapter, remember that trying to recall is not a sign that you haven't learned. It's how you learn. The pause, the searching and the effort are the moments when memory is being built. Give them room.

@char A08 cabin_fireplace | Looking back | What testing taught us
So what did we learn? That retrieval practice beat rereading by about twenty points after a week. That it works for understanding as well as facts. That even failed attempts help, and that feedback makes it better. And that the feeling of difficulty is a good sign.
