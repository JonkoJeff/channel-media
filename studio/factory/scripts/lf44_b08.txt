@study A19 cafe_sunset | Feinberg, Willer and Keltner | Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 2012 | Embarrassment signals that we can be trusted
In 2012, Matthew Feinberg, Robb Willer and Dacher Keltner, working at the University of California, Berkeley, published a set of studies in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology. Its title was Flustered and Faithful. They wanted to know what embarrassment tells other people about us, and whether it changes how much they're willing to trust us.

@char A03 cabin_fireplace | What they found | Flustered, and trusted
Across their studies, people who showed more embarrassment tended to be seen as more prosocial, meaning more generous, more cooperative and more caring toward others. Observers trusted them more, and were more willing to be close to them. In some of the experiments, the people who were more easily embarrassed also behaved more generously themselves when given the chance to share.

@chapter A15 autumn_overlook | The spilled coffee
There's an even older study that points in a similar direction. It was published more than half a century ago, and it involves a quiz show, a tape recorder and a cup of coffee that ended up in the wrong place.

@study A19 living_room_day | Aronson, Willerman and Floyd | Psychonomic Science, 1966 | A competent person who blunders can seem more likeable
In 1966, Elliot Aronson, Ben Willerman and Joanne Floyd published a short paper in the journal Psychonomic Science. Students listened to a recording of someone trying out for a quiz team. In some versions, the contestant was highly capable and answered nearly every question correctly. Then, in some recordings, came the sound of a clumsy accident. The contestant spilled a cup of coffee all over himself.

@char A13 cafe_sunset | The pratfall effect | A little more human
The highly capable contestant who spilled the coffee was rated as more likeable than the same capable contestant who didn't. The small blunder seemed to make him more relatable, a little less perfect, a little more human. Researchers later called this the pratfall effect. Interestingly, the same clumsy accident didn't help the contestant who had performed poorly. The effect depended on the bigger picture.

@quote A10 attic_rain | A small slip can make a capable person easier to like. | The pratfall effect, in one line
So the lesson isn't that blunders are always charming. It's something gentler. A small slip can make a capable person easier to like. Being perfect can feel distant. Being good at things, and still occasionally waving at the wrong person, can feel warm and approachable. Like someone you'd actually want to sit next to.

Let's come back to the blush one more time, because researchers in the Netherlands have looked at it in a particularly careful way. They wanted to know what a blush does after something has actually gone wrong, after a real mistake or a small accident that affects someone else.

@study A19 cafe_sunset | Dijk, de Jong and Peters | Emotion, 2009 | Blushing after a mishap makes people judge us more kindly
In 2009, Corine Dijk, Peter de Jong and Madelon Peters published a study in the journal Emotion called The remedial value of blushing. Participants were shown people who had caused a mishap or a small transgression, some blushing and some not. The people who blushed were judged more positively. A blush, it seems, softens how others see a mistake, almost like a quick and sincere apology.
