@chapter A06 cafe_sunset | The just world
There's a belief so common that most of us hold it without noticing. That the world is basically fair. That good things happen to good people, and bad things to bad ones. We teach it to children in stories. We repeat it in phrases like, what goes around comes around. And it turns out to shape how we treat people who suffer, often in ways we don't intend.

@char A12 living_room_day | The researcher | Melvin Lerner
The social psychologist Melvin Lerner spent much of his career studying this belief, which he called the belief in a just world. He was struck by something he'd seen as a young researcher in a psychiatric hospital: that staff members, who were caring people, often spoke of patients as if their misfortune must somehow be their own fault. He wondered why.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Lerner & Simmons | Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 1966 | Observer's reaction to the innocent victim: compassion or rejection?
In a now classic experiment, women students watched what they believed was a live video of another woman, who was apparently receiving painful electric shocks as part of a learning task. In reality, it was a recording. When the observers were told that they couldn't help, and that the woman would keep receiving shocks, many of them began to devalue her, rating her as less attractive and more responsible for her fate.

@char A04 attic_rain | The surprise | Powerlessness turns into blame
The finding is uncomfortable. When people couldn't relieve the suffering, they didn't just feel sorry. Many of them reinterpreted the victim, so that the suffering seemed deserved. It's a way of protecting the belief that the world is fair. If bad things only happen to people who deserve them, then we're safe, because we're not like them.

@list A03 living_room_day | How the just world belief can show up | Wondering what the victim did to deserve it ;; Asking why didn't she leave, why didn't he see it coming ;; Feeling that people should have prevented their own misfortune ;; Believing that careful people are never hurt
These reactions are common and often well meant. They come from the same assumption that Janoff-Bulman described, the need for the world to be meaningful. If we can find a reason, then the world still makes sense, and so do our own chances.

@chapter A13 rooftop_dusk | Is it a stable finding?
Because the Lerner and Simmons experiment was so striking, other researchers repeated it in many forms. A large review of the work, decades later, looked at what had been found.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Hafer & Bègue | Psychological Bulletin, 2005 | Experimental research on just-world theory: problems, developments, and future challenges
Carolyn Hafer and Laurent Bègue reviewed many studies. They concluded that the tendency to blame or devalue victims in order to maintain belief in a just world was supported in numerous experiments, but that the details were more varied than the early work suggested. People differed in how strongly they held the belief, and in how they responded to threats to it, and some of the original effects depended on the setup.

@char A12 living_room_day | A balanced reading | Real, and complicated
So it's a real tendency, but not an iron law. Some people hold the belief more strongly than others. And it doesn't always lead to blaming. In some situations, people respond to injustice with compassion, or with action. It seems that the belief becomes most dangerous for others when we feel threatened, and unable to help.

@chapter A06 cafe_sunset | The cost to the one who suffers
Now turn the picture around. If the people around us tend to look for reasons, and sometimes blame, what's it like to be the one who has been hurt? Many survivors report not only their original pain, but a second one, from the reactions of others. Well-meaning remarks can sting.

@list A12 living_room_day | Remarks that can wound | Everything happens for a reason ;; At least it wasn't worse ;; You'll be stronger for it ;; Have you thought about what you could have done differently
Each of these is usually said with love, and each can land as a small injury. They come from the listener's need to restore a sense of order. But for the person who is hurting, they can feel like a request to hurry, or a hint of blame, or a denial of the size of the loss.

@char A19 cabin_fireplace | A kinder alternative | Be with, don't fix
Research on social support suggests that what helps most isn't an explanation. It's presence, and acknowledgement, and practical help. Saying, I'm so sorry, I'm here, and I don't know what to say, is almost always better than offering a reason. It tells the person that you can bear to be near their pain, and that they don't have to make it neat for you.

@chapter A12 living_room_day | The belief in ourselves
There's another side to the just world, and it's more personal. When something terrible happens to us, we may begin to question the belief that we are protected, because we're good. And, in the aftermath, some people turn the unfairness inward. They decide that they must have deserved it. That's a heavy burden, and it's very common.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Janoff-Bulman | Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 1979 | Characterological versus behavioral self-blame: inquiries into depression and rape
Janoff-Bulman studied women who had survived rape and found two kinds of self-blame. Characterological self-blame, believing it was because of the kind of person they were, was linked to depression. Behavioural self-blame, focused on something they did, was in some cases linked to a sense of future control, though it too was not simply helpful. The study didn't say that victims were at fault. It showed how people struggle to find a sense of control after harm.

@char A04 attic_rain | A sensitive point | Blame is a way of seeking control
Researchers have noted that self-blame, though painful, can offer a sense that the world is still predictable. If it was my fault, then I can do something differently next time. It's a trade: some safety in return for a lot of pain. Understanding that doesn't make self-blame accurate. It makes it more understandable, and perhaps easier to meet with kindness.

@list A03 living_room_day | Words to keep from this chapter | Belief in a just world ;; Victim blaming ;; Self-blame ;; Social support
Four terms from this chapter. The belief in a just world is the assumption that people get what they deserve. Victim blaming is attributing the cause of harm to the one harmed. Self-blame is holding oneself responsible. And social support is the practical and emotional help that others give.

@list A12 living_room_day | If someone you know is hurting | Listen more than you explain ;; Avoid searching for reasons or lessons ;; Offer something specific and practical ;; Keep showing up after the first weeks
Here is how you can apply this chapter, if someone close to you has been through something hard. Listen more than you explain. Avoid reasons and lessons, even kind ones, unless they ask for them. Offer something specific, such as a meal on a particular day, a lift to an appointment, or an hour of company, rather than the vague, call me if you need anything. And keep showing up after the first few weeks. Support often fades just when it's most needed, because the world has moved on, and the person hasn't. A message at three months can mean more than a visit at three days. Picture this scene. A student hears that a classmate has been the victim of a scam, and her first thought is, how could she not have noticed? She catches herself, and realises that the thought is a small defence. If the classmate was careless, then she is safe, because she is careful. It is a comfortable story, and it is exactly the just-world instinct that Lerner described. Noticing it does not make her a bad person. It makes her a more compassionate one, because she can now choose a kinder thought on purpose.

A pair of scales that won't quite balance. The belief in a just world is the wish that they always do. It's a comforting wish, and it also leads us to be harder on those who suffer than we'd ever want to be.

@char A15 cabin_fireplace | A gentle thought | Unfairness is not your fault
If you've been hurt, and found yourself wondering what you did to deserve it, there's a gentle reminder in the research. Looking for a reason is something that minds do to feel safe, not a sign that you were at fault. Sometimes terrible things happen without a cause that anyone could have avoided, and it's all right to say that.

@char A08 cabin_fireplace | Looking back | What fairness taught us
So what did we learn? That many people believe the world is just, and cling to it. That in a classic experiment, observers devalued a suffering stranger when they couldn't help. That well-meant remarks can wound. And that self-blame is partly a search for control.
