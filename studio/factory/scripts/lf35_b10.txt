@chapter A19 bedroom_night | Myth eight: a free mind is a happy mind
Here's a picture of happiness that many of us carry around without ever saying it out loud. The happiest moments are the ones when the mind is free. Free to drift. Free to daydream about the weekend, or a holiday, or the way things will be once everything is finally sorted out. A mind let off the lead, wandering wherever it likes.

@char A13 attic_rain | Somewhere else | The future, or the past
And a lot of our happiness seems to live somewhere other than right now. In plans. In memories. In the next good thing coming up on the calendar. We tell ourselves we'll be happy when the holidays arrive, or when the busy month is over, or when we finally get round to that trip. So that's our eighth myth. That happiness lives mostly in the future or the past, and that a wandering mind is a happy one.

@char A10 living_room_day | Nora at the window | Thinking about something else
Think of Nora again, standing at her window on an ordinary Tuesday morning, a cup of tea in her hand. The sea is right there, grey and silver, the way she once dreamed it would be. But Nora isn't really there. She's in a meeting that hasn't happened yet. She's replaying something a friend said on the phone last week. She's wondering whether she remembered to reply to an email. Her eyes are on the view. Her mind is miles away.

@char A12 cafe_sunset | A question you can measure | Where is your mind now
For a long time, this was very hard to study. You can ask people afterwards how happy they were, but memory is unreliable, as we heard earlier tonight. What researchers really wanted was to catch people in the moment, right in the middle of an ordinary day, and ask them two simple questions. What are you doing right now? And what are you thinking about?

@study A18 living_room_day | Matthew Killingsworth and Daniel Gilbert | Harvard University | Phone check-ins at random moments of ordinary days
In 2010, Matthew Killingsworth and Daniel Gilbert at Harvard published a study in the journal Science that did exactly that. They built an app for the iPhone. At random moments during the day, it would contact people and ask them how they were feeling, what they were doing, and whether they were thinking about something other than what they were doing. About two thousand two hundred and fifty adults took part.

@stat A13 cafe_sunset | How often minds wandered | 46.9% | of the moments sampled
And the first result was striking all on its own. People's minds were wandering in 46.9 percent of the moments sampled. Almost half of waking life, spent thinking about something other than what was actually happening. And mind wandering showed up in nearly every activity people reported, apart from one.

@list A19 living_room_day | What they found | Minds wandered in almost half the moments ;; People were less happy when wandering ;; Pleasant daydreams were no happier, on average ;; What people thought about mattered more than what they did
The second result was the one that made headlines. People were less happy when their minds were wandering than when they weren't. Even when they wandered to pleasant topics, they were, on average, no happier than when they were focused on the task in front of them. And what people were thinking about predicted their happiness better than what they were actually doing.

@quote A12 rooftop_dusk | A human mind is a wandering mind, and a wandering mind is an unhappy mind. | Killingsworth and Gilbert, Science, 2010
The authors put it in one line that has been quoted ever since. A human mind is a wandering mind, and a wandering mind is an unhappy mind. Now, that's a strong sentence, and it's worth handling with some care.

@char A03 cafe_sunset | The honest caveat | Correlation, not proof
Because this kind of study shows what tends to happen together, not exactly what causes what. The researchers did look at the timing, and found that wandering tended to come before low moods more than the other way round. But it was still observational, in the end. And mind wandering isn't all bad. Daydreaming can help us plan, and sometimes solve problems. The point isn't that you should never let your mind drift. It's that drifting away isn't the happiness shortcut it often feels like.

@chapter A15 autumn_overlook | Savouring
So if a wandering mind tends to miss the good things, what happens when we deliberately do the opposite? When we stay with a good moment, on purpose, and let it fill up our attention? Psychologists have a word for that. Savouring.

@study A18 cafe_sunset | Fred Bryant and Joseph Veroff | Loyola University Chicago and the University of Michigan | Savouring as a skill people can practise
The psychologists Fred Bryant and Joseph Veroff spent many years studying this, and in 2007 they published a book called Savoring: A New Model of Positive Experience. Their central idea was simple and rather lovely. Feeling good isn't only something that happens to us. It's also something we do. People differ in how much they notice, stretch out and deepen the good moments of their lives. And that ability can be practised.

@list A12 living_room_day | Ways people savour | Slowing down to notice details ;; Sharing a good moment with someone ;; Remembering it later, on purpose ;; Looking forward to it beforehand
Bryant and Veroff described several ways people do this. Slowing down to notice the details of a good moment. Sharing it with someone else, telling them about it. Remembering it later, on purpose, going back over it. And looking forward to it beforehand, enjoying the anticipation. Savouring, in this sense, can reach into the past, the present and the future.

@char A05 rooftop_dusk | Wait a moment | Isn't that wandering?
Now, you might notice something interesting there. Looking back, and looking forward. Isn't that exactly the mind wandering we were just talking about? Not quite. The difference is that savouring is chosen. It's attention, pointed on purpose at something good. Mind wandering is attention slipping away, often toward worries and unfinished business, without our ever deciding to go there.

#ai lf35_b10_01.jpg | Editorial still-life of a ceramic cup of tea held on a wide wooden windowsill, steam rising, a grey silver sea and soft morning light outside the window, calm unhurried mood, no people, no text, no logos

@photo lf35_b10_01.jpg | One slow minute at the window
And so Nora tries an experiment of her own. Just once a day, when she's standing at the window with her tea, she gives it one full minute. Nothing else. No phone. No planning. She just looks. The colour of the water. The line where it meets the sky. The way the light moves across it when a cloud passes.

@char A20 living_room_day | What she notices | Things she had never seen
And she notices things she had genuinely never seen before, in all those weeks of living there. A small boat that goes out at the same time every morning. A patch of water near the harbour that always looks darker than the rest. The way the sea seems to change colour, very slightly, before rain. The view had been there all along. She just hadn't been there with it.

@char A19 cabin_fireplace | Why it works | Adaptation, interrupted
Think back to what we learned about adaptation near the start of tonight. The brain stops responding to what's steady and familiar. But adaptation isn't a locked door. It relies on attention drifting off. When you deliberately pay attention to the details of something, you find what's new in it again. Each look becomes slightly different from the last one.

#ai lf35_b10_02.jpg | Editorial still-life of a small harbour at dawn seen from high above, one small fishing boat leaving a soft wake on calm grey water, gentle mist, quiet peaceful mood, no people, no text, no logos

@photo lf35_b10_02.jpg | The small boat at seven
Picture that little boat Nora noticed, heading out of the harbour in the first grey light, leaving a thin silver line behind it on the water. It had been going out every single morning since the day she moved in. It was in her view the whole time. It took one deliberate, unhurried minute for it to finally become part of her morning. And now, most days, she finds herself looking for it, and feeling a small, quiet pleasure when it appears.

@list A13 cafe_sunset | Small savouring habits | One slow minute with something you love ;; Tell someone about a good moment ;; Linger at the end, before moving on ;; Look forward to small things
None of this needs to be grand. One slow minute with something you love. Telling someone about a good moment, instead of letting it pass unsaid. Lingering at the end of a nice meal or a walk, rather than rushing on to the next thing. Looking forward to small things, as well as big ones. These are tiny acts of attention. But attention is exactly what adaptation quietly takes away from us.

@char A12 rooftop_dusk | Not about forcing | Gentle, not strict
And it's worth saying that savouring isn't about forcing yourself to feel happy. If you stand at a window and command yourself to feel delighted, you'll probably just feel a bit silly. It's gentler than that. You simply give a good thing a little more of your attention than you normally would, and let whatever feeling comes, come.

@char A03 living_room_day | Where we go next | Not alone
But there's something you might have noticed in that list of savouring habits. One of them involved another person. Telling someone. Sharing it. And that's a clue to the next myth we're going to look at. Because so far tonight, we've mostly treated happiness as something that happens inside one person's head. And the research suggests that this picture is missing something rather important.
