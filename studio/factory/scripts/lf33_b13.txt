@chapter A15 autumn_overlook | Imagining the future
Underneath all of Mara's columns, there's really only one question. Will I be happier there, or here? Every point she writes down is a guess about her future feelings. How lonely she'll be without her friends. How lovely it will be to see her sister every week. How the sea will feel in winter.

@char A13 cabin_fireplace | A forecast | Of feelings
Psychologists call these guesses affective forecasts. Affect is simply the scientific word for feeling. And for about thirty years, researchers led by Daniel Gilbert at Harvard and Timothy Wilson at Virginia have been studying how good people are at forecasting their own feelings. The answer is that we're decent at the direction, and rather poor at the size and the length.

@study A01 living_room_day | Daniel Gilbert, Timothy Wilson and colleagues | Harvard and Virginia, 1998 | Young academics expected to be unhappy for years if their job application failed
In a study published in 1998, Gilbert, Wilson and their colleagues asked young university teachers how happy they expected to be in the years after an important decision about their careers, depending on whether it went their way or not. Those still waiting predicted that a no would leave them much less happy for years.

@char A10 rooftop_dusk | What really happened | Much closer
But when the researchers asked people who had already been through it, those who had received the bad news were not much less happy than those who had received the good news. The fall people expected was mostly imagined. The researchers found something similar when people predicted how they'd feel after a romantic relationship ended.

@char A12 cafe_sunset | The impact bias | Too big, too long
Wilson and Gilbert called this the impact bias. We tend to overestimate how strongly, and especially how long, future events will affect us, good and bad. The wonderful thing turns out less wonderful than we hoped. The terrible thing turns out less terrible than we feared. Life tends to drift back towards the middle.

@chapter A05 cabin_fireplace | A hidden helper
Why do we get it wrong? One reason, Gilbert and Wilson suggested, is that we forget about a quiet helper we all have. When bad things happen, the mind gets to work making sense of them, finding silver linings, adjusting, moving on. They called it the psychological immune system. And because it works out of sight, we don't count on it when we imagine the future.

@char A19 bedroom_night | Mara's fears | Lonely by the sea
So if Mara pictures herself lonely in a new town, missing her friends, she may be right that she'll feel that way at first. But she's probably underestimating how quickly she'd adapt. How she'd find a new café, and new faces, and new reasons to walk out of the door in the morning. Most people do.

@chapter A16 autumn_overlook | The focusing illusion
There's a second reason our forecasts go wrong, and it's captured beautifully in a study about sunshine. In 1998, David Schkade and Daniel Kahneman asked students in the American Midwest and in Southern California about their lives.

@study A03 cafe_sunset | David Schkade and Daniel Kahneman | Midwest and California, 1998 | Both groups expected Californians to be happier; their actual satisfaction was about the same
Both groups expected that Californians would be more satisfied with their lives. After all, the weather is lovely. But when the researchers measured it, life satisfaction in the two places was about the same. Students in both places rated the climate in California as far better. It simply didn't matter much for how their lives felt overall.

@quote A13 rooftop_dusk | Nothing in life is as important as you think it is, while you are thinking about it. | Daniel Kahneman
Kahneman later summed it up in one lovely sentence. Nothing in life is as important as you think it is, while you are thinking about it. When you focus on one difference between two lives, the weather, the sea, the distance, that difference swells. In the real days, it's just one thing among hundreds.

@char A10 living_room_day | Mara's sea | One thing among many
Mara's picture of the coast is full of the sea. Walks along the water, salt in the air, wide skies. But on most days of life by the sea, she'd be doing the same things she does now. Working, cooking, washing up, answering messages, getting tired. The sea would be there. It just wouldn't be everything.

Picture an ordinary evening in a little town by the sea. The lights come on in the windows, someone is making dinner, someone is washing up. The sea is there at the end of the street. But most of life is happening indoors.

@chapter A20 cafe_sunset | The cost of keeping options open
Here's a third surprise about our feelings and our choices. Most of us like to keep our options open. A choice we can reverse feels safer than one we can't. But a clever study suggests that keeping the door open may make us less happy with whatever we chose.

@study A01 cabin_fireplace | Daniel Gilbert and Jane Ebert | Harvard University, 2002 | Students who could swap their chosen photograph liked it less than students who could not
In 2002, Daniel Gilbert and Jane Ebert ran a photography course for students. At the end, each student printed two of their favourite photographs, and had to choose one to keep. Some were told their choice was final. Others were told they could swap their photo for the other one within a few days, if they changed their mind.

@char A12 rooftop_dusk | The open door | Less content
Most people, when asked in advance, preferred to have the chance to change their minds. But the students whose choice was final ended up liking their photograph more than the students who could swap. When the door is closed, the mind's quiet helper gets to work, making peace with the choice. When the door stays open, it keeps comparing.

@chapter A18 living_room_day | Asking someone who knows
So if our own forecasts are shaky, what can we do? Gilbert and his colleagues tested one surprisingly simple answer. Instead of imagining the future, ask someone who is already living it.

@study A03 cafe_sunset | Daniel Gilbert, Matthew Killingsworth and colleagues | Science, 2009 | A stranger's report of a real experience predicted enjoyment better than a full description of it
In a study published in 2009, women were about to go on a brief speed date with a man. Some were given his profile and photo, and asked to predict how much they'd enjoy the date. Others knew nothing about him, but were told how much another woman had enjoyed a date with him. The second group predicted their own enjoyment more accurately.

@char A15 autumn_overlook | Other people's present | Our future
That's a striking result. A stranger's report of a real experience predicted their feelings better than all the information they could imagine with. Yet most people, when asked, said they'd prefer the profile. We trust our own imagination more than other people's experience, even when the experience is the better guide.

@char A19 cabin_fireplace | Mara's best source | People who moved
So perhaps the most useful thing Mara could do isn't adding to her columns at all. It's talking to people who have already made a move like hers. People who moved to be near family. People who moved to small towns. How did they feel after a year? After three? Their present is the closest thing she has to her own future.

@char A02 living_room_day | A kind thought | We adapt
And under all of this, there's a kind thought worth keeping. Whatever Mara chooses, she will probably adapt more than she fears. Neither choice is likely to bring the endless joy or the endless sadness she sometimes imagines. That doesn't make the choice unimportant. But it might make it a little less frightening.
