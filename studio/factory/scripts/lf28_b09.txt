@chapter A10 attic_rain | A question with no single answer
So far we have looked at how dreams are made and what they are made of. Now comes the harder question, the one people have argued about for more than a century. Why do we dream at all? It sounds simple, but there is still no settled answer, and that is worth saying plainly before we begin. What we have instead are several serious ideas, each explaining a part of the picture.

@char A19 cabin_fireplace | The old answer | Messages from the unconscious
For a long time, the popular answer was that a dream is a hidden message. Your mind, it was said, tucks your secret wishes into symbols, and a clever interpreter can unpack them. It is a beautiful idea, and it has a long history. The trouble is that it is very hard to test. If any image can mean almost anything, then the theory can never be wrong, and science has a difficult time with theories like that.

@char A03 living_room_day | A different starting point | Start with the brain
So in the 1970s, a different kind of question appeared. What if we stopped asking what a dream means, and first asked what the sleeping brain is physically doing? That change of question led to one of the most influential ideas in the history of dream research, and we will look at it first, including the parts that have not held up.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Hobson & McCarley | American Journal of Psychiatry, 1977 | The brain as a dream state generator: an activation-synthesis hypothesis of the dream process
In 1977, two Harvard researchers, Allan Hobson and Robert McCarley, published a paper with a bold title. They proposed that dreams begin in the brainstem. During REM sleep, a region called the pons fires off bursts of electrical activity, and these signals travel upward to the forebrain. The forebrain, which loves to make sense of things, then does what it always does. It builds a story.

@char A05 cabin_fireplace | Where the idea began | Clues from sleeping cats
Part of the evidence came from the laboratory, and a surprising amount of it from sleeping cats. Researchers recording from the cat brainstem found rhythmic bursts of activity during REM sleep, which became known as PGO waves, and they became a central clue for Hobson and McCarley. If a sleeping brain could generate its own vivid input from the inside, then perhaps no outside message was needed at all. It was a neat, physical, testable picture.

@list A12 cabin_fireplace | The activation-synthesis idea | The pons sends out bursts of activity ;; The signals spread to visual, motor and emotional areas ;; The forebrain tries to make sense of them ;; The result is a dream story
Think of it as a kind of improvised storytelling. The brainstem hands over a handful of random fragments, a staircase, a face, a feeling of hurry, and the forebrain does its best to stitch them into something that hangs together. This is why, on this view, dreams can feel both vivid and oddly unfinished. The raw material is scattered, and the story is a clever patch over the gaps.

@char A15 cabin_fireplace | Why it caught on | A biological explanation
It is easy to see why the idea caught on. It fits several things we already know. During REM sleep your motor output is blocked, so the movement signals that the brainstem sends out go nowhere, and your body stays still while the dream runs on. REM also comes in regular waves through the night, about every 90 minutes, which matches the rhythm of the signals the theory describes. And it gave researchers something they could measure, instead of something they could only interpret.

@char A03 cabin_fireplace | A fair question | Is it all random?
But there is a gentle question hiding inside the theory. If the signals are random, why are your dreams so often about your own life? Why do people, places and worries from your day keep turning up, rather than a stream of unrelated nonsense? It is a fair challenge, and the critics were quick to make it.

@list A13 living_room_day | What the critics said | Dreaming also happens outside REM sleep ;; Dream content often reflects the dreamer's own concerns ;; The story is not as random as the theory suggests ;; Hobson himself later revised his view
Two of the criticisms stand out. First, as we saw earlier, people report dreams from sleep stages other than REM, and a theory built around REM signals does not predict that. Second, dream content is surprisingly personal. It tends to follow the concerns of the dreamer's waking life, which sits uneasily with the idea of pure randomness. Hobson took these points seriously, and in later years he moved toward a broader view in which dreaming might have a function of its own.

@char A01 living_room_day | What the theory still gets right | A useful lesson
Even with its weak points, the theory left us something useful. It reminded us that the dreaming brain is not blank. It is active, sometimes as active as when you are awake, and it is working with whatever it has. It also reminded us that strangeness does not need a secret meaning to explain it. Sometimes a strange scene is simply what happens when a story-making system works with scattered pieces.

@quote A19 cabin_fireplace | The brain as a dream state generator | Hobson & McCarley, 1977 title
The title of that paper is worth pausing on. The brain as a dream state generator. Notice the framing. It does not say that dreams are a message, or a memory, or a wish. It says the brain generates a state, a whole world to be in, and that idea has outlasted many of the details of the original theory.

@photo lf28_b09_01.jpg | A lamp left on
Picture a lamp left on in a dark room. Nothing in it is moving, and yet something is clearly still switched on. That is a little like the sleeping brain in REM. The usual daytime inputs are gone, the eyes are closed, the body is still, but the machinery of experience keeps humming, and it has to build its scenes from somewhere inside.

@chapter A12 rooftop_dusk | The dream as rehearsal
Activation-synthesis asks how a dream is made. A second family of ideas asks a different question: what might a dream be for? One of the most discussed answers comes from a Finnish philosopher and neuroscientist, and it is rather dramatic.

@study A06 conspiracy_board | Antti Revonsuo | Behavioral and Brain Sciences, 2000 | The reinterpretation of dreams: an evolutionary hypothesis of the function of dreaming
In 2000, Antti Revonsuo, working in Finland, proposed what he called the threat simulation theory. His argument runs like this. For most of human history, life contained real dangers, predators, strangers, accidents. A brain that could rehearse those dangers at night, safely, would be better prepared to spot and escape them by day. So perhaps dreaming evolved as a protected practice room for the things we most need to survive.

@char A13 rooftop_dusk | What that would predict | Chases, falls, close calls
If dreams really were a rehearsal for danger, you would expect them to be full of threats, and in fact they often are. Being chased, being lost, being late, losing something important. Researchers had noticed this long before the theory existed. In 1966, Calvin Hall and Robert Van de Castle had already shown that aggression and misfortune are common in ordinary dream reports. Revonsuo's idea offered a reason why.

@stat A14 conspiracy_board | Recurrent dreams with at least one threat | 66% | in an analysis of 212 recurrent dreams by Zadra and colleagues, 2006
One study that tested the theory looked at recurrent dreams, the ones that come back again and again. Antonio Zadra and his colleagues analysed 212 of them in 2006, and found that 66 percent contained at least one threat. Six of the eight predictions they tested were supported. For supporters of the theory, that was encouraging.

@char A13 rooftop_dusk | Why this matters | Dreams as data
Notice what researchers did here. They did not decide in advance what dreams mean. They collected hundreds of real dream reports and counted what was in them, then asked whether the theory's predictions held. That patient counting, which began with Hall and Van de Castle, is what turns a mysterious experience into something science can examine. It is slower than interpretation, but it can be checked by anyone.

@char A03 attic_rain | Another piece of evidence | Children and trauma
There is also work with children. Katja Valli, Revonsuo and their colleagues compared Kurdish children who had lived through severe trauma with children who had not, and the traumatised children reported more threatening dreams. That fits the idea that the dreaming mind turns its attention to danger when danger is real. It is a sobering finding, and a careful one, because it shows how closely dreams follow lived experience.

@list A18 attic_rain | What critics have said | Fewer than 15% of those recurrent dreams showed realistic survival threats ;; Realistic life threats seemed rare in other analyses ;; Successful escapes were rarer still ;; No study shows dream practice improves real-life escape
This is where the story becomes more honest, and more interesting. In that same recurrent dream study, fewer than 15 percent of the dreams depicted a realistic, probable survival situation. Other researchers, including Mark Solms and a colleague, found that life-threatening events in dreams were rare, and successful escapes rarer still. Revonsuo and Valli replied that these tests misjudged what the theory predicts. Much of the disagreement comes down to how you define a threat.

@char A02 autumn_overlook | A lasting objection | Where is the proof?
There is one more objection, and it is a quiet one. Nobody has yet shown that dreaming about a danger actually makes a person better at avoiding it when awake. The theory is therefore an open hypothesis. It is neither confirmed nor ruled out, and good scientists are comfortable saying so.

@chapter A12 autumn_overlook | Dreams that follow your days
Between the brainstem and the evolutionary story, there is a third voice, and it is quieter. It begins with a simple observation about what people actually dream of.

@char A10 cafe_sunset | A middle path | Dreams reflect your life
Another voice in this debate belongs to G. William Domhoff, who has spent decades studying dream reports. His view is that dreams mostly reflect what you are already concerned about when you are awake, and that dreaming may be a by-product of a brain that is simply good at simulating experience, rather than something shaped by evolution for a particular job. So the same facts, the fears, the chases, the ordinary worries, can be read in more than one way.

@list A03 attic_rain | Questions worth keeping | If dreams are partly random, why are they so personal? ;; If dreams rehearse danger, why so many ordinary scenes? ;; Can one theory explain every kind of dream?
Here are three questions that the research leaves open, and they are worth carrying with you. If dreams are partly random, why do they feel so personal? If dreams rehearse danger, why are so many of them about ordinary places and ordinary people? And can any single theory explain a nightmare, a pleasant daydream-like scene and a dull office dream all at once? Researchers are still working on each of these, and that is an honest place to stand.

@char A01 autumn_overlook | A quiet observation | Familiar faces
Look at your own recent dreams, if you remember them. The people in them are usually people you know, and the places are usually places you have been. That ordinary quality is one of the best-documented features of dreaming, and any good theory has to explain it.

@list A20 autumn_overlook | Three ideas side by side | Hidden message: hard to test ;; Activation-synthesis: the story is stitched from signals ;; Threat simulation: the dream is a rehearsal
It helps to hold the three ideas next to each other. The hidden message idea is hard to test. Activation-synthesis explains how a dream might be put together, but says little about why. Threat simulation offers a purpose, but struggles with the plain fact that many dreams are about ordinary life. None of them is the whole answer, and each one gets something right.

@char A18 living_room_day | In everyday terms | The chase dream
Take a dream that many people know, running from something while your legs feel heavy. Activation-synthesis would say that the brain received signals about movement and fear, and the forebrain wove them into a chase. Threat simulation would say that the chase is practice. Domhoff would ask what you were worried about this week. Three readings, one dream, and each of them is a serious attempt to explain it.

@char A19 cabin_fireplace | A kinder way to hold it | You do not need one answer
Here is a comfortable way to hold all this. You do not have to choose a winner tonight. A dream can be built from the brain's spontaneous activity and still be shaped by what worries you. It can feel like a rehearsal without being one. Science often moves like this, slowly, by letting several partial truths sit next to one another until a larger picture appears.

@char A13 cabin_fireplace | What comes next | A different kind of function
There is, however, a third family of ideas, one that has gathered a great deal of evidence over the last twenty years. It says that dreams may have to do with memory. When we meet it, we leave the dramatic world of chases and predators, and step into something quieter, with falling blocks and a virtual maze.
