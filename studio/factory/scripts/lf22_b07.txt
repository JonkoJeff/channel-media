@chapter A10 attic_rain | Myth seven: intensity means it's right
Here's a belief many of us hold, often without realising it. If a connection feels incredibly intense, it must be special. The pounding heart, the sleepless nights, the way you can't stop thinking about someone. Surely all of that means something profound. But what if part of that intensity comes from somewhere else entirely?

@char A13 rooftop_dusk | Bodies and minds | How we label feelings
To understand that, we need to go back to an idea about emotions from the early 1960s. It suggests that our feelings are not just something our bodies do. They're also something our minds interpret.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Schachter & Singer | Psychological Review, 1962 | Cognitive, social, and physiological determinants of emotional state
In 1962, Stanley Schachter and Jerome Singer proposed what's often called the two-factor theory of emotion. First, our bodies become stirred up. Our hearts race, our breathing quickens. Then our minds look around for an explanation, and that explanation shapes which emotion we feel. The same racing heart could be fear, excitement or attraction, depending on how we read the situation.

@char A12 living_room_day | A caution | The original study is debated
Their original experiment, which involved injections of adrenaline, has been criticised and has proved hard to repeat in its full form. But the broader idea, that we sometimes interpret our own bodily arousal using clues from our surroundings, inspired one of the most famous experiments in the psychology of attraction.

@chapter A15 autumn_overlook | The wobbly bridge
And that experiment took place not in a laboratory, but on a bridge high above a river in Canada.

@photo lf22_b07_01.jpg | A high suspension bridge
The Capilano Suspension Bridge, near Vancouver, is a long, narrow walkway of wooden boards and cables, slung high above a rocky river canyon. It sways and wobbles as people walk across it, and has low handrails. For many people, crossing it is a genuinely nerve-wracking experience.

#ai lf22_b07_01.jpg | Editorial illustration of a long narrow wooden suspension bridge stretching across a deep forested river canyon in soft morning mist, tall evergreen trees, calm painterly documentary style, no people, no text, no logos

@study A04 attic_rain | Dutton & Aron | Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 1974 | Some evidence for heightened sexual attraction under conditions of high anxiety
In 1974, Donald Dutton and Arthur Aron arranged for an attractive young woman to approach men who had just crossed the bridge. She asked them to fill out a short questionnaire, which included writing a brief story about a picture. Afterwards, she gave each man her phone number, in case he wanted to know more about the study. The researchers repeated the same thing on a solid, low bridge nearby.

@char A03 cafe_sunset | The result | The shaky bridge group called more
The men who had just crossed the high, wobbly bridge wrote stories with more romantic and sexual content. And many more of them called the woman afterwards than the men who had crossed the solid bridge. The researchers suggested that some of the men had mistaken the pounding of their hearts from the bridge for attraction to the woman.

@char A13 rooftop_dusk | Misattribution of arousal | A confused heart
This became known as the misattribution of arousal. Our bodies get stirred up for one reason, and our minds wrongly credit it to something else, like the attractive person standing in front of us. It's a striking idea. Some of the electricity we feel around someone might come from the setting, not the person.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | White, Fishbein & Rutsein | Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 1981 | Passionate love and the misattribution of arousal
Other researchers tested this in different ways. In 1981, Gregory White and his colleagues had men either run on the spot for a couple of minutes, or for just a few seconds, before watching a video of a woman they were told they might meet. Those who had run for longer, with their hearts still racing, rated an attractive woman as more attractive than those who had barely run.

@list A12 living_room_day | Holding it carefully | Some of the original studies were small ;; Later work has debated how strong the effect is ;; A 1998 review by Craig Foster and colleagues still found the effect overall ;; Arousal seems to amplify feelings that are already there
Like many classic studies, these have been debated. Some were small, and later researchers have argued about how strong the effect really is. A review published in 1998 by Craig Foster and his colleagues concluded that arousal does tend to increase attraction overall. One common view now is that arousal mostly amplifies feelings that are already there, rather than creating attraction out of nothing.

@char A10 cabin_fireplace | What it means for us | Exciting settings, intense feelings
So it may not be a coincidence that intense connections often begin in intense settings. A festival, a trip abroad, a crisis, a late night of long talks. When our bodies are already stirred up, a person who happens to be there may seem even more captivating. The feeling is real. But part of its intensity may belong to the moment.

@chapter A13 rooftop_dusk | Limerence
There's a word for the most intense form of romantic longing, and it was coined by a psychologist in the 1970s.

@study A04 attic_rain | Dorothy Tennov | Love and Limerence, 1979 | Described an involuntary state of intense longing for another person
In her 1979 book Love and Limerence, the psychologist Dorothy Tennov described a state she had heard about from hundreds of people in interviews and questionnaires. She called it limerence. It involves intrusive, almost constant thoughts about one person, a desperate longing for them to feel the same way, and moods that soar or crash depending on tiny signs of their interest.

@char A03 cafe_sunset | Uncertainty fuels it | Not knowing makes it stronger
Tennov noticed that limerence seemed to be fed by uncertainty. When the other person's feelings were clear, in either direction, the intensity often faded. When they were unclear, a smile one day and distance the next, it could grow stronger and stronger.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Whitchurch, Wilson & Gilbert | Psychological Science, 2011 | He loves me, he loves me not: uncertainty can increase romantic attraction
A study published in 2011 found something related in the lab. Erin Whitchurch, Timothy Wilson and Daniel Gilbert told women that some men had seen their profiles. Some women were told the men liked them a lot. Others were told the men rated them as average. And a third group were told it was uncertain, the men might like them a lot, or only a little. The women in the uncertain group thought about the men the most, and were the most attracted to them.

@char A12 living_room_day | The pull of not knowing | Why mixed signals grip us
That helps explain why mixed signals can be so gripping. Not knowing keeps the mind busy, turning a person over and over in our thoughts. And the more we think about someone, the more important they can seem. Intensity, in this case, may say more about uncertainty than about compatibility.

@study A04 attic_rain | Fisher, Aron & Brown | Journal of Neurophysiology, 2005 | Romantic love: an fMRI study of a neural mechanism for mate choice
In 2005, Helen Fisher, Arthur Aron and Lucy Brown scanned the brains of people who said they were intensely in love, while they looked at a photograph of their beloved. Areas of the brain linked to reward and motivation became active, including regions rich in the brain chemical dopamine. The researchers suggested that intense early romantic love works partly like a powerful motivational drive.

@char A10 cabin_fireplace | Not a verdict | Intensity is information, not proof
So here's the gentle lesson of this chapter. Intense feelings are real, and they can be wonderful. But intensity is not proof that a connection is right. It might come partly from an exciting setting, from uncertainty, or from a brain in the grip of a powerful drive. The deepest connections often feel less like a storm, and more like a calm, warm room where you can finally relax.

@char A13 rooftop_dusk | Calm connection | Safe is not boring
And that's worth saying clearly, because many people mistake calm for boring. A connection where you feel safe, where you don't have to decode mixed signals, where you know where you stand, may feel less dramatic. But it's often the kind that lasts.
