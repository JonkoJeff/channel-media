CHAPTER: The Governor
In Roman Judea, the power to execute rested with the governor. Pontius Pilate held that office from about the year twenty-six to thirty-six. A stone found at Caesarea in 1961 carries his name and his title, and it is the only inscription we have that names him. || Pilate Stone, Israel Museum || wikimedia
The inscription is partly damaged, but it appears to record that Pontius Pilatus, prefect of Judea, dedicated a building to the emperor Tiberius. It confirms two things: that he existed, and that his title was prefect, not procurator, as Tacitus later wrote. || Pilate Stone inscription, Caesarea Maritima || wikimedia
Pilate normally lived in Caesarea, on the coast, and came up to Jerusalem for major festivals, when crowds swelled and Roman troops were stationed near the Temple. He is said to have stayed either in Herod's palace in the western part of the city, or in the Antonia fortress. || Herod's palace, Jerusalem, reconstruction || wikimedia
What kind of governor was he? The Jewish philosopher Philo of Alexandria, writing a few years later, portrays him as inflexible, vindictive and cruel, accusing him of corruption and of executions without trial. Philo had reasons to write harshly, but Josephus also records several clashes. || Philo, Embassy to Gaius 299–305 || text-card
Josephus reports that Pilate brought military standards with the emperor's image into Jerusalem, provoking a mass protest, and that he used money from the Temple treasury to build an aqueduct, which led to a riot. In each case he met crowds with force. || Josephus, Jewish War 2.169–177 || text-card
A governor like this had little patience for anyone who might stir up a crowd at Passover. It's the context in which the Gospels place the trial: a tense festival, an anxious governor, and a prisoner brought before him at dawn. || Roman cohort, Trajan's Column relief || wikimedia
In Mark, Pilate asks him one question: are you the King of the Jews? Jesus answers, you say so. The chief priests accuse him of many things, but Jesus does not respond, and Pilate is amazed. || Mark 15:2–5 || text-card
The title King of the Jews is the charge that matters to Rome. A Jewish religious dispute, such as blasphemy, would not interest a Roman governor. A claim to kingship, however, was a challenge to the emperor. It is the reason that the Gospels say Pilate had to act. || Roman denarius of Tiberius || wikimedia
Luke adds a scene in which Pilate, learning that Jesus is from Galilee, sends him to Herod Antipas, who was in Jerusalem for the festival. Herod questions him at length, receives no answer, mocks him, and sends him back to Pilate. || Luke 23:6–12 || text-card
John gives the longest conversation. Jesus tells Pilate that his kingdom is not from this world, and Pilate asks, what is truth? In John, the scene is a drama of two kinds of power, with the governor strangely unsure of himself. || John 18:33–38 || text-card
The Gospels portray Pilate as reluctant, saying that he found no case against Jesus. Many historians think that this portrait has been softened. The Gospel writers were addressing a Roman world and had reasons not to blame the empire too sharply. Philo and Josephus show a much harder man. || Paula Fredriksen, Jesus of Nazareth, King of the Jews, 1999 || text-card
Even so, the basic outline has strong support. A prisoner accused of claiming to be king, brought before a governor known for decisiveness, and sentenced to the punishment that Rome kept for rebels and slaves. That fits what we know of Roman rule. || Roman fasces and eagle, Pompeii fresco || wikimedia
Then comes a scene that has often been misunderstood. A crowd gathers, and Pilate offers a choice. || Praetorium, Jerusalem, 19th century engraving || loc
CHAPTER: Barabbas and the Crowd
The Gospels say that it was the custom at the festival for the governor to release one prisoner of the people's choice. A man named Barabbas, held with rebels who had committed murder in an uprising, was in prison at the time. || Mark 15:6–7 || text-card
Pilate asks the crowd whether they want the King of the Jews released. The chief priests stir up the crowd to ask for Barabbas instead. When Pilate asks what to do with the one they call king, they cry, crucify him. || Mark 15:8–14 || text-card
In Matthew, Pilate's wife sends a message about a dream, warning him to have nothing to do with that innocent man. Pilate takes water and washes his hands in front of the crowd, saying that he is innocent of this man's blood. || Matthew 27:19, 24 || text-card
Matthew then adds that all the people answered, his blood be on us and on our children. This verse has had a terrible history. It was used for centuries to justify hatred of Jews, though there is no historical basis for treating a crowd's cry as a verdict on a people. || Matthew 27:25 || text-card
Modern scholars point out several problems with the whole scene. There is no other record of a Roman custom of releasing a prisoner at Passover, and it would be unusual for a governor like Pilate to hand over the decision to a crowd. || Raymond E. Brown, The Death of the Messiah, 1994 || text-card
Some have suggested that the Gospel writers, writing after the Jewish revolt against Rome, shifted blame from the Roman governor to the Jewish crowd, to protect their communities in a world where Rome held the power. The more brutal picture of Pilate in Philo and Josephus lends weight to this view. || Josephus, Jewish War 2.169–177 || text-card
The name Barabbas itself is interesting. It means son of the father, in Aramaic. Some manuscripts of Matthew give his full name as Jesus Barabbas, so that the crowd's choice would have been between two men called Jesus. Scholars debate whether that reading is original. || Matthew 27:16–17, Codex Koridethi || wikimedia
What the story preserves is a memory that Jesus was condemned in the shadow of a rebel's release, at a time when Roman power and popular unrest were close to the surface. Whatever the details, the outcome is not in doubt: Pilate gave the order. || Roman Judea, map of the province || map
It is worth hearing what the Gospels do not say. They do not say that a Roman governor was powerless. They do not describe all the people of Jerusalem as present. And they do not say that Jesus's own followers, many of them from the same crowds, had abandoned him by choice rather than fear. || Jerusalem crowd, 19th century photograph || loc
In Mark, the verdict is stated simply. Wanting to satisfy the crowd, Pilate released Barabbas for them. And after flogging Jesus, he handed him over to be crucified. The punishment has begun even before the cross. || Mark 15:15 || text-card
CHAPTER: The Soldiers' Game
Before crucifixion, a Roman prisoner was usually flogged. The practice was brutal and was meant to weaken the condemned, and to make a public example. Mark and Matthew mention it only briefly, in a single phrase, without describing the suffering. || Mark 15:15; Matthew 27:26 || text-card
Josephus gives an example of what a Roman scourging could mean. He describes a man named Jesus son of Ananias, who predicted the Temple's destruction in the years before the revolt. The Jewish authorities beat him, then handed him to the Roman governor, who had him scourged until his bones showed. || Josephus, Jewish War 6.300–309 || text-card
After the flogging, the soldiers take Jesus into the governor's headquarters and gather the whole cohort. They dress him in a purple cloak, press a crown of thorns on his head, and salute him with the words, hail, King of the Jews. || Mark 15:16–19 || text-card
They strike his head with a reed, spit on him, and kneel in mock homage. The scene is a parody of a royal court. A Roman soldier knew exactly what a claimed king looked like, and the soldiers made a cruel joke of it. || Roman Imperial court scene, Ara Pacis || wikimedia
Historians note that mock-kingship scenes appear in other ancient sources. The Jewish writer Philo describes an incident in Alexandria in the year thirty-eight in which a crowd dressed a madman as a king to mock the Jewish king Agrippa. Such games were a known form of humiliation. || Philo, Against Flaccus 36–39 || text-card
The crown of thorns is a vivid image, but historians cannot say exactly what plant was used, and the Gospels do not say how it was made. In art, it has been drawn in countless forms, from a small circlet to a heavy wreath. || Crown of thorns reliquary, Notre-Dame de Paris || wikimedia
Then, Mark says, when they had mocked him, they stripped him of the purple cloak, put his own clothes back on him, and led him out to crucify him. The mockery ends where the Roman procedure begins. || Mark 15:20 || text-card
Notice how restrained the Gospels are. They describe what was done in a few lines, without lingering. Some ancient readers might have found that surprising, for a world used to public executions. Others may have known precisely what the short phrases implied. || Codex Alexandrinus, Mark 15 || wikimedia
The restraint is part of why later artists filled the silence. Over the centuries, painters and sculptors have imagined the scenes in great detail, shaped by the sufferings of their own times and by their own faith. || Ecce Homo, Antonio Ciseri, Galleria d'Arte Moderna || wikimedia
Now the procession leaves the city. Roman soldiers lead a condemned man out through a gate, carrying the instrument of his death, and the Gospels have a few small details about who was on the road. || Jerusalem Old City gate, 19th century photograph || loc
CHAPTER: How Rome Crucified
To understand the next scene, we need to know what crucifixion was in the Roman world. It was not a single method, but a range of cruel public punishments, usually reserved for slaves, rebels and those without citizenship. Roman citizens were normally spared it. || Martin Hengel, Crucifixion in the Ancient World, 1977 || text-card
The Roman orator Cicero called it the most cruel and disgusting punishment, and said that the word cross should be kept far from the thoughts, the eyes and the ears of a Roman citizen. For him, the shame was almost as great as the pain. || Cicero, Against Verres 2.5.165 || text-card
Josephus, describing the siege of Jerusalem in the year seventy, says that the Roman soldiers crucified so many Jewish prisoners around the walls that there was no room for the crosses, and no crosses for the bodies. He writes that the soldiers amused themselves by nailing victims in different postures. || Josephus, Jewish War 5.449–451 || text-card
This tells us something about how the Gospel accounts would have sounded to their first readers. Crucifixion was a familiar sight, and an object of horror. For the early Christians, proclaiming a crucified Messiah was a hard claim to make in public. || Arch of Titus, relief of Jewish War, Rome || wikimedia
Archaeology has given us a little more. In 1968, in a tomb at Givat HaMivtar in northern Jerusalem, excavators found an ossuary with the bones of a man named Yehohanan, with an iron nail still lodged in his heel bone. It is the only direct physical evidence of a crucified person from the first century. || Heel bone with nail, Israel Museum || wikimedia
The initial study suggested other injuries as well, but later re-examination revised several of those conclusions. The nail through the heel is still widely accepted as genuine evidence of crucifixion, though scholars differ about how the man was positioned. It is a sober reminder of how little physical evidence survives. || Joe Zias and Eliezer Sekeles, Israel Exploration Journal, 1985 || text-card
The Gospels give a few details that fit what we know. The condemned carried the crossbeam, or at least that was the custom. A soldier detail guarded the execution. A written notice stated the charge. And the victim's clothes were divided among the soldiers. || Roman execution site, reconstruction drawing || wikimedia
Mark says that the soldiers compelled a passer-by, Simon of Cyrene, the father of Alexander and Rufus, to carry the cross. Mark's mention of the sons suggests that readers knew the family. Cyrene was a city in North Africa with a Jewish community. || Mark 15:21 || text-card
John says only that Jesus went out carrying the cross by himself. The Synoptic Gospels and John differ here, as they do in many details. Each tells the story for readers, and each has its own focus. We shall see more of such differences in the hours ahead. || John 19:17 || text-card
Women follow the procession, and in Luke, Jesus turns to them and says not to weep for him but for themselves and their children. Luke also mentions a large crowd following. In Mark, some of the women who had followed him from Galilee watch from a distance. || Luke 23:27–31 || text-card
This is a good moment to think about the place itself. All four Gospels call it by the same name, an Aramaic word that Mark translates as the place of a skull. Where was it? And how do we know? || Golgotha, Jerusalem, 19th century photograph || loc
CHAPTER: The Place Called Golgotha
Golgotha is the Aramaic word that Mark translates as the place of a skull, and in Latin it became Calvary. The Gospels say that it was outside the city, near a road, and near a garden with a tomb. They do not say more. || Mark 15:22; John 19:17–20 || text-card
Why a skull? Some think the hill looked like one. Others think the name came from the execution ground itself. The Gospels do not explain, and the ancient writers do not give us a clear answer. We can only say that the name suggests a place of death. || Aramaic Gospel manuscript, Peshitta || wikimedia
John says that the place was near the city, and that the sign above the cross was written in Hebrew, Latin and Greek, so that many Jews read it. That detail suggests a road near a gate, where a public execution would be seen by travellers. || John 19:20 || text-card
Roman law and Jewish custom both placed burial grounds and execution sites outside the walls. In the first century, the city's northern wall had not yet been extended, and the area to the north-west was a disused quarry, with tombs cut into the rock. || Jerusalem quarry and tombs, Church of the Holy Sepulchre excavations || wikimedia
The tradition that identifies the site goes back to the fourth century. After Constantine became sole emperor, he ordered a church to be built over the place that local Christians pointed out as Golgotha and the tomb. Eusebius of Caesarea describes the work in his Life of Constantine. || Eusebius, Life of Constantine 3.25–40 || text-card
According to Eusebius, the site had been buried under a Roman temple, built when the emperor Hadrian reshaped the city after the Bar Kokhba revolt, in the second century. Workers removed the pagan structure, dug down, and discovered a tomb. The church was dedicated in the year 335. || Church of the Holy Sepulchre, interior || wikimedia
That church still stands, and is known as the Church of the Holy Sepulchre. Inside the building today, pilgrims stand before a rocky outcrop said to be Golgotha, and an edicule said to enclose the tomb. Several Christian communities share the church, each caring for part of it. || Holy Sepulchre edicule, Jerusalem || wikimedia
Archaeologists have studied the site closely. Excavations under the church have shown that the area was indeed a quarry, with first-century tombs nearby, outside the walls of the time. That fits the Gospel description, though it cannot prove that the exact spot is correct. || Martin Biddle, The Tomb of Christ, 1999 || text-card
A second site, the Garden Tomb, north of the Damascus Gate, has been favoured by some Protestant visitors since the nineteenth century. Most archaeologists date its tombs to the Iron Age, centuries before Jesus, and do not regard it as the Gospel location. Many pilgrims find it a peaceful place all the same. || Garden Tomb, Jerusalem || wikimedia
So we may say, carefully, that the traditional site is plausible, because it fits the evidence for a first-century quarry outside the walls, and because a local memory seems to have been kept. But historians stop short of certainty, as they do with many sites. || Aerial view, Old City of Jerusalem || wikimedia
That is where the procession arrives. The Gospels now slow down, and each tells the hours on the hill in its own way. || Rock of Calvary, Holy Sepulchre || wikimedia
