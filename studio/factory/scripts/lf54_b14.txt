@chapter A03 cabin_fireplace | Apes who catch yawns
Close your eyes for a moment, if you like, and leave the train behind. Picture a warm enclosure in the afternoon, where a group of chimpanzees are resting. One of them is looking at a small screen. On it, another chimpanzee opens its mouth very wide. And after a little while, the watcher's mouth begins to open too.

@study N29 rooftop_dusk | James Anderson, Masako Myowa-Yamakoshi and Tetsuro Matsuzawa | Proceedings of the Royal Society B, published 2004 | Chimpanzees watching videos
In a study published in 2004, James Anderson, Masako Myowa-Yamakoshi and Tetsuro Matsuzawa showed videos to six adult female chimpanzees. Some videos showed other chimpanzees yawning. Others showed them making open-mouthed movements that weren't yawns. The researchers counted how often each watching chimpanzee yawned under the two conditions.

@stat A12 autumn_overlook | Chimpanzees who caught yawns from video | 2 of 6 | adult females yawned significantly more to yawn videos
Two of the six adult females yawned significantly more when watching the yawning videos. None showed the opposite pattern. Three infant chimpanzees who were present didn't yawn to the videos at all. Two out of six is a tiny sample, of course, but it was an early and striking sign that the contagious yawn might not belong to humans alone.

@char A10 bedroom_night | A familiar pattern | Adults yes, infants no
Notice how that echoes what we heard about children. Some adults caught yawns, others didn't, and the very young stayed out of it. With only a handful of animals, one shouldn't build too much on the resemblance. Still, it's the kind of parallel that makes researchers sit up and design a larger study.

@study N09 attic_rain | Elisa Demuru and Elisabetta Palagi | PLoS ONE, published 2012 | Bonobos at the Apenheul Primate Park
Eight years later, Elisa Demuru and Elisabetta Palagi published observations of bonobos, close relatives of chimpanzees, at the Apenheul Primate Park in the Netherlands. They watched a group of twelve animals for about three months in 2009, over five hundred hours, recording more than twelve hundred yawns as they happened naturally, with no screens involved.

@list N10 living_room_day | What the bonobo observations showed | Yawns spread within the group ;; Most catching came in the first minute ;; Stronger among kin and friends ;; A hint that females' yawns spread more
Yawns clearly spread among the adults, and most of the catching happened within the first minute. It was more frequent between relatives and friends than between weakly bonded animals, roughly twice as high on their measure. There was also a trend for yawns from females to be caught more often than yawns from males, though the group held only two adult males.

@char N20 cafe_sunset | Close ties again | A shared theme
Kin and friends first: that's the same ordering we heard from the human observations, and one of the same researchers took part in both. It's encouraging to see the pattern in two species, and it's also a reason to want other teams to look. A dozen animals in one park can show something interesting without showing that all bonobos everywhere behave alike.

@photo lf54_b14_01.jpg | Forest canopy in morning mist
A forest canopy in soft morning mist. Somewhere in places like this, wild apes wake, stretch and yawn at the start of the day, much as Oskar did at six this morning. The resemblance isn't a coincidence we need to explain away. It's a reminder that many of our smallest habits are older than any word we have for them.

@char N01 cabin_fireplace | Now to the grassland | A much bigger cat
Our next observers weren't watching apes at all. They were out on the grassland, watching lions, which spend a great deal of their time lying around, and therefore offer excellent opportunities to anyone interested in yawning. A lion's yawn is hard to miss. The question was whether it travels, and if it does, what comes after.

@study N27 autumn_overlook | Grazia Casetta, Andrea Paolo Nolfo and Elisabetta Palagi | Animal Behaviour, published 2021 | Wild lions in South Africa
In 2021, Grazia Casetta, Andrea Paolo Nolfo and Elisabetta Palagi published a study of nineteen lions on the Greater Makalali Private Game Reserve in South Africa, observed over four months in 2019. They recorded when lions yawned, which pride members could see them, and what those animals did in the minutes that followed.

@stat A03 rooftop_dusk | After seeing a pride member yawn | About 139x | as likely to yawn within three minutes, as reported
According to the reporting of their results, a lion that had just seen a pride member yawn was about a hundred and thirty-nine times as likely to yawn within three minutes as one that hadn't. That is an enormous ratio, partly because without a trigger a yawn in any given short window can be quite rare. The spread itself was very clear.

@char A10 attic_rain | And then what? | Moving together
The second finding is the one that caught people's imagination. When a lion had caught another lion's yawn, it was then far more likely to copy that same animal's next movements, such as getting up and walking off. The reported figure was about eleven times as likely. The shared yawn seemed to be followed by shared action.

@curve N10 bedroom_night | The proposed chain | Time | Togetherness | 0.7 | yawn, then rise together
The researchers suggested that contagious yawning may help a pride get into step, so that animals shift state together and move as a group. This line is simply a picture of that proposed sequence, from one lion's yawn, to another's, to both rising. It illustrates the idea and doesn't plot the study's measurements.

@char N29 living_room_day | Why togetherness might matter | Safety and hunting
For a lion, being in step with others is no small thing. Pride members hunt together, guard cubs together and defend their ground together. An easy, quiet signal that helps everyone become alert at the same moment could be useful. Could. The study shows a link between catching a yawn and copying movements. The longer story about its benefits is an interpretation.

@char A12 cafe_sunset | Back to the signalling idea | A third strand
Still, this gives the signalling idea some flesh. If yawns gather around changes of state, as the human diaries suggested, and if they pass between companions, then a group could use them, without any intention at all, to drift toward the same state. Winding down together. Waking up together. That's a gentle and rather appealing possibility.

@list N09 cabin_fireplace | Animals so far | Some adult chimpanzees caught video yawns ;; Bonobos caught more from kin and friends ;; Wild lions caught yawns and then moved alike ;; Samples were small
So from our animal tour: two of six adult chimpanzees caught yawns from video, bonobos passed yawns most among relatives and friends, and wild lions who caught a yawn went on to mirror the yawner's movements. Each study used a modest number of animals, and together they suggest that contagious yawning has company beyond our own species.

@char N20 rooftop_dusk | A word on other animals | Careful claims
Researchers have reported signs of contagious yawning in a few other social animals as well, including some birds. The list is still being argued over, and many species have been studied little or not at all. Absence from the list may mean that an animal doesn't catch yawns, or only that nobody has yet found a fair way to check.
