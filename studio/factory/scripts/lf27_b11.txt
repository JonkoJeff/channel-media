@chapter A06 cafe_sunset | Teaching people to step in
If the situation shapes whether we help, then it should be possible to prepare people for the situations. That is the practical promise of this whole field. And psychologists have tested it, beginning with a simple idea: teach people about the bystander effect and see if they behave differently.

@char A04 attic_rain | A neat experiment | Does knowing help?
In the late 1970s, a team of psychologists designed a short study. Some students attended a lecture about the bystander effect, including the research on diffusion of responsibility. Others heard a lecture on a different topic. Some time later, the students were placed in a staged emergency, to see whether the lecture had made a difference.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Beaman, Barnes, Klentz & McQuirk | Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin, 1978 | Increasing helping rates through information dissemination: teaching pays
The students did not know that the later scene was connected to the lecture. They were walking past a person who had apparently collapsed. Those who had heard the lecture about bystander behaviour were more likely to stop and help than those who had heard the other lecture. A short piece of teaching about the pitfalls of crowds appeared to make students more helpful.

@list A03 living_room_day | What the lecture might have changed | Awareness that crowds can freeze people ;; A habit of questioning the calm of others ;; A sense of personal responsibility ;; Readiness to act
The study was small and has not been repeated many times, so it is best treated as encouraging rather than conclusive. But it matches a wider finding: that knowing about a trap can help people avoid it.

@chapter A13 rooftop_dusk | Programmes in schools and colleges
Since then, many programmes have been developed to teach bystander skills, especially to young adults, mostly with the aim of reducing sexual violence and bullying. These programmes typically teach people to notice risky situations, to take responsibility, and to practise safe ways of intervening.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Banyard, Moynihan & Plante | Journal of Community Psychology, 2007 | Sexual violence prevention through bystander education: an experimental evaluation
Victoria Banyard and her colleagues at the University of New Hampshire developed a community-based approach in which every person is seen as a potential bystander and helper, and not as a potential perpetrator or victim. They tested a training programme with college students, comparing them with a group who did not receive it, and reported improvements in attitudes and in reported willingness to act.

@char A12 living_room_day | The wider evidence | Mixed but promising
Reviews of these programmes find that they tend to increase people's intentions and reports of intervening, though effects on actual harmful behaviour are less clear. Researchers continue to refine them, and are cautious about claims. The consistent finding is that practical, skills-based training works better than lectures alone.

@list A12 living_room_day | What good bystander training includes | Practice, not just information ;; Realistic scenarios ;; A range of safe options, not only direct confrontation ;; Support for people who act
The range of options matters. Not everyone is comfortable confronting a stranger, and nobody should put themselves in danger. Good programmes teach several ways of helping, from distracting the person at risk to getting help from someone else.

@chapter A19 living_room_day | The options available
Researchers and educators often describe a few basic choices for a bystander. You can intervene directly, if it's safe. You can distract, by creating an interruption. You can delegate, by getting someone else to help, such as security or a staff member. And you can delay, by checking in with the person afterwards.

@list A03 living_room_day | Four ways to help | Direct: speak up if it is safe ;; Distract: interrupt the situation ;; Delegate: ask someone in authority to help ;; Delay: check in afterwards
These are practical rather than scientific categories, and they appear in many training programmes. What the research supports is the broader principle behind them: that having a plan, and knowing there's more than one way to act, makes people more likely to do something.

@char A01 cafe_sunset | An important freedom | You don't need to be a hero
This is liberating. Helping does not have to mean a dramatic confrontation. Quietly asking a person if they're all right, sitting beside someone who looks uncomfortable, or telling a bartender about a worrying situation are all forms of help. They're modest, and they work.

@chapter A06 cafe_sunset | Rehearsal
There's good evidence that rehearsing a response makes it easier to carry out when the time comes. Psychologists call such a plan an implementation intention, an if-then rule that links a situation to an action.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Gollwitzer & Sheeran | Advances in Experimental Social Psychology, 2006 | Implementation intentions and goal achievement: a meta-analysis of effects and processes
Peter Gollwitzer and Paschal Sheeran combined results from ninety-four studies and found that people who formed an if-then plan, such as, if I see X, then I will do Y, were substantially more likely to follow through on their goals than people who only formed the intention. The effect was of medium to large size.

@list A12 living_room_day | If-then plans for helping | If someone looks distressed, then I will ask if they are okay ;; If I see a crowd standing still, then I will check what is wrong ;; If someone is being harassed, then I will stand near them and ask a question ;; If I can't act safely, then I will get help
A plan like this removes a decision from the moment of stress. You don't have to decide whether to act. You've already decided, and only have to carry it out.

@list A03 living_room_day | Words to keep from this chapter | Bystander training ;; Implementation intention ;; Delegate ;; Skills-based training
Four terms from this chapter. Bystander training teaches people to recognise and respond to situations. An implementation intention is an if-then plan. To delegate is to ask someone better placed to act. And skills-based training means practising actions, not only learning facts.

@list A12 living_room_day | Make your own if-then plan | Choose one situation where you hesitate ;; Write the if-then sentence ;; Say it aloud twice ;; Review it next week
Here is how to make your own plan. Choose one situation where you tend to hesitate, such as seeing someone upset in public. Write an if-then sentence for it. Say it aloud twice. And review it in a week. Picture this scene: a woman who always freezes when a stranger seems unwell has a plan. If someone looks faint, I will ask, are you all right? On a crowded train, a man leans against the door looking grey. She asks. He says he needs to sit. Three people make room. The plan took ten seconds to write, and it changed the minute.

@list A12 living_room_day | Pause and reflect | What would I like to be able to do in an emergency? ;; Have I ever learned first aid? ;; Which if-then plan could I write? ;; Who could practise with me?
A pause to think about preparation. What would you like to be able to do if something happened in front of you? Many people say they would like to know first aid, to stay calm, or to know what to say. Those are all things that can be learned. If you've never done a first aid course, think about whether this might be the time. And think about the plan we wrote. If someone looks distressed, then I will ask if they are all right. If I see a crowd standing still, then I will check what is wrong. Sentences like these seem too simple to matter, but the studies suggest that they work, precisely because they spare us from deciding in the moment. Who could you practise with? A friend, a family member or a colleague might enjoy saying them aloud together. Preparation is a quiet form of courage.

@photo lf27_b11_01.jpg | A classroom, ready for practice
An empty classroom, set up for practice. Skills are built in rooms like this, where people can try, stumble and try again, long before the moment that counts. The most encouraging finding in this chapter is also the simplest: people who were taught about the pitfalls of crowds did better. Knowledge of the trap is part of the way out of it, and you now have that knowledge.

@char A15 cabin_fireplace | A gentle thought | Preparation is kindness
If you have ever wished you had known what to do, you are in good company. The research is encouraging: a little knowledge and a little rehearsal go a long way. You can prepare to be kind, and that preparation is itself an act of kindness.

@char A08 cabin_fireplace | Looking back | What training taught us
So what did we learn? That teaching about the bystander effect made students more helpful in one study. That skills-based programmes seem to improve intentions and reported action. That there are several safe ways to help. And that if-then plans make action more likely.
