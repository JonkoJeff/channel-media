@chapter A12 living_room_day | The limits of working memory
Try a small test. I'm going to say a string of digits, and I want you to hold them in mind. Seven, one, four, nine, two, eight, five. Now say them back. For most people that's about right, a challenge but possible. Add two or three more digits, and things start to slip. That slipping point tells us something very important about how the mind works.

@char A04 attic_rain | The bottleneck | A small mental workspace
The part of the mind that holds things for a few seconds while you work on them is called working memory. Think of it as a small desk. It's where you hold a phone number while you dial it, or the first half of a sentence while you wait for the second. Everything you learn has to pass across this desk. And the desk is small.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Miller | Psychological Review, 1956 | The magical number seven, plus or minus two
In 1956, George Miller, at Harvard University, published one of the most quoted titles in psychology: The Magical Number Seven, Plus or Minus Two. He surveyed experiments on how many separate things people could keep track of, from musical tones to digits, and found it was often around seven.

@char A12 living_room_day | A revision | Probably nearer four
But later research suggested that seven was generous. In 2001, Nelson Cowan, at the University of Missouri, reviewed the evidence in a paper in Behavioral and Brain Sciences. When people couldn't rehearse or group items, the real capacity seemed to be about four chunks.

@stat A14 conspiracy_board | Estimated capacity of working memory | About 4 chunks | when rehearsal and grouping are prevented, according to Cowan's 2001 review
Four. That's a very small number, and it shapes how everything should be taught. If a lesson throws six new ideas at you at once, you cannot hold them, no matter how clever you are. The desk is simply too small.

@chapter A13 rooftop_dusk | The power of chunking
But Miller also noticed something hopeful, and it's in his title. The unit isn't a digit or a letter. It's a chunk, and a chunk can be as big as you like, so long as it's meaningful to you.

@char A12 living_room_day | An example | One chunk or nine?
Look at this string of letters: F, B, I, C, I, A, N, A, S, A. Ten separate letters would overflow the desk. But if you recognise them as the FBI, the CIA and NASA, you've only got three chunks. Same letters, much smaller load. The difference is knowledge. Experts see chunks where beginners see a jumble.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Chase & Simon | Cognitive Psychology, 1973 | Perception in chess
The classic demonstration comes from chess. William Chase and Herbert Simon showed chess positions to players for five seconds and asked them to reproduce them. Masters were far better than beginners. But when the pieces were arranged at random, in ways that never occur in real games, the masters' advantage mostly disappeared.

@char A04 attic_rain | What it means | Experts chunk
The masters weren't blessed with a larger working memory. They had stored thousands of familiar patterns, so a real position was a handful of chunks, while a random one was a pile of loose pieces. Expertise, in other words, grows partly by stretching the workspace, using what's already in long-term memory.

@photo lf20_b11_01.jpg | A small tray, only a few stones
There's an encouraging side to this. It means that learning begins to speed up as you go. The more you know about a subject, the easier it is to take in more about it, since new information attaches to existing chunks.

@chapter A12 living_room_day | How far chunking can go
One experiment shows how far this can be pushed. In 1980, K. Anders Ericsson, whom we met earlier, with William Chase and Steve Faloon, reported a case study in Science.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Ericsson, Chase & Faloon | Science, 1980 | Acquisition of a memory skill
A student, known as SF, was a runner and an ordinary college student, with an average memory. He was asked to practise remembering strings of digits an hour a day. At first he managed about seven digits, just like everyone else. Over many months, and roughly two hundred and thirty hours of practice, he reached seventy-nine digits.

@char A12 living_room_day | His method | Running times
How? SF was a keen runner, so he began to see groups of digits as running times. Three, four, nine, two became three minutes and forty-nine point two seconds, a very good mile. Then he arranged the groups into larger groups. His working memory hadn't grown. His chunks had.

@char A04 attic_rain | The catch | It didn't transfer
There was a catch, too. When the researchers switched him from digits to letters, his span fell back to about the normal six or seven. The skill belonged to digits. That's a first hint of a lesson we'll return to: training generally makes you better at the thing you trained, and not at everything.

@chapter A13 rooftop_dusk | Cognitive load
In the 1980s, an Australian educational psychologist, John Sweller, took these ideas into the classroom. His theory, called cognitive load theory, asks a simple question: how much is each task asking of the small desk?

@list A03 living_room_day | Three kinds of load | The difficulty of the material itself ;; Load added by confusing presentation ;; Effort that goes into building understanding
Teachers can't change the first, but they can reduce the second. A messy slide, a long block of text or a speaker who keeps jumping topics spends your precious four chunks on nothing. Good teaching removes that waste, so that the effort goes where it counts.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Sweller & Cooper | Cognition and Instruction, 1985 | The use of worked examples as a substitute for problem solving in learning algebra
Their most famous finding is called the worked example effect. Students learning algebra either solved a series of problems, or studied worked examples with the steps shown and then solved a few. The worked-example group learned faster, made fewer errors and solved the test problems more quickly.

@char A12 living_room_day | Why examples win | Beginners can't search
For a beginner, solving problems by trial and error uses up the whole desk, searching for a way, and nothing is left for learning the pattern. A worked example shows the route, so all the space goes to understanding it.

@char A04 attic_rain | A twist | Experts need the opposite
But there's a twist. Slava Kalyuga and Sweller found, in a series of studies, that as people become more expert, worked examples stop helping, and can even get in the way. Then it's better to solve problems by yourself. They called it the expertise reversal effect. The right method depends on where you are on the road.

@list A03 living_room_day | What to do with a small desk | Learn one new idea at a time ;; Start with worked examples, then fade the help ;; Cut out distractions while you are learning ;; Name the pieces, so they become chunks ;; Come back to it, so it moves into long-term memory
There's relief in that list. If a topic is overwhelming, that doesn't mean you're slow. It means you're trying to fit too much on the desk. Break the topic down and the difficulty often melts.

@chapter A06 cafe_sunset | Distraction and the desk
There's one more consequence, and it's the modern one. If the desk has four spaces, then every notification that pulls your attention takes one of them. This has been studied directly.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Ward, Duke, Gneezy & Bos | Journal of the Association for Consumer Research, 2017 | Brain drain: the mere presence of one's own smartphone reduces available cognitive capacity
Adrian Ward and colleagues asked people to do tests of working memory and problem-solving, with their phones either on the desk, face down, in a pocket or bag, or in another room. Those with the phone in another room did best, and those with the phone on the desk did worst, even though the phones were silent and face down.

@char A04 attic_rain | A fair note | Debated, but sensible
This result is a good one to keep in perspective. Other teams have had trouble reproducing it exactly, so the size of the effect is debated. But it points in a direction that is easy to try for yourself. If you want to learn, put the phone in another room. The cost is a few minutes of comfort. The potential gain is a clearer desk.

@char A01 cafe_sunset | In practice | A cleared desk
So when you sit down to study, clear the desk. Close the extra tabs, silence the phone, and keep one task in front of you. It isn't dramatic, and it won't feel like a hack. But it makes room, and room is what learning is made of.

@char A15 cabin_fireplace | A gentle thought | Room to think
There's something calming in knowing about this limit. You aren't supposed to hold everything in your head at once. Nobody can. That's why we write lists, draw diagrams and talk things through. Our tools are extensions of the little desk, and using them well is a sign of sense, and not weakness.

@char A08 cabin_fireplace | Looking back | What the desk taught us
So, what did we learn? That working memory is tiny, perhaps four chunks. That chunking, built from knowledge, stretches it. That worked examples help beginners and hinder experts. And that a nearby phone, even silent, may take a bite out of your attention.
