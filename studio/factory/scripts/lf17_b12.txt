@chapter A06 cafe_sunset | The people who think they are above average
In 1999, a paper appeared in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology with one of the best titles in the field. It was called Unskilled and Unaware of It: How Difficulties in Recognizing One's Own Incompetence Lead to Inflated Self-Assessments. The authors were two psychologists at Cornell University, David Dunning and Justin Kruger.

@char A12 living_room_day | The origin | A bank robber in lemon juice
The story of how they came to the idea is a good one. In 1995, a man named McArthur Wheeler robbed two banks in Pittsburgh in broad daylight, with his face covered in lemon juice. He believed that, because lemon juice is used as invisible ink, it would make him invisible to the cameras. He was arrested the same evening.

@char A04 attic_rain | The question | Why so sure?
Dunning read about the case and asked himself an interesting question. It wasn't only that the man was wrong. It was that he was so confident. Could it be that the skills you need to do something well are the same skills you need to tell whether you're doing it well?

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Kruger & Dunning | Cornell University, 1999 | Students rated their own ability in humour, logic and grammar
They tested this in four studies. Students at Cornell took short tests of humour, logical reasoning and English grammar. Afterwards, they were asked to estimate how well they had done, in comparison with others. Then the researchers compared each person's estimate with their actual score.

@stat A14 conspiracy_board | The lowest scorers | Bottom 25% | estimated themselves at about the 60th percentile
The pattern was striking. The students who scored in the bottom quarter, on average, rated themselves as above average, somewhere around the sixtieth percentile. Their real performance was much lower, around the twelfth. They weren't just wrong, they were wrong in a flattering direction.

@char A10 attic_rain | The top end | Experts underestimate
The best performers made the opposite mistake. The top quarter estimated themselves a little below their real position. Dunning and Kruger suggested that because the task was easy for them, they assumed it was easy for everyone else too. They weren't lacking confidence. They simply thought others were as good as they were.

@bars A05 autumn_overlook | How people rated themselves | Average self-estimate (percentile) | Bottom quarter:58 ;; Second quarter:60 ;; Third quarter:67 ;; Top quarter:70
These figures are rounded and based on the paper's first study. The most important thing to notice is that, although everyone guessed in the middle to upper range, the real scores were widely spread. Those who knew the least had the least information about how much there was to know.

@chapter A13 rooftop_dusk | The double burden
Dunning and Kruger called it a double burden. People who lack skill make mistakes, and then can't recognise them. And there was a hopeful second half of the story. In one of their studies, they gave poor performers a short training course in logical reasoning. After it, the participants became much better at judging how badly they had done before.

@char A01 cafe_sunset | The gentle point | Learning shows you what you don't know
That might be the most useful finding of all. Getting better doesn't only make you more skilled, it makes you better at seeing your limits. Think of the beginner who says that they don't know how little they know, and the expert who says that the more they learn, the more there is to learn.

@char A19 cabin_fireplace | A familiar feeling | The curve of confidence
You may have met a graph that's circulated online. It has a peak of confidence at the start, a deep valley of doubt, and a slow climb after that. It's called the Dunning-Kruger curve, and it's very popular. But it's worth knowing that it doesn't come from the original paper.

@list A03 living_room_day | What the popular version gets wrong | The famous mountain-and-valley curve is not in the 1999 paper ;; The paper didn't say that fools are the most confident people ;; It showed a gap between estimates and results ;; The gap was biggest at low skill
That's a fair summary. The original finding was a more modest one than the memes suggest. The bottom group overestimated, the top group slightly underestimated, and everyone was better at spotting skill in others once they'd learned it themselves. There was no claim that stupid people are the most sure of everything.

@chapter A10 attic_rain | The criticisms
Like every study in this video, this one has been challenged. And in this case, the criticisms are interesting, because some of them are about statistics. In the 2000s and 2010s, several researchers argued that part of the pattern could appear for reasons that have nothing to do with skill or self-awareness.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Nuhfer et al. | Numeracy, 2016 | Statistical arguments about self-assessment data
One argument is that when you plot people's own estimates against their scores, you will tend to see the bottom group overestimate and the top group underestimate, even if the data were random. This is a statistical effect known as regression to the mean, along with a general tendency for people to rate themselves as above average.

@char A04 attic_rain | The reply | Part of the pattern is real
Dunning and others have answered that the basic effect holds even when you control for these things. And studies in the years since, across different tasks and countries, have found that people with low skill generally overestimate how well they've done. But how much of the pattern is true self-blindness, and how much is statistical, is still debated.

@char A12 living_room_day | A cross-check | Do people ever know?
There's also evidence that people are not hopeless at judging themselves. In many tasks, people's estimates track their real ability to a moderate degree. We're better judges of ourselves in areas where we get quick, clear feedback, such as sports, and worse where feedback is vague, such as leadership, humour or social skills.

@stat A06 cafe_sunset | Where we misjudge most | Fuzzy skills | where feedback is slow, rare or unclear
That makes sense. If you shoot a basketball, you immediately see if it goes in. But if you tell a joke, or give a speech, or manage a team, the feedback can be mixed, delayed or polite. And so our picture of ourselves can drift away from the facts, in either direction.

@char A15 cabin_fireplace | The kinder reading | Everyone has blind spots
It's also worth saying that the effect isn't about other people. Each of us is a beginner at most things, and in those areas we are all likely to be a bit too sure or a bit too unsure. A kinder way of reading the research is that blind spots are normal, and the sensible response is to go and look for them.

@list A12 living_room_day | Ways to see your blind spots | Ask for specific feedback from someone you trust ;; Test yourself against a clear standard ;; Learn a little more about the field ;; Notice when you feel certain and rarely check
None of these is complicated. And the most important one might be the last. The feeling of certainty isn't evidence. It's a feeling, and it can be as strong when we're wrong as when we're right.

@photo lf17_b12_01.jpg | Glasses, a mirror, an open book
Glasses, a mirror and a book, an odd little still life, and yet it describes the whole idea. To see ourselves clearly, we need something to look through, something to look into, and something to learn from. Self-knowledge is a skill, and like any skill, it improves with practice.

@chapter A13 rooftop_dusk | What twelve experiments leave us
We've covered a lot of ground. We began with a child and a marshmallow, and ended with a robber and some lemon juice. In between, we met rooms full of people giving wrong answers, a calm voice asking others to go on, a doll, two mothers made of wire and cloth, a crowd, a hospital ward, a dog, a baby, a forgotten list, a basement and a boring task.

@list A03 living_room_day | Twelve experiments, twelve lessons | Waiting: self-control depends on trust and context ;; Asch: one ally can break the group's spell ;; Milgram: authority is powerful, and so is refusing ;; Bobo: children learn by watching ;; Harlow: comfort is a need ;; Bystanders: say it out loud, ask one person ;; Rosenhan: labels distort what we see ;; Pavlov: what's learned can be unlearned ;; Ebbinghaus: space it out, test yourself ;; Stanford: roles are not destiny ;; Festinger: we justify what we do ;; Dunning and Kruger: look for your blind spots
That's a long list, and it's quite a human one. These aren't really lessons about strange laboratories. They're about the ordinary things we do every day, among people we know. And they're a good reminder that psychology is a conversation, not a set of final answers.

@char A01 cafe_sunset | A thread through them | We are social, storytelling creatures
If there is one thread that ties them together, it's that we aren't isolated minds. We watch, we copy, we belong, we justify, we label and we are labelled. We're shaped by the rooms we're in and the people in them. And, at the same time, we have more choice than we often think.

@char A04 attic_rain | The other thread | Be careful with famous results
There's a second thread, and it's a more sobering one. Many of the most famous experiments in psychology have been challenged, corrected or complicated, from the marshmallow test to the Stanford basement. That doesn't make psychology worthless. It makes it a science that notices its own mistakes, which is how a science should be.

@char A12 living_room_day | A habit worth keeping | Hold ideas loosely
So the habit I'd suggest is this: when you hear that a study has proved something about human nature, ask who was studied, how big the study was, whether anyone has repeated it, and what the effect really was. It takes only a moment, and it protects you against a great many confident headlines.

@char A19 cabin_fireplace | And a kinder habit | Be gentle with people
And there's a gentle habit too. If we know that situations pull on people, that labels distort, that groups press and that confidence misleads, we can afford to be a little kinder, to others and to ourselves. Most of us are doing the best we can, in rooms we didn't design.

@char A15 cabin_fireplace | A last thought | Pick one
If you would like to take something away, pick one experiment, and one small habit, and try it this week. Write down what you want to remember and test yourself tomorrow. Be the ally in a meeting. Ask a stranger if they're okay. Look twice before you label someone. Small things, repeated gently, are how people change.

The idea that our reasons for doing things are sometimes written after the fact? The one that lingers is probably the one that touches something in your own life, and that is a good place to start thinking.
