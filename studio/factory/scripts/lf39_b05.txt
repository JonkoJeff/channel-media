@chapter A13 cafe_sunset | Chills in a scanner
So far, we've followed the shiver through the skin and through a few surprising clues. A drug that blocks the body's own opioids seemed, for some listeners, to soften the thrill. But a clue isn't a map. If music really reaches into the brain's pleasure systems, someone would have to look inside a living brain at the very moment a chill arrives. And in 2001, two researchers in Montreal did exactly that.

@study A12 living_room_day | Anne Blood and Robert Zatorre | Published in 2001 | McGill University: 10 musicians, their own chill-inducing music, PET scans
What does a chill look like inside a living brain? Anne Blood and Robert Zatorre, at McGill University in Montreal, set out to see. Their study was published in 2001 in the journal PNAS. They invited 10 musicians into the lab. And they asked each of them to do something a little unusual. Instead of listening to music chosen by the scientists, every person brought a piece of their own, one that reliably gave them chills.

@char A05 living_room_day | Why your own music | A chill can't be ordered
That detail matters more than it might seem. Chills are personal. A passage that sends a shiver down one person's arms can leave the next person completely still. So if the researchers had picked the music, many listeners might have felt nothing at all, and there'd be nothing to measure. By letting each musician choose a piece they already knew would work, Blood and Zatorre made the chill far more likely to show up on time.

@char A10 cafe_sunset | A clever twist | Someone else's song
There was a clever twist, too. Each person's chosen music could also serve as someone else's comparison music. One musician's beloved passage was, for another musician, just a pleasant tune with no special pull. So the very same sounds could be heard by one brain with a shiver and by another without one. That helped the researchers separate the feeling of the chill from simply hearing music.

@list A18 rooftop_dusk | How PET works | Busy areas need more blood ;; A tiny, harmless tracer ;; A map of working areas
Now, how do you watch a brain feel something? Blood and Zatorre used a method called PET, short for positron emission tomography. The idea, put gently, is this. Busy parts of the brain need more blood. A tiny, harmless amount of a tracer is carried along with that blood, and the scanner picks up where it gathers. So the pictures show which areas are working harder while the music plays. It's slow and a little blurry, but it lets us peek inside.

@char A13 cabin_fireplace | Lying very still | Listening anyway
Imagine being one of those musicians. You're lying very still, your head inside a large ring, the room quiet and a little clinical. And then, through headphones, comes the music you love most. The piece you've played or heard a hundred times. Somewhere in it is the moment you know is coming. And even here, in this strange setting, the shiver still finds its way to you.

@list A15 autumn_overlook | Where activity rose | The ventral striatum ;; The midbrain ;; The orbitofrontal cortex ;; The insula
So what did the scans show? As the chills grew stronger, activity rose in a group of areas linked to reward and emotion. The ventral striatum, deep in the brain, part of the reward system. The midbrain, which helps send reward signals. The orbitofrontal cortex, just behind the eyes, which helps weigh how good something is. And the insula, which helps us sense what's happening inside our own bodies.

@char A03 cafe_sunset | Where activity fell | A quieter alarm
At the same time, something else went down. Activity fell in the amygdala, a small almond-shaped area that's often involved in vigilance and alarm. It's a bit as if, while the shiver rose, part of the brain's watchfulness relaxed. Pleasure going up, and worry going down, at the very same time. The body was buzzing, the skin was tingling, and yet one of the brain's lookouts was quietly settling down.

@char A13 rooftop_dusk | Stronger chill | Stronger change
And there's one more detail worth noticing. These changes weren't simply on or off. They tracked the strength of the chill. When a musician's shiver was mild, the shift in the scans was smaller. When the shiver grew more intense, the reward areas rose further and the amygdala quieted further. The brain wasn't just hearing a favorite song. It seemed to be following the feeling itself, step by step.

Picture the hall where Theo sat, now empty, the chairs in their quiet rows. Every listener who ever sat here brought a different brain and a different history. A piece that moved one of them deeply might pass over the next like a breeze. And yet, under all those differences, the same old reward systems may have been switching on, each time a shiver arrived.

@char A12 rooftop_dusk | Old circuits | Ancient rewards
Here's the part that surprised many people. These weren't special music areas. They were the same systems that respond to other basic pleasures, like eating good food when we're hungry. Circuits that, over a very long history, helped animals stay alive. And yet here they were, lighting up for a melody, a chord, or a voice. Something with no obvious survival value at all. That's a strange and rather lovely puzzle.

@quote A19 cabin_fireplace | Music borrows the brain's oldest rewards. | Tonight's thought
So here's tonight's thought, in one line. Music borrows the brain's oldest rewards. The circuits that once helped our ancestors notice food and comfort can also respond to a pattern of sounds in the air. Nobody eats a song. Nobody needs one to survive the night. Nobody is kept warm by a chord. And yet the brain seems to treat a beautiful passage as something worth wanting.

@char A20 living_room_day | Theo's shiver | Seen from inside
Think back to Theo in that wooden hall. The hush, then the single soprano voice, then the shiver from his neck down his arms. If we could have looked inside his head at that moment, this study suggests what we might have seen. Reward areas warming up. A small alarm center easing off. A very old system saying, quietly, this is good.

@stat A15 cafe_sunset | Musicians in the study | 10 | a small first look
Of course, we should hold this gently. The study had just 10 people, and all of them were musicians, who may listen differently from the rest of us. They chose their own pieces, so each person's music was different. And a PET scan shows activity, not feelings directly. These are good reasons for care. But they're also exactly why other researchers soon set out to look again, with new tools and new questions.

@char A13 rooftop_dusk | A first look | Not the final word
Still, it was a landmark. For the first time, the shiver had been caught inside a working brain. And it pointed somewhere interesting. Not just to hearing, or to memory, but to reward, to the parts of us that make things feel worth having. If that was true, a natural next question followed. Which chemical messenger might be carrying the signal?

@char A05 living_room_day | A chemical question | One likely suspect
The striatum, one of the areas that lit up, is well known for one messenger in particular. It's called dopamine. Dopamine is often linked to rewards like food and other things we seek out. So, did music actually release dopamine in the brain? Blood and Zatorre's scans couldn't say for sure. Answering that would take a different method, and a much more patient search for listeners.

@quote A12 cabin_fireplace | A shiver is a small reward. | A gentle reminder
So here's a gentle reminder to carry with you. A shiver is a small reward. When music gives you chills, it isn't only your skin reacting. Your brain may be marking that moment as good, as something worth coming back to. Perhaps that's part of why we play the same songs again and again, hoping to find the shiver waiting there, right where we left it.

@char A10 cafe_sunset | Coming next | The moment before
And in a moment, we'll meet the team that took the next step, also at McGill, ten years later. They searched through more than two hundred people to find just a few reliable chill listeners. And what they found wasn't only about the peak. It was about the seconds just before it, the waiting, the same kind of hush that Theo sat in.
