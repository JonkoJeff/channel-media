@chapter N09 attic_rain | Older than words
Nadia's route home takes her past the edge of a small city park, its gates closed for the night, its trees dripping. She remembers taking her nephew to the zoo last summer, and the young chimpanzees chasing each other round their enclosure, mouths open, panting, clearly having the time of their lives. At the time, she'd thought, they look like they're laughing.

@char A13 living_room_day | Were they laughing? | A fair question
Was she right? It's a fair question, and a scientific one. If laughter is truly ancient, older than our species, then we'd expect to find something like it in our closest relatives, the great apes. And if we found it, we might learn something about where our own laughter came from, and what it was originally for.

@char A03 rooftop_dusk | The play face | A relaxed open mouth
Researchers who study primate behaviour have long described a particular expression in apes and monkeys, often called the play face, or the relaxed open-mouth display. The mouth is open, the teeth are mostly covered, and the face looks loose and relaxed. It appears during play-fighting, chasing and tickling, and it seems to say, this is just a game.

@study A10 autumn_overlook | Jan van Hooff | In Non-verbal Communication, edited by Robert Hinde, 1972 | A comparative approach to the phylogeny of laughter and smiling
In 1972, the Dutch primatologist Jan van Hooff published an influential comparison of these expressions across many primate species. He suggested that human laughter grew out of the play face, while the human smile grew out of a different expression, a silent bared-teeth display often linked to calming or appeasement. Two expressions, he argued, with different histories.

@char N29 living_room_day | Panting, not ha ha | The sound of ape play
When young apes play, the play face often comes with a sound: a breathy, panting noise. It doesn't sound like ha, ha, ha. It sounds more like heavy, rhythmic breathing, in and out. But it comes in the same situations where human children laugh: when they're tickled, chased or rough-and-tumbling with a friend.

@study A19 rooftop_dusk | Marina Davila Ross, Michael Owren and Elke Zimmermann | Current Biology, 2009 | Reconstructing the evolution of laughter in great apes and humans
In 2009, Marina Davila Ross and colleagues put this idea to a careful test. They recorded the sounds made by young orangutans, gorillas, chimpanzees and bonobos while they were being tickled, and compared them with recordings of tickled human babies. Then they analysed the acoustic features of each sound in detail.

@char N09 autumn_overlook | A family tree of laughter | Built from sound alone
Using only those sounds, they built a kind of family tree, grouping species by how similar their tickle sounds were. The tree they built closely matched the known family tree of the great apes and humans, worked out from genetics. In other words, laughter has changed gradually over evolutionary time, just as bodies and genes have.

@list N01 cafe_sunset | What the tickle study suggests | Ape tickle sounds are related to human laughter ;; Laughter is millions of years old ;; It changed gradually across species ;; Human laughter is one branch of an old tree
The researchers concluded that human laughter and the tickle sounds of great apes share a common origin, going back at least to the common ancestor of today's great apes and humans, millions of years ago. Our laughter, in other words, is not a recent invention. It's one branch of a very old tree.

@char N14 bedroom_night | What changed in us | From panting to ha ha
So what changed? One of the clearest differences is in the breath. Apes tend to make their laughing sound on both the in-breath and the out-breath, a back-and-forth panting. Humans mostly laugh on a single out-breath, chopping it into short pieces. Researchers have linked this to the greater control over breathing that humans need for speech.

@char N20 autumn_overlook | Why that matters | A laugh made for talking
That difference might seem small, but it matters. Laughing on one out-breath lets us produce louder, more voice-like laughter, the ha, ha, ha that carries across a restaurant. It also lets laughter slot neatly into the pauses of conversation, the punctuation effect we met earlier. Our laughter seems to have been shaped to sit beside our speech.

@char A05 street_day | Play, at its root | What laughter was for
If laughter began in play, that tells us something about its original job. Rough play can look like fighting. Chasing can look like attacking. A clear signal that says this is just a game would be very useful, so that nobody takes a playful nip seriously. The panting laugh of a young ape may be exactly that kind of signal.

@char A03 cabin_fireplace | Still true for us | Play signals in daily life
And in a way, it's still true for us. Think of how often human laughter marks something as not serious. A gentle tease followed by a laugh. A mild complaint softened with a chuckle. A small disagreement that ends in shared laughter. We still use laughter to say, don't worry, this is play, we're still friends.

@char A10 bedroom_night | A quick caution | Reading animal minds
A quick caution, though. We can measure the sounds and faces of other animals, but we can't ask them what they feel. When we say a young chimpanzee is laughing, we're describing a sound and an expression in a particular situation. Whether it feels to the chimpanzee the way laughter feels to us is something we can reasonably guess at, but can't fully know.

@list A13 rooftop_dusk | What we can say with some confidence | Apes make laugh-like sounds in play ;; Those sounds share an origin with ours ;; They appear in similar situations ;; Their inner feelings remain uncertain
So here's what we can say with some confidence. Great apes make laugh-like sounds during play and tickling. Those sounds appear to share an evolutionary origin with human laughter. They come up in situations very like the ones that make human children laugh. And what exactly they feel inside, we can only carefully infer.

Nadia thinks again of the young chimpanzees chasing each other at the zoo, panting and wide-mouthed. Her instinct had been right, more or less. She was watching a cousin of her own laughter, a sound that's been passed down and reshaped over millions of years. It's a lovely thought to walk with in the rain.
