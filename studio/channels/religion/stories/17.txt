[00:23:26] NARRATION: The three versions agree on the core. A light from heaven. Saul on the ground. A voice calling his name. Jesus identifying himself as the one Saul persecuted. A command to go into the city. But there are small differences, and readers have noticed them for centuries.

CHAPTER: Conversion or Calling?
[00:25:30] NARRATION: Is the word conversion even the right one? For centuries, Christians described the event as the moment when Saul the Jew became Paul the Christian. But in the nineteen sixties, a Lutheran scholar named Krister Stendahl challenged that picture, and the discussion has never been quite the same.
VISUAL: Krister Stendahl, Harvard Divinity School
SOURCE: wikimedia
[00:25:52] NARRATION: In a famous essay, Stendahl argued that the western tradition had read Paul through the lens of Augustine and Martin Luther, who struggled with guilt. But Paul, Stendahl said, never claims to have been tormented by guilt. He calls himself blameless under the Law. He describes what happened not as a conversion but as a call, like the call of the prophets.
VISUAL: Martin Luther, Lucas Cranach the Elder
SOURCE: wikimedia
[00:26:21] NARRATION: Stendahl pointed out that Paul speaks about his call with echoes of the prophets. When Paul says God set him apart before he was born, he is echoing Jeremiah, to whom God said, Before I formed you in the womb I knew you. And the language of being sent to the nations echoes the servant of the Lord in Isaiah.
VISUAL: Jeremiah, Michelangelo, Sistine Chapel
SOURCE: wikimedia
[00:26:48] NARRATION: If Stendahl is right, then Paul did not leave Judaism the way a person today might leave one religion for another. He believed that he had been called to a task within the story of Israel, to bring the nations to the God of Israel through the Messiah. For him the story was not abandoned. It was fulfilled.
VISUAL: Prophet Isaiah, Michelangelo, Sistine Chapel
SOURCE: wikimedia
[00:27:15] NARRATION: Other scholars push back. Alan Segal, a Jewish historian, wrote a book called Paul the Convert, in which he argued that Paul really did change communities. He compared Paul's experience with the visionary traditions of Jewish mysticism, and he saw a person who moved from one group to another while remaining deeply Jewish in his language and thought.
VISUAL: Alan Segal, Paul the Convert
SOURCE: wikimedia
[00:27:42] NARRATION: Paula Fredriksen, a historian of ancient Christianity, offers yet another view. She stresses that Paul remained a Jew who believed the end of the age was near and that the nations were about to turn to the God of Israel. In her reading, he was not leaving Judaism but trying to bring about its great hope.
VISUAL: Paula Fredriksen, Paul the Pagan's Apostle
SOURCE: wikimedia
[00:28:08] NARRATION: So when historians talk about Damascus today, they often use several words side by side. Call, revelation, conversion, reorientation. Each carries something real. And what everyone agrees about is the practical result. A fierce opponent of the movement became its tireless advocate.
