@chapter A15 street_day | Trying very hard not to
By now, Ines has been in her new flat for several weeks, and she has made a decision. Tonight, she is simply not going to look at her phone in bed. No scrolling, not even a quick glance. She plugs it in, puts it face down on the shelf, and gets under the covers feeling quite proud of herself. And then, almost at once, a very curious thing happens. The phone is all she can think about. It's as if the little rectangle on the shelf had been quietly switched on, and was now glowing in the corner of the room.

@char A10 bedroom_night | The loudest thing in the room | A phone, face down
It's a funny feeling, isn't it? The more she tells herself not to think about it, the more present it becomes. She wonders if anyone has written to her. She wonders what the weather will be on Thursday. She wonders about that one video she didn't finish. None of these thoughts were there a minute ago. They arrived exactly when she told them to leave. If you've ever tried to stop humming a song, or to forget a sentence someone said to you, you'll know the feeling very well. It isn't a flaw in Ines. It's just how a busy mind tends to behave.

@char A19 rooftop_dusk | An old experiment | Not one bear
Psychologists noticed this long ago, and there is a famous little experiment about it. It involves, of all things, a white bear. The psychologist Wegner and his colleagues asked people to say whatever came into their heads for five minutes, out loud. Then they gave them one simple instruction for that time. Try not to think of a white bear. And if a bear does come to mind, ring a small bell.

@study A13 cafe_sunset | Wegner and colleagues | Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, published 1987 | Trying not to think of a white bear
Here is what they found. People could not fully manage it. The bear kept slipping into their thoughts, and the bell kept ringing. Then came the second half. When the same people were later told they were allowed to think about the bear, they thought about it more than people who had been invited to think about it from the very beginning. The authors suggested that suppressing a thought can backfire.

@list A18 attic_rain | What the white bear showed | Trying not to think of it ;; It kept coming back ;; Later, even more ;; A different thought helped
Let's gather that up quietly. When people tried not to think about the bear, it still came to mind. Afterwards, it came to mind even more. And there was one more small detail, from a second experiment. When people were given a particular thing to think about instead, a distracting thought, they were less preoccupied with the bear afterwards. So the trying itself seemed to be part of the trouble.

@char A18 living_room_day | A small caution | Thoughts, and actions
Now, we should be a little careful here. The white bear experiment is about thoughts, not about actions. A stray thought is one thing, and reaching for a phone is another. So no one should take away the idea that trying not to do something never works. What it does give us is a gentle warning, that gritting our teeth against a pull can leave that pull sitting right at the front of our minds.

@char A15 autumn_overlook | A puzzle for Ines | Pushing and pulling
Ines doesn't know about any bear, but she knows the feeling very well. Every night she has tried the pushing approach. Be strict, be firm, stop. And every night the thing she was pushing against grew a little bigger. Perhaps, she thinks, there must be some other way to meet an urge than by shouting at it. It's a question researchers have asked as well, and we'll look at their answer next.

@chapter A03 cabin_fireplace | What works for real habits
So what does help with an everyday habit that is already strong? A research team that included Wendy Wood asked exactly that question. They didn't want to test it only in a laboratory. They wanted to know what people actually do, in ordinary life, when they try to stop doing something automatic, and which of those things really seems to make a difference.

@study A10 cafe_sunset | Quinn, Pascoe, Wood and Neal | Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin, published 2010 | Which strategies help with strong habits?
In their diary studies, university students carried a small diary around with them. Every time they tried to resist an unwanted response, they wrote it down, and later they rated how well it had gone. The unwanted responses were very ordinary ones. Sleeping too late. Eating junk food. Putting things off. Then the researchers compared the different strategies people had used, one against another.

@stat A19 cabin_fireplace | One week with a pocket diary | 99 | university students in the first diary study
The first diary study asked ninety-nine students at Duke University to keep this record for a full week. A second one ran for two weeks. It's a lovely, humble kind of science. Nobody is in a laboratory, nobody is watching. People are simply writing down, in the moment, the small ordinary battles of their days, and then saying honestly how those went.

@char A15 cafe_sunset | The one that stood out | Watching, quietly
And here is what stood out. For strong habits, one strategy was the most effective, and it was something the authors called vigilant monitoring. In plain words, that means thinking, don't do it, and watching carefully for slipups. Doing nothing at all was the least successful choice. And when the researchers looked at distraction, or at changing the surroundings, those did not seem to help with strong habits in the same way.

@list A12 rooftop_dusk | How people tried | Doing nothing ;; Vigilant monitoring ;; Controlling the surroundings ;; Distraction
Here are the main strategies, as the students used them. They could do nothing in particular. They could keep a watchful eye on themselves. They could try to control what was around them, hiding the cookies, say. Or they could distract themselves. For temptations, meaning the occasional urges, controlling the surroundings did well. But for strong habits, the watchful eye was the one that seemed to make the difference.

@char A13 living_room_day | Why watching helps | The pause before the move
Why might that be? The researchers did a laboratory experiment too, to look more closely. They found that monitoring did not make the habit itself any weaker. The pull was just as strong as before. What it seemed to do was strengthen the brake. Their own phrase was that vigilance heightens inhibitory, cognitive control. You could say it gives a person a small pause between the pull and the action.

@quote A18 cabin_fireplace | Only vigilant monitoring provided traction. | Quinn and colleagues, 2010
That is how the authors summed it up in their paper. Of all the strategies they evaluated, only vigilant monitoring gave people traction with strong habits. It's worth hearing this gently. It isn't a story about iron will. Nobody is being told to glare at themselves all day. It's simply a finding about what seemed to help most with the strongest habits, once the pull has already started.

@char A20 street_day | A fair question | What these studies can tell us
It's fair to ask how far we can lean on a study like this. The people in it were university students, and they were describing their own days in their own words, so it isn't the last word on anything. But it does fit together nicely with the laboratory part. Two different ways of looking, and the same quiet idea. Watching for the pull seems to help more than hoping the pull will simply go away, and that is a very usable idea.

@chapter A19 attic_rain | Ines practises noticing
Back in her flat, a few days later, Ines has still never heard of any of these studies. She has only her own evenings to learn from. So she simply tries something different, and nobody is there to tell her it's a good idea. Instead of fighting the thought of her phone, she starts to watch for the moment it begins. She doesn't scold herself. She just notices, quietly, the way you might notice a bus approaching. There it is. The urge. I can see it coming.

@char A10 rooftop_dusk | The moment before | Notice, and pause
Over the next few evenings, she finds that the urge has a very clear starting point. Her hand moves before the thought arrives. So she begins to watch her hand. When she sees it drifting towards the shelf, she smiles a little, takes a breath, and leaves it where it is. Some nights she picks the phone up anyway. That's fine. She has still seen it happening, and seeing it is the first thing that has been different in weeks. For Ines, a habit that is noticed feels a little less like it's running by itself. And that small difference gives her room to choose, which she hadn't had before, and which she now feels a quiet pleasure in.

@char A13 autumn_overlook | Noticing is not nagging | A softer kind of watching
It matters, though, that this kind of watching is warm and not harsh. Ines isn't hunting for mistakes. She is more like a friendly lighthouse keeper, watching the water and noticing what comes in. Some nights the boats come, and some nights they don't. Either way, the light stays on, and nobody has to be told off. That friendliness is part of why it feels possible to keep doing.

@char A19 living_room_day | Letting the evening settle | A gentle end to the day
And as the week goes on, Ines starts to feel a little lighter about all of this. Trying not to think about the phone had made it enormous. Simply noticing it, and letting it be there, makes it smaller. The evening settles. The kettle clicks off. Outside, a few early leaves turn over on the pavement. And for a little while, the phone is just a phone, resting on a shelf.
