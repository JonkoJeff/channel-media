@chapter A12 living_room_day | A sound that changes with a face
In the early 1970s, a British psychologist named Harry McGurk was studying how babies learn to talk, working at the University of Surrey with his research assistant, John MacDonald. They wanted to see how infants react when the sound they hear doesn't match the face that they see. To do this, they dubbed a new sound track onto a video of someone speaking.

@char A01 cafe_sunset | The accident | When the dub went wrong
When the two of them watched the dubbed film, something odd happened. The soundtrack said one syllable, and the lips on the screen said another. And yet what they heard was neither. It was a third syllable, one that wasn't in either the sound or the picture. They thought that the dub had gone wrong, and ran the test again.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | McGurk & MacDonald | Nature, 1976 | Hearing lips and seeing voices
Their paper, published in 1976 under the title Hearing Lips and Seeing Voices, described the effect. A sound of ba, played over a face mouthing ga, was heard by most adults as da. Played over a face mouthing ba, the same sound was heard as ba. The sound hadn't changed at all, but the face had changed what was heard.

@char A10 attic_rain | What it feels like | A very convincing illusion
Anyone who has experienced it will tell you how strong the effect is. With your eyes open, you hear one thing. Close your eyes, and the same recording changes into something else, instantly. It feels as though someone is playing tricks with the speaker. And, crucially, knowing about the trick doesn't make it go away.

@list A03 living_room_day | What the McGurk effect shows | Hearing and seeing are blended ;; The brain combines them automatically ;; Knowing the trick doesn't switch it off ;; We all lip-read a little, without noticing
That last point is worth dwelling on. We think of speech as something we hear. But the brain is using every source of evidence, and the movements of a speaker's lips are a rich one. In a noisy room, watching someone's face can help you understand them as much as turning up the volume.

@stat A14 conspiracy_board | The benefit of seeing a speaker | About 15 decibels | the equivalent improvement in noisy conditions, found in a classic 1954 study
In 1954, William Sumby and Irwin Pollack at Harvard found that when people could see a speaker's face, they were able to understand speech in noise as though the noise were much quieter. The advantage was roughly equal to turning down the background by fifteen decibels or more. Seeing the lips is a bit like a hearing aid, built into everyone.

@chapter A13 rooftop_dusk | How strong is it, really?
It's worth asking how universal the McGurk effect is. Researchers have found that it varies, quite a lot, from person to person. Some people experience it almost every time, and others rarely do. In 2015, a study by Basu Mallick, Magnotti and Beauchamp found that susceptibility varied widely between healthy adults.

@char A04 attic_rain | An unexpected finding | Not everyone gets it
Studies have also reported that the effect differs across languages and cultures, with some research suggesting that Japanese listeners show weaker effects than English listeners in some conditions. Later work has complicated that story, and it's best to say simply that the effect is robust, but that its strength depends on the person and the situation.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Basu Mallick, Magnotti & Beauchamp | Psychonomic Bulletin & Review, 2015 | Variability and stability in the McGurk effect
That study, which tested many people on a number of syllables, found that individuals differ enormously in how likely they are to experience the illusion, though each person's own rate is quite stable over time. It suggests that the way the brain weighs sound and sight is a bit like a personal fingerprint.

@char A12 living_room_day | The deeper idea | Optimal guessing
Scientists have a neat way to think about this. The brain has two sources of information, the sound and the sight, and each is a bit noisy. To get the best estimate, it combines them, giving more weight to the one that is more reliable. In a quiet room, the sound wins. In a loud one, the lips count for more.

@list A03 living_room_day | How the brain weighs the senses | Clear sound, fuzzy sight: trust the sound ;; Fuzzy sound, clear sight: trust the sight ;; Both fuzzy: guess from experience ;; Disagreement: make a compromise
The McGurk effect is what happens when the two senses disagree. The brain does its best, and the compromise is a syllable that nobody said. It's a small window onto a principle that applies everywhere: perception is a negotiation.

@chapter A06 cafe_sunset | Sounds that become words
Here is another demonstration, even more curious. In the early 1980s, Robert Remez at Barnard College and his colleagues made what's called sine-wave speech. They took a spoken sentence and reduced it to three or four whistling tones that follow the main peaks of the voice. Played cold, it sounds like a series of strange bird-like beeps.

@char A01 cafe_sunset | The strange recording | Chirps and whistles
Nobody hearing it for the first time recognises it as speech. It sounds like electronic chirping. But if you're then told the sentence, or you hear the original voice first, something remarkable happens. When you play the beeps again, you can suddenly hear the words, and you can't stop hearing them.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Remez, Rubin, Pisoni & Carrell | Science, 1981 | Speech perception without traditional speech cues
In their study, many listeners who were told that the sounds were speech could write down much of the sentence, even though the signal lacked the usual features of a voice. The finding shows how much the brain's expectations shape what it hears. The same sound can be noise or speech, depending on what you expect.

@char A04 attic_rain | The same trick again | Knowing changes hearing
This is another kind of top-down influence, where what you know changes how you perceive. It's the same logic as the dress and the filled-in blind spot. Perception is not one-way traffic from the world into the brain. Expectation travels the other way, and it shapes what arrives.

@char A10 attic_rain | A viral moment | Yanny or Laurel
In May 2018, a short audio clip went around the world. Some people heard a voice saying Yanny, and others heard Laurel, and each was sure. It started a fresh round of arguments and a bunch of studies. The clip was a low-quality recording of the word Laurel, and the answer depended on small things, such as the speakers, the volume and the listener.

@stat A06 cafe_sunset | The split | Roughly even | a survey by the New York Times found people divided between Yanny and Laurel
The explanation is that the recording contains energy at both low and high frequencies, and listeners differ in which they attend to. Turn the bass up, and most people hear Laurel. Turn the treble up, and most hear Yanny. It was the auditory version of the dress, and it taught the same lesson.

@list A12 living_room_day | The common thread | An ambiguous signal ;; Different expectations ;; Different, confident answers ;; A small change of the input can flip the result
When the evidence is clear, the brain's guess is the same for everyone. When it's ambiguous, we land in different places, and the surprise is how convincing our own version feels. Each of us sits inside a private result, and that's worth remembering when the next disagreement comes along.

@chapter A12 living_room_day | Hearing in the real world
Beyond these demonstrations, the same principles do real work in daily life. When you're at a party and can follow one voice among many, you're combining location, pitch, the rhythm of speech and the movements of a face. This ability is known as the cocktail party effect, a phrase introduced by the researcher Colin Cherry in 1953.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Cherry | Journal of the Acoustical Society of America, 1953 | Some experiments on the recognition of speech, with one and with two ears
Cherry's early experiments asked people to repeat one message while another was played into the other ear. They could do so easily, and afterwards remembered almost nothing about the unattended message, apart from the fact that it was speech and whether the voice was male or female. But people often noticed their own name in the ignored channel.

@char A04 attic_rain | The name effect | A signal that breaks through
Later studies found that a fraction of people notice their own name in the unattended ear, in one study by Neville Moray in 1959, roughly a third. It suggests that important words get a little processing even when we aren't attending, and that our attention has some way of being tapped on the shoulder.

@char A19 cabin_fireplace | A note on hearing loss | Seeing helps
It's worth saying that these findings have a kind side. For people who have some hearing loss, watching a speaker's face is a real help, and that's one reason why masks and video calls with poor connections can make it harder to follow a conversation. Looking at a person while they speak is a form of listening.

@photo lf18_b06_01.jpg | Headphones and a mirror
It's a plain picture, but it holds the principle. Sound and sight come into the same room, and the brain mixes them without asking. If you ever find yourself straining to understand someone in a noisy place, remember to look at them, and let both senses do the work.

@char A15 cabin_fireplace | A gentle thought | Listen with your eyes
There's a lovely side to this. When we truly want to understand someone, we turn towards them, and watch their face, and the understanding gets easier. Attention, in the end, is not only about the ears. It's about showing up with all of our senses.

@char A08 cabin_fireplace | Looking back | What the face taught the ear
So what did a dubbed film teach us? That hearing is a blend of senses. That the brain weighs evidence to find the best guess. That expectation can turn noise into speech. And that, when the evidence is ambiguous, we can each hear a different thing and feel completely sure.
