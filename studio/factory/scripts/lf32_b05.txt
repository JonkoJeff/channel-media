@chapter A03 living_room_day | Needs: what we are all reaching for
So far, we've looked at the machinery: drives, curiosity, the wanting system, the pull of a reward. But there is an older and much bigger question underneath all of it. What are we actually reaching for? If you could see every goal you have, from tonight's glass of water to the life you hope to build, would there be a shape to it?

@char A13 cabin_fireplace | A familiar picture | Or is it?
You have almost certainly seen one answer to that question. It's a pyramid. Food and sleep at the bottom, then safety, then love and belonging, then esteem, and right at the top, a small triangle called self-actualization. It appears in school textbooks, in office training slides, and on posters. And there's a quiet surprise hidden inside it.

@study A12 autumn_overlook | Abraham Maslow | Published in Psychological Review, 1943 | Proposed that human needs form a hierarchy, from basic physical needs up to the need to become what one is capable of being
The ideas come from an American psychologist named Abraham Maslow. In 1943 he published a paper called A Theory of Human Motivation. In it, he described a set of basic needs, the physical ones, then safety, then love and belonging, then esteem, and finally what he called self-actualization, the need to become what you are capable of becoming.

@char A15 cafe_sunset | His central idea | One need at a time
His central idea was a kind of ordering. When a more basic need is badly unmet, it tends to fill the mind. If you are starving, you don't spend much time wondering whether your work is meaningful. As the more basic needs are reasonably satisfied, the next ones come forward and start to shape what you want.

@char A19 rooftop_dusk | Not a staircase | Maslow's own caution
But Maslow was careful about this. He wrote that the order was not rigid, that most people have all their needs partly satisfied and partly unsatisfied at the same time, and that there are exceptions. He described tendencies, not a staircase you must climb one step at a time.

@study A05 living_room_day | William Bridgman, Stephen Cummings & John Ballard | Published in 2019 | Traced where the famous pyramid drawing came from
And here's the surprise. In 2019, three management researchers, William Bridgman, Stephen Cummings and John Ballard, went looking for the origin of the pyramid itself. They reported that Maslow did not draw it. The pyramid shape seems to have been added later, around 1960, by a management consultant, and then spread through textbooks.

@char A04 attic_rain | Why that matters | A shape changes a meaning
That might sound like a small detail, but a picture can quietly change an idea. A pyramid suggests rigid layers, each one sitting on top of the other, with only a few people ever reaching the narrow tip. Maslow's own writing was looser and more human than that. The image became more famous than the paper.

@photo lf32_b05_01.jpg | Balanced, not stacked in order
Picture a small stack of smooth stones on a windowsill. Each one rests on the others, and if you took one away, the whole thing would shift. That might be a gentler picture of our needs than a pyramid. They lean on each other.

@chapter A10 living_room_day | Does it hold up around the world?
So, if we set the pyramid aside, is there anything to the idea of universal needs? Do people in very different places reach for the same things?

@study A01 autumn_overlook | Louis Tay & Ed Diener | Published in 2011 | Studied needs and wellbeing in survey data from 123 countries
In 2011, the psychologists Louis Tay and Ed Diener looked at survey data from 123 countries, gathered by the Gallup World Poll. They asked whether people's needs were being met, things like food and shelter, safety, social support, respect, and the chance to learn and use their skills, and how those needs related to how people felt about their lives.

@char A02 cafe_sunset | What they found | Needs that travel
They found that these needs were linked to wellbeing almost everywhere they looked, across very different cultures. Basic needs were tied most strongly to how people rated their lives overall. Social needs and respect were tied more strongly to everyday positive feelings.

@char A12 rooftop_dusk | The twist | The order was loose
But the order was not as strict as the pyramid suggests. People could get real benefits from good relationships, or from learning something new, even when their more basic needs were not fully met. The needs worked more like ingredients than stairs. Each one added something of its own.

@chapter A18 cabin_fireplace | A longer list
Maslow wasn't the only one trying to list human needs. A few years earlier, someone had tried an even more detailed map.

@study A13 living_room_day | Henry Murray | Explorations in Personality, 1938 | Described a long list of psychological needs, such as achievement, affiliation and autonomy
In 1938, the American psychologist Henry Murray published a book called Explorations in Personality. In it, he described a long list of psychological needs, such as the need for achievement, the need for affiliation, the need for autonomy, and the need for order. People, he suggested, differ in how strongly each one pulls on them.

@char A19 attic_rain | Telling stories | A window into wanting
Murray and Christiana Morgan also developed a test in which people were shown ambiguous pictures and asked to tell a story about them. The idea was that the stories people told would reveal what they were reaching for, even things they might not say directly. It's still debated how well such tests work.

@study A16 street_day | David McClelland | Research from the 1950s and 1960s | Studied the need for achievement and how it shapes the challenges people choose
One of the people who carried this work forward was David McClelland. He spent years studying one need in particular, the need for achievement, the pull to do something well, to improve, to meet a standard of excellence. He believed it could be measured in the stories people told.

@char A11 cafe_sunset | Not too easy, not too hard | The sweet spot
One finding from this line of work is easy to picture. In ring-toss games, people who scored high on the need for achievement tended to stand at a middle distance. Not so close that success meant nothing, and not so far that it was pure luck. They seemed to prefer a challenge where their effort would make the difference.

@char A15 autumn_overlook | A glimpse of yourself | The challenge you choose
You might notice this in yourself. A crossword that's too easy feels flat. One that's impossible feels hopeless. The ones you can't put down are the ones just at the edge of what you can do. We'll come back to that edge later tonight, when we talk about flow.

@chapter A20 living_room_day | The need to belong
There's one need on almost every list, and some researchers think it may be one of the strongest of all.

@study A05 cafe_sunset | Roy Baumeister & Mark Leary | Published in Psychological Bulletin, 1995 | Argued that the need to belong is a fundamental human motivation
In 1995, the psychologists Roy Baumeister and Mark Leary published a long review called The Need to Belong. They argued that people have a basic, powerful drive to form and keep at least a small number of lasting, caring relationships. Not just company, but bonds with people who care about us over time.

@char A12 living_room_day | The evidence they gathered | Bonds form easily
They pointed out how easily people form bonds, even in strange conditions, and how reluctant we are to let them go. They noted that being cut off from others is linked with stress and poorer health, and that so much of our thinking and feeling revolves around relationships. Losing a bond, they argued, is one of the most painful things people experience.

@char A10 cabin_fireplace | A quiet thought | Why it's on every list
It's worth pausing on that. So much of what looks like motivation for something else, working hard, keeping fit, being good at a skill, may also be partly about belonging. Being seen. Being part of something. Being someone others can count on.

@char A13 cabin_fireplace | A glimpse of yourself | Your own list
If you made your own list tonight, what would be on it? Maybe rest, and a feeling of safety at home. Maybe a few people who'd notice if you went quiet. Maybe the chance to be good at something, and to be respected for it. Most people's lists look surprisingly alike, even if the order differs from day to day.

@list A03 living_room_day | What we've seen | A pyramid Maslow never drew ;; Needs that travel across cultures ;; A pull toward the right challenge ;; A deep need to belong
So, a pyramid that Maslow never drew. Needs that seem to show up across the world, but in a looser order than the picture suggests. A pull toward challenges at the edge of our ability. And underneath much of it, a deep need to belong.
