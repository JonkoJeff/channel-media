@chapter N09 cafe_sunset | A jingle that spread
Ruth is on the bus now, sitting by the window, and the chorus is still with her. She catches herself tapping a finger on her knee in time with it. It's a very modern experience, it seems: shop speakers, pop choruses, playlists. But the feeling she's having is much older than any of that, and one of the first people to describe it in print was a writer who was famous for something else entirely.

@study N20 living_room_day | Mark Twain | The Atlantic Monthly, February 1876 | A Literary Nightmare
In February 1876, the American author Mark Twain published a short comic story in The Atlantic Monthly called A Literary Nightmare. It was later reprinted under the title Punch, Brothers, Punch. The story describes a man whose mind is taken over, for days, by a silly little rhyme he read in the morning newspaper. It's one of the earliest vivid descriptions of an earworm in English writing.

@char N07 cafe_sunset | The rhyme | A fare sign set to rhythm
The rhyme in Twain's story was real. It was based on the wording of a fare notice for passengers on a tram, and it was turned into verse in 1876 by a small group of writers, Isaac Bromley, Noah Brooks, W. C. Wyckoff and Moses Handy. It first appeared in the New York Tribune, and people really did find it hard to forget, which is exactly what gave Twain his idea.

@char N10 living_room_day | The narrator's days | He can't think of anything else
In the story, the narrator reads the jingle once, and from then on its rhythm runs through his head without stopping. He can't work, he can't follow a conversation, and he can't enjoy his meals. Everything he does seems to happen in time with the jingle. It's played for laughs, of course, but anyone who has had a stubborn earworm will recognise the feeling underneath the joke.

@char N09 cabin_fireplace | The cure in the story | Pass it on
The most interesting part is how the narrator is finally cured. He goes for a long walk with a friend, a minister referred to as the Reverend, and while they walk, he recites the jingle to him. The narrator is suddenly free, and the Reverend now has the jingle instead. Later in the story, the Reverend frees himself in the same way, by passing it on to a group of university students.

An old newspaper folded on a table. Twain's idea, that a catchy fragment can travel from one mind to the next, was a joke in 1876. But it touches on something real. A tune or a phrase can spread through a group of people simply because each person who has it keeps humming it, saying it or singing it, and so passes it to the people nearby.

@char N20 living_room_day | A later echo | A meme, before the word
More than a century later, the biologist Richard Dawkins pointed to the Punch, Brothers, Punch jingle as an example of what he called a meme, an idea or habit that spreads by being copied from person to person. Whatever you think of that wider theory, the jingle is a neat illustration of how something small, simple and repetitive can travel a long way.

@char N07 cafe_sunset | Ruth on the bus | Humming under her breath
Ruth, meanwhile, realises she has been humming under her breath. The woman sitting next to her glances over and smiles. Ruth stops, a little embarrassed. Twain would have enjoyed that moment. In his story, the jingle spread because people said it aloud. In real life, a hummed chorus on a bus might do the very same thing to the next person.

@char N09 living_room_day | The word itself | Borrowed from German
The word earworm is borrowed from German, where it's Ohrwurm, literally ear worm. In English it caught on easily, perhaps because it captures the feeling so well: something small that has wriggled in and won't come out. Today it's used in newspapers, in everyday conversation and, alongside the more formal term, in scientific papers too.

@list N27 rooftop_dusk | Names for the same thing | Earworm ;; Involuntary musical imagery, or INMI ;; Stuck song syndrome ;; A sticky tune
Over the years, the experience has picked up several names. Earworm is the everyday one. Involuntary musical imagery is the careful scientific term. Some researchers have used the playful phrase stuck song syndrome, though it isn't a medical diagnosis. And in the title of one large study we'll meet later, they were simply called sticky tunes.

@char N10 cabin_fireplace | Why the science came late | Hard to catch in the lab
For a long time, earworms weren't studied scientifically at all. One reason is that they're difficult to catch. They happen in daily life, unpredictably, and they often vanish as soon as you try to examine them. It wasn't until the late 2000s that psychologists began to study them systematically, with large surveys, diaries and laboratory experiments.

@study N20 living_room_day | C. Philip Beaman and Tim Williams | British Journal of Psychology, published 2009 | Earworms: towards a natural history of intrusive thoughts
One of the first of these studies was published at the end of 2009 by C. Philip Beaman and Tim Williams at the University of Reading, in the British Journal of Psychology. Its title describes earworms as a kind of intrusive thought, and the authors set out to write what they called a natural history of the experience: when it happens, how long it lasts and how people feel about it.

@char N09 cafe_sunset | Who they asked | Travellers, students, garden visitors
Beaman and Williams surveyed just over a hundred people, including railway travellers, students and visitors to a garden, about their earworms. Then they asked twelve other people to keep a diary of every earworm they had for four weeks. Surveys tell you what people remember about their experiences. Diaries catch the experiences closer to the moment, and the two methods don't always agree.

@char N07 cafe_sunset | A thought for the ride | You're in good company
If you've ever felt slightly silly about a song that won't leave you alone, it's worth remembering that a famous author wrote a whole story about it in 1876, and that serious researchers have since spent years studying it. Ruth isn't being strange. She's having one of the most widely shared experiences in all of mental life, as the numbers in the next chapter will show.

@char N10 living_room_day | Looking back | Where the word came from
So, in this chapter, we've gone back to Mark Twain's story from 1876, with its jingle based on a tram fare sign, and its narrator who could only escape by passing the tune on. We've seen that the word earworm comes from the German Ohrwurm, and that scientists began to study the experience seriously only around the end of the 2000s, with work such as Beaman and Williams's natural history.
