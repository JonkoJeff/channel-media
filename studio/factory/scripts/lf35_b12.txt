@chapter A05 autumn_overlook | Myth ten: chase it harder
And so we come to the last myth of the night. It's the one that sits quietly behind almost every other idea about happiness. If happiness matters, then surely the sensible thing is to go after it. Make it a goal. Value it highly. Pursue it with all your energy. The harder you chase it, the more of it you'll catch.

@char A13 cabin_fireplace | Nora's new plan | A happier year
Nora, being thoughtful and a little determined, has a moment like this herself. One evening she decides that this year is going to be her happiest year ever. She has the flat she wanted. She has the view. There's really no excuse. She's going to be happy, properly happy, and she's going to keep checking that she is.

@char A10 attic_rain | A strange worry | Can chasing backfire
It sounds perfectly reasonable. But a few researchers began to wonder about something odd. What if valuing happiness very highly could sometimes make people less happy? What if constantly measuring your own mood, and finding it short of where you think it should be, became its own small source of disappointment?

@study A18 cafe_sunset | Iris Mauss, Maya Tamir, Craig Anderson and Nicole Savino | University of Denver and others | Can seeking happiness make people unhappy?
In 2011, Iris Mauss, Maya Tamir, Craig Anderson and Nicole Savino published a paper in the journal Emotion, with a title that asks exactly that question. Can seeking happiness make people unhappy? They approached it in two ways. In one study, they simply measured how much people valued happiness, and how they were doing in their lives. In another, they tried to change how much people valued happiness, and then watched what happened.

@list A12 living_room_day | What they found | Valuing happiness very highly went with lower well-being ;; People nudged to value happiness felt less happy after a happy film ;; The effect showed up when things were going well ;; Disappointment may be part of the reason
In the first study, people who valued happiness very highly tended to report lower well-being, especially when life wasn't particularly stressful. In the second, some people first read an article praising the importance of happiness. Then everyone watched a film clip. And after a happy film, the people who had been nudged to value happiness actually felt less happy than the others. The researchers suggested that when you expect to feel wonderful, ordinary good feelings can seem disappointing.

@char A03 rooftop_dusk | But wait | It isn't that simple
Now, that might seem to settle it. Don't chase happiness, and it will find you. But science rarely ends that neatly, and this is no exception. Because a couple of years later, another team found something that seemed to point in the opposite direction.

@study A18 living_room_day | Yuna Ferguson and Kennon Sheldon | University of Missouri | Trying to feel happier while listening to music
In 2013, Yuna Ferguson and Kennon Sheldon published two experiments in the Journal of Positive Psychology. In one, people listened to cheerful classical music by Aaron Copland. Some were simply asked to listen. Others were asked to actively try to feel happier while they listened. And the ones who tried did report a better mood afterwards.

@char A12 rooftop_dusk | Over two weeks | Small, regular sessions
In their second experiment, the researchers stretched the idea out over two weeks. People came back to listen to positive music several times, and again, some were asked to try to feel happier while they listened. By the end, those who had been trying reported being a little happier than those who had simply listened. The key detail is what they were doing. Not grasping at a feeling. Just turning, regularly and gently, towards something good.

@char A20 cafe_sunset | Both can be true | How you try matters
So which is it? Well, the picture is mixed, and it's honest to say so. But one way to reconcile them is this. Gently turning towards something that tends to lift you, like music you enjoy, may help. Treating happiness as a test you keep marking yourself on, and keep failing, may not. It's less about whether you try, and more about how tightly you hold on.

Nora, after a few weeks of her happiest year plan, notices something. The more she checks whether she's happy, the less happy she feels. Every quiet evening becomes a small exam. So she lets the plan go. And on the evenings when she simply puts on music she likes and sits by the window, with no target at all, she often feels better than on the nights she was trying so hard.

@chapter A15 rooftop_dusk | Can good things keep glowing?
And that brings us right back to the question I asked you to hold on to at the very beginning of tonight. Nora's sea faded so quickly. Six weeks, and she was walking past it with her phone. So is there a way to make good things keep glowing? Or is everything we love destined to slip into the background? It's a question with a surprisingly practical answer, and a hopeful one.

@study A18 cafe_sunset | Kennon Sheldon and Sonja Lyubomirsky | University of Missouri and University of California, Riverside | The hedonic adaptation prevention model
In 2012, Kennon Sheldon and Sonja Lyubomirsky published a study in the journal Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin that tackled exactly this. They tested what they called the hedonic adaptation prevention model. Students reported a recent positive change in their lives, and the researchers followed them over the weeks that followed, to see who held on to their happiness boost, and who drifted back.

@list A12 living_room_day | What keeps a good change glowing | It keeps bringing new good moments ;; Those moments keep varying ;; You keep appreciating it ;; Wanting more doesn't run too far ahead
And the answer was surprisingly hopeful. A positive change kept giving when it kept producing new good experiences, rather than just one. When those experiences kept varying, so they didn't all feel the same. When people kept appreciating the change, rather than taking it for granted. And when their wanting more didn't race too far ahead of what they already had.

@quote A20 autumn_overlook | Variety, surprise and appreciation slow the fade. | The idea behind the hedonic adaptation prevention model
Put simply, adaptation isn't a wall. It's more like a slope. Things drift down it when they stay exactly the same and stop being noticed. But variety, surprise and appreciation can slow the slide. Not stop it forever. Just slow it, and sometimes, gently, lift things back up again.

@list A19 cabin_fireplace | Tonight's gentle findings | Fading is how the brain works, not a flaw ;; We often mispredict what will last ;; Frequent small joys beat rare big ones ;; Pauses and variety refresh a good thing ;; Noticing, sharing and thanking slow the fade ;; Hold happiness lightly
So let's gather together what we've found tonight. Fading is how a healthy brain works, not a sign that something is wrong. We often mispredict what will make us happy, and for how long. Frequent small joys tend to matter more than rare big ones. Short pauses and a little variety can refresh a good thing. Noticing it, sharing it and feeling thankful for it can slow the fade. And happiness, it seems, is best held lightly, not gripped.

@char A13 living_room_day | Not therapy | Just curiosity
I want to say again that this has been about curiosity, not about fixing anyone. None of these ideas is a cure, and if your low moods ever feel heavy or go on for a long time, the right step is to talk to a doctor or someone you trust. Tonight we've simply been looking, with interest, at the strange and rather clever way our minds handle good things.
