@chapter A15 cafe_sunset | When choice gets heavy
Most of us believe, without ever really thinking about it, that more choice is better. More options mean more chance of finding exactly the right one. And in many ways, that's true. Nobody wants to go back to a world with one kind of bread and one colour of coat. But researchers have found that choice has a weight, and that sometimes, the weight starts to matter.

@study A01 living_room_day | Herbert Simon | Carnegie Institute of Technology, 1956 | Satisficing: searching until something is good enough, rather than the best
The story begins with Herbert Simon, an American scientist who worked across psychology, economics and computer science, and who won the Nobel Prize in economics in 1978. In a paper from 1956, he made a simple point. Real people don't have the time, the information, or the brainpower to find the very best option in every situation.

@char A13 cabin_fireplace | Good enough | A clever word
Instead, Simon argued, people satisfice. It's a word he built from two others, satisfy and suffice. We search until we find something that's good enough, and then we stop. Simon didn't see this as a failure. He saw it as a sensible way for a limited mind to live in a complicated world.

@chapter A19 cabin_fireplace | Two kinds of chooser
Almost fifty years later, the psychologist Barry Schwartz and his colleagues picked up Simon's idea, and asked whether people differ in how they choose. Some of us, they suggested, are natural satisficers. Others are maximisers, people who feel they must find the very best.

@study A03 rooftop_dusk | Barry Schwartz and colleagues | Swarthmore College, 2002 | Maximisers searched harder, and reported more regret and less happiness
In a set of studies published in 2002, Schwartz's team gave people a questionnaire to measure this tendency. Maximisers are the ones who keep flicking through channels long after something decent is on, or who try on every coat in the shop. And they found that maximisers, on average, reported more regret, less happiness, and less satisfaction with their choices.

@char A10 living_room_day | The cost of the best | Always looking back
It makes a kind of sense. If you're looking for the best, every choice leaves you wondering about the options you didn't take. If you're looking for good enough, you can settle into what you chose. The maximiser may sometimes end up with a slightly better result, and still feel worse about it.

@char A20 cafe_sunset | Mara the maximiser | One more column
Mara, if she's honest, has a little of the maximiser in her. She keeps adding to her columns, hoping that one more point will finally make the answer obvious. But for a choice like hers, there may never be a point where the answer becomes certain. At some point, good enough has to become enough.

@chapter A05 autumn_overlook | Too many jams
You may have heard of a famous study about jam. In 2000, Sheena Iyengar and Mark Lepper set up a tasting table in a food shop. Sometimes it offered six flavours, sometimes twenty four. The big display attracted more people, but far fewer of them ended up choosing a jar. Too much choice, it seemed, made people walk away.

@char A12 cabin_fireplace | The doubt | Hard to repeat
That study became the symbol of what's called choice overload. But in 2010, Benjamin Scheibehenne and his colleagues gathered dozens of experiments on choice overload and combined them. The average effect was close to zero. Sometimes more choice discouraged people, and sometimes it didn't, and sometimes it helped.

@study A18 living_room_day | Alexander Chernev, Ulf Böckenholt and Joseph Goodman | Journal of Consumer Psychology, 2015 | Choice overload appears under particular conditions
In 2015, Alexander Chernev and his colleagues looked again, and found a more useful answer. Choice overload does appear, but mostly under particular conditions. When the choice is hard, when the options are hard to compare, when people don't already know what they want, and when they want to decide quickly. In those conditions, many options really can weigh people down.

@char A15 rooftop_dusk | Mara's situation | Hard to compare
Mara's choice fits that description rather well. Her options aren't like jams, where you can taste one and then another. Life by the sea and life in the city are made of completely different things, sisters and cafés, sea air and friendships. They're hard to compare. And that, as much as the size of the choice, may be what makes it feel so heavy.

Picture a long shelf filled with little jars of jam, every colour you can imagine. It looks generous, and inviting. But standing in front of it, trying to choose just one, can feel surprisingly tiring.

@chapter A16 autumn_overlook | Tired of deciding
Tiring is the right word. Because there's another famous idea about decisions, which says that making choices wears us out. That after a long day of deciding, we start choosing badly, or stop choosing at all. It's called decision fatigue. And its most famous evidence comes from a courtroom.

@study A01 cafe_sunset | Shai Danziger, Jonathan Levav and Liora Avnaim-Pesso | Israel, 2011 | Parole decisions seemed to depend on how long since the judges' last break
In 2011, Shai Danziger, Jonathan Levav and Liora Avnaim-Pesso studied over a thousand parole decisions made by eight experienced judges in Israel. The judges' days were divided into sessions, separated by a snack break and a lunch break. And the pattern the researchers found was startling.

@curve A03 rooftop_dusk | Favourable rulings across a session | Time since the break | Share of favourable rulings | 0.1 | Highest just after a break
At the start of each session, about sixty five percent of the rulings were favourable to the prisoner. As the session went on, that share fell, until it reached nearly zero just before the break. After the break, it jumped back up to around sixty five percent again. As if a meal had reset the judges' willingness to take a chance.

@char A13 attic_rain | A famous story | Hungry judges
It became one of the most quoted findings in all of psychology, often summed up as hungry judges are harsher. It was in books, talks and articles all over the world. And then, as often happens, other researchers looked more closely.

@char A10 cabin_fireplace | The order of cases | Not random
In the same year, Keren Weinshall-Margel and John Shapard pointed out that the order of cases wasn't random. Prisoners without lawyers tended to be heard at the end of a session, and those cases were less likely to succeed for other reasons. In 2016, Andreas Glöckner used computer simulations to show that ordinary scheduling could produce a similar pattern on its own.

@char A19 living_room_day | What's left | A smaller idea
So the hungry judges story may be much less dramatic than it first seemed. And the broader idea of willpower as a fuel tank that runs dry has also struggled in large replications. What remains is a quieter and more ordinary truth. When we're tired, rushed, or hungry, we tend to fall back on defaults and easy options.

@char A20 cafe_sunset | Mara at midnight | Not the time to decide
That's worth remembering for Mara, sitting at her kitchen table late in the evening, after a long day. This might not be the moment to make her final choice. Not because her brain has run out of fuel, but because tiredness tends to favour the easy path, and tonight, the easy path is simply to stay.

@list A12 autumn_overlook | When choices weigh | Good enough can be enough ;; Hard comparisons feel heavy ;; Big choices deserve a rested mind
So, a few gentle lessons. Good enough can be enough, especially when there's no clear best. Choices feel heavy when the options are hard to compare, not only when there are many of them. And big choices deserve a rested mind, in the morning light rather than at midnight.
