@chapter N14 bedroom_night | What makes something funny
Nadia turns into a long, quiet street lined with plane trees, their leaves pasted flat on the wet pavement. She tries to work out what was actually funny about the teapot lid. It wasn't clever. Nobody was hurt. It just landed, with a tiny plop, exactly where it shouldn't have been. Maybe that was it. Maybe it was just so wrong, and so harmless.

@char A10 cabin_fireplace | An old question | Thinkers have tried for centuries
People have been trying to explain what makes things funny for a very long time. Philosophers in ancient Greece wrote about it. So did thinkers in the Enlightenment, and psychologists in the twentieth century. Over the years, three big families of ideas have emerged. None of them explains everything, but each catches something true.

@list N01 autumn_overlook | Three classic families of ideas | Superiority: laughing at others' misfortune ;; Relief: letting off built-up tension ;; Incongruity: something unexpected or out of place
They're usually called superiority, relief and incongruity. Superiority theories say we laugh when we feel above someone else, at their mistakes or misfortunes. Relief theories say laughter lets off built-up nervous energy. And incongruity theories say we laugh when something is unexpected, out of place, or doesn't fit the pattern we expected.

@char N09 cabin_fireplace | Superiority | Laughing at
The superiority idea is very old. Plato and Aristotle both wrote about laughter at other people's faults, and the English philosopher Thomas Hobbes, in the seventeenth century, described laughter as a sudden glory at our own advantage over others. There's some truth in it. Mocking laughter exists, and most of us have felt it, or been on the receiving end.

@char N20 attic_rain | But it doesn't fit everything | Nobody felt superior to a teapot
But superiority can't explain everything. Nobody at Nadia's table felt superior to the teapot. Babies laughing at peekaboo aren't gloating. Friends laughing together over a shared memory aren't putting anyone down. Superiority captures one kind of laughter, but it misses the warm, inclusive kind, which, as we've seen, is probably the most common of all.

@char A04 neon_alley_rain | Relief | Letting off steam
Relief theories were popular with nineteenth-century thinkers like the philosopher Herbert Spencer, and later with Sigmund Freud. They saw laughter as a way of releasing pent-up nervous energy, like steam escaping from a valve. That catches something real too. Think of the giggles after a near-miss, or the laughter that bursts out when a tense meeting finally ends.

@char A03 living_room_day | But the steam idea is shaky | The body doesn't store tension like that
But the specific idea, that nervous energy builds up inside us like steam in a pipe, doesn't really match what we now know about the nervous system. Relief laughter is real, but the steam-valve picture is more of a metaphor than a mechanism. Today's researchers tend to describe it in terms of shifts in mood and attention instead.

@char N29 rooftop_dusk | Incongruity | Something doesn't fit
Incongruity is the idea that has had the longest life in modern research. Thinkers from the philosopher James Beattie in the eighteenth century to modern psychologists have argued that humour depends on noticing a mismatch, something that doesn't fit what we expected, and then, often, making sense of it in a new way. A punchline, in this view, is a sudden, surprising shift.

Just one thing in a place it should never have been, at a moment when everyone was relaxed enough to notice how ridiculous it looked.

@char A19 living_room_day | But not every surprise is funny | A missing ingredient
Incongruity seems to catch a lot. But it has a problem too. Plenty of unexpected things aren't funny at all. A sudden loud bang. A stranger in your kitchen. Bad news out of the blue. These are incongruous, but they're frightening or upsetting, not amusing. So there must be another ingredient, something that turns an odd surprise into a funny one.

@study A13 cafe_sunset | Peter McGraw and Caleb Warren | Psychological Science, 2010 | Benign violations: making immoral behavior funny
In 2010, two researchers, Peter McGraw and Caleb Warren, proposed a simple answer, which they called the benign violation theory. Something becomes funny, they suggested, when it seems wrong or threatening in some way, a violation, while at the same time seeming okay, safe, or harmless, benign. Both have to be true at once.

@char A15 living_room_day | Wrong, but okay | The sweet spot
In their studies, people read short scenarios describing small rule-breaking, and rated how funny and how wrong they seemed. Mild violations, ones that were wrong but didn't really hurt anybody, were the ones people most often found amusing. Too harmless, and there was nothing to laugh at. Too harmful, and it stopped being funny and became upsetting.

@list N20 cabin_fireplace | The benign violation idea | Something seems wrong ;; But it also seems safe ;; Both at the same time ;; Too harmless or too harmful kills the laugh
Put simply: something seems wrong. But it also seems safe. Both at the same time. And if it's too harmless, or too harmful, the laugh disappears. It's a neat idea, and it explains a fair amount, including why tickling works best from a loving parent, and why a joke that's fine among friends can fall flat with strangers.

@char N14 neon_alley_rain | Still debated | No theory explains it all
It's worth saying clearly that no single theory of humour has won. Benign violation has its critics, who argue that it's so broad it can be stretched to fit almost anything. Other researchers focus on different ingredients, like playfulness, or the pleasure of solving a small puzzle. The honest answer is that humour is probably made of several things at once.

@char N07 living_room_day | The teapot, explained | As far as we can
Still, the teapot lid fits rather well. A lid in a sugar bowl is wrong. It's out of place, and slightly absurd. But it's also completely harmless. Nobody's hurt, nothing's broken, and the tea will be fine. Add five relaxed friends, a long history of shared jokes and a late-evening mood, and you have, it seems, the perfect conditions.

@char A10 attic_rain | Back to the people | Humour needs company too
And notice that the theories still come back to people. Whether something feels safe depends a great deal on who you're with. The same small accident might feel embarrassing in front of your boss, and hilarious in front of old friends. Humour isn't just in the joke. It's in the relationship between everyone who hears it.

@char N01 bedroom_night | Next question | Not all laughs are the same
That brings Nadia to another thought. Not every laugh at that dinner was the same. There were the helpless laughs at the teapot. But there were also the polite ones, earlier, when someone told a long story about their job. Can people tell the difference between a real laugh and a polite one? It turns out that they can, and surprisingly well.
