@chapter N10 living_room_day | What it costs
Anton has been thinking about a lecture he sat through a few years ago, about an hour about the geology of river valleys. It was good, he remembers, and he remembers almost nothing of it.

@char N02 cafe_sunset | A classroom memory | An hour that vanished
Most of us have a version of that lecture. A talk we meant to follow, and drifted away from after about ten minutes. We nodded at the right places, we copied down a few words, and afterward we realized that most of the hour had slipped by. It leads naturally to the first big question about mind wandering, which is whether it harms learning.

@char N19 autumn_overlook | A reasonable worry | Does drifting hurt memory?
There are good reasons to think it does. If attention has turned inward, the information coming in from the outside is processed less deeply, as we saw earlier. And information that isn't processed deeply tends not to be remembered. So you might expect that people who wander more during a lecture or a reading would remember less.

@study N07 cafe_sunset | Evan Risko and colleagues | Applied Cognitive Psychology, 2012 | Students watched a lecture and reported mind wandering during it, then answered questions about the material
In 2012, Evan Risko, Nicola Anderson, Amara Sarwal, Megan Engelhardt and Alan Kingstone published a study in Applied Cognitive Psychology, in which students watched a video lecture and were asked, at intervals, whether their minds were wandering. Afterward, they took a test on the content. The students who reported more mind wandering tended to do worse on the test.

@char N23 rooftop_dusk | Not a dramatic effect | A steady one
That might seem obvious, but the details are interesting. In several studies, the effect wasn't dramatic. It was steady, though, and it appeared across different kinds of material. And it fits a larger picture, which comes from a review of dozens of studies. In 2014, Amanda Randall and colleagues published a meta-analysis in Psychological Bulletin, finding a consistent negative relationship between mind wandering and performance on a range of tasks.

@char N10 bedroom_night | A word about that review | A pattern across studies
A meta-analysis is a kind of study of studies, which combines the results from many separate experiments. It can't tell us about any one person, and it can't prove cause and effect. But when a pattern shows up across many different experiments, it becomes a lot harder to dismiss as a fluke.

@char N02 living_room_day | The next question | Can it be reduced?
And that raises the practical question. If drifting during a lecture harms learning, is there anything that helps? One of the most interesting answers came from Karl Szpunar and his colleagues at Harvard, working with Daniel Schacter.

@study N07 autumn_overlook | Karl Szpunar, Novall Khan and Daniel Schacter | PNAS, 2013 | Students watched a recorded statistics lecture, with or without short quizzes between segments, and reported mind wandering
In 2013, Szpunar, Khan and Schacter published a paper in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences. Students watched a recorded lecture on statistics, broken into several short segments. Some students were tested briefly after each segment. Others weren't tested until the end. And all of them reported how often their minds wandered.

@char N19 cabin_fireplace | The result | Short tests helped
The students who were tested between segments reported far less mind wandering, roughly half as much as those who weren't. They also took more notes, and did better on a final test of the material. The act of being asked a question, it seems, pulled their attention back and kept it there.

@list N11 cafe_sunset | Ideas that follow from this research | Break long material into shorter chunks ;; Test yourself between sections ;; Take brief notes as you go ;; Change what you're doing before attention fades ;; Notice the drift, and gently return
It's a helpful finding because it points to simple habits. Short chunks, brief self-tests, a few notes in your own words, a change of pace. They aren't magic, but they do give the mind something to hold on to, which is exactly what a wandering mind lacks.

@char N10 living_room_day | A broader view | More than just lectures
The costs show up elsewhere too. Reading comprehension, as we saw, suffers when we zone out. So do tasks that need steady attention, where a lapse of a second or two can matter. And everyday small mistakes, like putting the keys in the fridge or sending an email to the wrong person, are often blamed on a wandering mind, though research on those is much harder to do.

@quote N27 rooftop_dusk | The cost of wandering is real, but it depends on what the task needs. | a summary of the research
That's a fair summary of the evidence. The cost is real, but it depends on what the task demands. A lapse while reading a manual is one thing. A lapse while washing the dishes costs nothing at all.

@char N02 autumn_overlook | A review | The balance of costs
Mooneyham and Schooler reviewed this literature in 2013, in the Canadian Journal of Experimental Psychology, under the title The costs and benefits of mind-wandering. Their conclusion was balanced. There are real costs in tasks that need sustained, external attention. But there are also benefits, which we'll come to next, and the right question isn't whether wandering is good or bad, but when.

@char N20 bedroom_night | What Anton takes from this | A small adjustment
Anton thinks about his geology lecture, and wonders what he might have done differently. Perhaps he could have asked himself a question every ten minutes. Perhaps he could have written one line in his own words. He decides he'll try that the next time he finds himself in a long talk, and he writes a small note on the back of his hand, which makes him laugh.

@char N10 cabin_fireplace | A gentle point | Be kind to the lapse
There's also a gentle point here. The moment you catch yourself drifting is not a moment for self-criticism. It's the moment attention returns, and each time it does, you're practicing the very skill you'd like to have. The drift is part of the process, and the return is the skill.
