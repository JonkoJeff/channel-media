CHAPTER: Paul's List
In the mid-fifties, probably from Ephesus, Paul wrote to the young church in Corinth, and in the fifteenth chapter of that letter he reminds them of the message he had preached. It is the oldest written account of the resurrection that we have. || 1 Corinthians 15, Papyrus 46 || wikimedia
Paul says that he is passing on what he himself received: that Christ died for our sins according to the scriptures, that he was buried, that he was raised on the third day according to the scriptures, and that he appeared to Cephas, then to the twelve. || Saint Paul, El Greco || wikimedia
Cephas is Peter. Paul continues that Jesus then appeared to more than five hundred brothers and sisters at one time, most of whom were still alive when he was writing, though some had died. Then to James, then to all the apostles, and last of all to Paul himself. || 1 Corinthians 15:3-8 || text-card
SHORT: Christianity's Oldest Creed Predates the Gospels
Most scholars, whatever their own beliefs, think that the opening lines of this passage are a creed or a formula that Paul received from others. The language is compact, almost rhythmic, with words like received and passed on that belong to the handing down of tradition. || Saint Paul Writing His Epistles, Valentin de Boulogne || wikimedia
When did Paul receive it? He says that he visited Peter and James in Jerusalem about three years after his conversion, and many scholars think that this is where he learned the formula. If so, it goes back to within a few years of the crucifixion. Even skeptical scholars agree that it is very early. || Galatians 1:18-19 || text-card
The list also has some features that matter. It names living people who could be asked. It does not mention the women at the tomb, and it does not mention the empty tomb itself. It is a list of appearances. That fact has led to a long discussion about how the two traditions fit together. || Apostle Paul, Rembrandt || wikimedia
Paul's own experience is the last item on the list. In Acts, the encounter on the road to Damascus is told three times, as a blinding light and a voice. In his own letters he is brief: he says that God was pleased to reveal his Son to him, and elsewhere asks whether he has not seen Jesus our Lord. || Conversion of Saint Paul, Caravaggio || wikimedia
Paul is also clear about how much depends on it. If Christ has not been raised, he writes, then our preaching is in vain, and your faith is in vain. He does not treat the resurrection as an optional extra. For him, it is the ground on which everything else stands. || 1 Corinthians 15:14 || text-card
He also says something important about the nature of the risen body. In the same chapter he describes it as a spiritual body, sown in weakness and raised in power. Scholars still debate what Paul meant by that phrase, and how close it is to the physical bodily language of Luke and John. || Resurrection, Michelangelo, Last Judgment, Sistine Chapel || wikimedia
CHAPTER: James, Peter and the Five Hundred
Paul's list contains several names, and each opens a small window. Peter comes first. In Luke, the risen Jesus appears to Simon, and in John, Peter receives his charge at the lake. The man who had denied Jesus three times becomes the leader of the first community in Jerusalem. || Christ's Charge to Peter, Raphael cartoon, Victoria and Albert Museum || wikimedia
The five hundred are mentioned only here, and no Gospel describes the event. Some scholars have wondered whether it is a memory of Pentecost, or of a large gathering in Galilee. Paul's remark that most of them were still alive reads like an invitation to check, though we do not know whether anyone did. || 1 Corinthians 15:6 || text-card
James is especially interesting. The Gospels say that during the ministry, Jesus' brothers did not believe in him. John states it plainly. Yet by the time of Paul's letters, James is one of the pillars of the church in Jerusalem, and Josephus says that he was stoned to death in the year sixty-two. || Josephus, Jewish Antiquities 20.200 || text-card
Something changed the mind of a skeptical family member. Paul says that Jesus appeared to James, and an early Christian writing called the Gospel of the Hebrews, which survives only in quotations, describes a meeting between the risen Jesus and James, though scholars disagree on how early that tradition is. || James the Just, Jerusalem, Armenian Cathedral || wikimedia
Then Paul adds all the apostles, a wider group than the twelve. The word apostle means one who is sent. In the first generation, it seems to have included people like Barnabas, Andronicus and Junia, who are named in Paul's letters, and who may have counted as witnesses of the risen Lord. || Romans 16:7 || text-card
Paul does not suggest that the appearances went on forever. He says that last of all, as to one untimely born, Jesus appeared also to him. For Paul, the witnesses form a closed circle, and he joins it late and with humility, calling himself the least of the apostles. || Saint Paul, mosaic, Ravenna || wikimedia
Taken together, the list shows how the movement understood itself. Its authority did not rest on a book, since there was no New Testament yet. It rested on people who said that they had seen the risen Lord, and who were sent out to say so. That is why the earliest preaching is so short and so direct. || Apostles, Mosaic, Basilica of San Vitale || wikimedia
CHAPTER: Other Early Writings
The four Gospels are not the only early Christian texts to speak of the resurrection. The Gospel of Peter, a work from the second century, survives in a fragment discovered in Egypt in the nineteenth century. Unlike the four Gospels, it describes the moment of rising itself. || Gospel of Peter, Akhmim fragment || wikimedia
In that text, soldiers guard the tomb, and a great voice sounds from heaven. Two men come down in a blaze of light, and the stone rolls away by itself. Three figures walk out, two supporting one, and a cross follows them, and a voice from heaven asks whether the preaching has been made to those who sleep. || Gospel of Peter 9-10 || text-card
Scholars treat the work as later than the canonical accounts, and as an example of how storytellers began to fill in the silence of the earlier Gospels. The restraint of Mark, Matthew, Luke and John, which do not attempt a scene of the resurrection, is one reason that many readers find them more convincing. || Fragment of the Gospel of Peter, Cairo Museum || wikimedia
Other writers asked what the resurrection of the body would mean. In the second century, a group often called Gnostic Christians taught that salvation was the release of the soul from matter. One text discovered at Nag Hammadi, the Treatise on the Resurrection, says that the resurrection is a spiritual event rather than a rising of the flesh. || Nag Hammadi codices, Coptic Museum Cairo || wikimedia
Many bishops disagreed. Irenaeus of Lyon, writing around the year one hundred and eighty, argued that the God who made the body also redeems it, and that the creed must affirm the resurrection of the flesh. The Apostles' Creed, which developed in this period, says that Christians believe in the resurrection of the body. || Irenaeus, Against Heresies, Lyon || wikimedia
These debates show that from the start, there was more than one way to speak about what had happened. They also show that the question of what kind of body the risen Jesus had was a real puzzle even to those closest to the events, and that it has remained a lively one in every century since. || Papyrus, early Christian writings, Oxyrhynchus || wikimedia
CHAPTER: Was the Tomb Really Empty?
Historians who consider the question usually ask what best explains the earliest Christian preaching in Jerusalem. According to Acts, the apostles proclaimed the resurrection in the very city where Jesus had been buried, within weeks. If the body were still in a known tomb, that preaching would have been easy to refute. || Holy Sepulchre, aedicule, Jerusalem || wikimedia
That is one of the arguments that many scholars find persuasive for the empty tomb. It is not a proof. It is an inference about what people would have said and done. Others reply that we do not know whether anyone would have bothered to check, or whether the body of an executed man could even be identified. || Tomb of Jesus, Edicule restoration, 2016 || wikimedia
A second argument concerns Joseph of Arimathea. Crucified criminals were often left on the cross or buried in a shared grave. The Gospels, however, name a specific member of the council who gave Jesus an honorable burial. Some historians think that the detail is too specific to be invented, since it casts a Jewish leader in a kind role. || Joseph of Arimathea, Entombment, Rogier van der Weyden || wikimedia
A third argument concerns the women. In the ancient world, the testimony of women was often given less weight in legal settings, and some ancient authors, such as Celsus in the second century, mocked the Christian story because it relied on women. If someone were inventing the story, critics reason, they would not have chosen those witnesses. || Celsus, Origen, Against Celsus 2.55 || text-card
Not all scholars accept these arguments. Some, such as the New Testament scholar Bart Ehrman, have argued that we cannot know what happened to the body, and that Jesus may have been buried in a common grave, as crucifixion victims often were. The Gospel story of Joseph, on that view, could be a later development. || Bart Ehrman, How Jesus Became God, 2014 || text-card
Others, such as the late Geza Vermes, himself a Jewish scholar of the period, concluded that historians can describe what the earliest followers believed, but that the sources leave the explanation open, and that the question is finally one of faith or doubt rather than of method. || Geza Vermes, The Resurrection, Penguin, 2008 || text-card
The archaeological record gives little direct help. We have only one skeleton of a crucified man from the period, found in Jerusalem in 1968, a man named Yehohanan, with a nail still through his heel bone. It shows that crucified men could, at times, receive a family burial in a rock-cut tomb. || Yehohanan ossuary, Israel Museum || wikimedia
As for the place itself, Christians in Jerusalem seem to have kept memory of the tomb. In the fourth century, the church historian Eusebius reports, Constantine ordered a Roman temple removed from the site, and a tomb was identified beneath it. Excavations suggest that the area was a quarry with first-century tombs. || Church of the Holy Sepulchre, Rotunda || wikimedia
CHAPTER: What Resurrection Meant to a First-Century Jew
It is easy to forget how strange the claim would have sounded. The ancient Greeks and Romans generally thought that death was final, or that the soul left the body for good. In Homer's Odyssey, the ghost of Achilles says he would rather be the lowliest servant on earth than king among the dead, and none of the dead come back. || Odysseus in the Underworld, Greek vase || wikimedia
In Jewish thought, however, hope for resurrection had been developing for a few centuries. The book of Daniel says that many of those who sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake, some to everlasting life. Ezekiel's vision of the dry bones had become a picture of the people's restoration. || Vision of the Dry Bones, Ezekiel, Gustave Doré || wikimedia
By the first century, the Pharisees taught that the dead would be raised at the end of time, while the Sadducees denied it. The Gospels show Jesus in conversation with both. But in all these discussions, resurrection was a general event: God would raise the righteous together, at the end of history. || Pharisees and Sadducees, Jewish sects, first century || wikimedia
What the first Christians claimed was different. They said that one man had been raised ahead of everyone else, in the middle of history, and that it had already begun. The New Testament scholar N. T. Wright has argued that this was a surprising modification of Jewish hope, and that it requires explanation. || N. T. Wright, The Resurrection of the Son of God, 2003 || text-card
Wright's argument is that nothing in the Jewish world would have led people simply to invent such a claim, and that something must have happened. Other scholars answer that religious movements often reshape inherited ideas under pressure, especially after a shattering loss, and that the claim can be explained in more than one way. || Resurrection, Christ rising, Matthias Grünewald, Isenheim Altarpiece || wikimedia
The disciples, in any case, did not understand Jesus as a ghost or as a mere memory. Their language is that of bodies and of rising: egeiro, to wake or to raise, and anastasis, standing up again. They were saying that the crucified man had been given a new life that death could no longer touch. || Anastasis, Chora Church, Istanbul || wikimedia
