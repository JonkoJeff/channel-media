CHAPTER: One of You Will Hand Me Over
All four Gospels agree on something unsettling. During the meal, Jesus says that one of those eating with him will hand him over. In Mark's telling, the words land on the table like a stone: one of the Twelve, one who is dipping bread with him. || Mark 14:17–21 || text-card
The reaction is the human part of the scene. Matthew and Mark describe the disciples becoming distressed, and each begins to ask, one after another, whether it is him. Nobody points confidently at anyone else. || Matthew 26:20–22 || text-card
In Matthew, Jesus answers that the one who dipped his hand in the dish with him will hand him over, and adds that it would have been better for that person never to have been born. Judas then asks if it is he, and Jesus replies, as Matthew has it, that Judas has said so. || Matthew 26:23–25 || text-card
Luke has the announcement come after the bread and cup, and he says the disciples began to discuss which of them it could be. His order differs from Matthew and Mark, a reminder that each author arranged the evening in his own way. || Luke 22:21–23 || text-card
John adds the most intimate detail. The beloved disciple, reclining close to Jesus, asks who it is. Jesus answers that it is the one to whom he will give a piece of bread after dipping it. He gives it to Judas, and John says that Judas went out, and it was night. || John 13:21–30 || text-card
That last phrase, it was night, is one of the most quoted lines in John. It marks a physical departure into darkness, and also a symbolic one, since John often uses light and dark as signs of understanding and refusal. || Gospel of John, Codex Vaticanus || wikimedia
The name Judas Iscariot appears in all lists of the Twelve. The word Iscariot has been explained in several ways: a man from a place called Kerioth, or perhaps related to a word for dagger-man or for handing over. Scholars have not reached agreement. || Judas Iscariot, Giotto, Scrovegni Chapel || wikimedia
Why did Judas do it? The Gospels give different answers. Mark says only that he went to the chief priests and they promised him money. Matthew names thirty pieces of silver. Luke and John say that Satan entered him. || Matthew 26:14–16 || text-card
Notice how varied the explanations are. The authors share a conviction that betrayal happened, but they do not agree on the motive. Historians have therefore suggested many possibilities, and honest scholarship admits that the real motive is hard to recover. || Judas Returning the Thirty Pieces of Silver, Rembrandt || wikimedia
The word often translated betray is the Greek word paradidomi, which more literally means to hand over, or to deliver. It is the same verb used in other contexts for passing on a tradition. Even the language in this scene is layered. || Greek New Testament papyrus, Papyrus 75 || wikimedia
Historically, most scholars regard the fact that one of Jesus' own circle handed him over as very likely, precisely because it would have been awkward for the early Christians to invent. A leader betrayed by one of his chosen twelve was not a flattering story. || John P. Meier, A Marginal Jew, volume three || text-card
It is also important to guard against a danger that has haunted this scene for centuries. Judas was one disciple among twelve, all of them Jews. Using his story to blame Jewish people as a whole has no basis in the texts, and has caused terrible harm. || Judas Kiss, Giotto, detail || wikimedia
The Gospels themselves show how the table includes the faithful and the failing together. Peter, as we will see, will deny Jesus. Others will flee. Only one is named as the one who hands him over, yet none of them stand firm in the next hours. || Last Supper, Domenico Ghirlandaio, Ognissanti || wikimedia
In the narrative, the announcement does not interrupt the meal so much as deepen it. The story has told us from the start that this evening is moving toward crisis. Now the circle of friends has a crack running through it. || Last Supper, Andrea del Castagno || wikimedia
And yet the supper continues. The next thing the Synoptic Gospels record is not an accusation or a confrontation but a simple act: Jesus takes a loaf of bread. || Loaf of bread, ancient Roman carbonised bread, Herculaneum || wikimedia
CHAPTER: Bread and Cup in Four Accounts
Now we reach the heart of the meal. We have four versions of what happened with the bread and the cup: Matthew, Mark, Luke, and Paul. They are close, but they are not identical, and the differences are worth looking at slowly. || Matthew 26:26–29 || text-card
In Mark, the oldest of the three Gospels, Jesus takes bread, blesses it, breaks it, and gives it to them, saying take, this is my body. Then he takes a cup, gives thanks, and they all drink from it. || Mark 14:22–25 || text-card
Then he says of the cup that this is my blood of the covenant, poured out for many. And he adds that he will not drink again of the fruit of the vine until the day he drinks it new in the kingdom of God. || Mark 14:24–25 || text-card
Matthew follows Mark very closely. He adds two small details: that the disciples are told to eat as well as take, and that the blood is poured out for many for the forgiveness of sins. These additions give the cup a clearer purpose. || Matthew 26:26–28 || text-card
Luke gives a different pattern. In his longer version, there is a cup first, then bread, then a second cup after supper. Over the bread he adds the words given for you, and says do this in remembrance of me. || Luke 22:14–20 || text-card
Paul's version, in First Corinthians, is closest to Luke. He too has the instruction to do this in remembrance, and he says that the cup is the new covenant in Jesus' blood. He adds that whenever you eat and drink, you proclaim the Lord's death until he comes. || 1 Corinthians 11:23–26 || text-card
There is a notable wrinkle in Luke's text. In some important ancient manuscripts, part of the longer passage about the second cup is missing, leaving a shorter form. Scholars still debate which version is original. || Codex Bezae Cantabrigiensis, Luke || wikimedia
This is a good example of how textual criticism works. Copyists made thousands of manuscripts by hand, and in certain places they left slightly different words. Scholars compare them to reconstruct the earliest reading, but sometimes the evidence is genuinely divided. || Codex Sinaiticus, Luke || wikimedia
Bruce Metzger, in his Textual Commentary on the Greek New Testament, discusses this problem at length. He explains why the shorter text is regarded by some as original and why others favour the longer. It is a reminder that even this famous passage has a manuscript history. || Bruce Metzger, A Textual Commentary on the Greek New Testament || text-card
The wording of the central sayings varies in small ways. In Mark and Matthew, the cup is blood of the covenant. In Luke and Paul it is the new covenant in his blood. Both traditions stress covenant, but they phrase it differently. || Exodus 24:8 and Jeremiah 31:31 || text-card
Joachim Jeremias, a German scholar who wrote a classic study of these words, argued that behind the Greek lies an Aramaic original, spoken by Jesus. Other scholars are less sure that we can reconstruct such words with confidence. || Joachim Jeremias, The Eucharistic Words of Jesus || text-card
What we can say is that several independent lines, Paul and the Synoptic tradition, preserve a meal where bread and a cup were given a meaning connected with Jesus' coming death. That is among the best-attested features of the whole supper. || Last Supper, Jacopo Bassano || wikimedia
John, as we noticed, has no such scene. But he does contain a long discourse in chapter six about eating the flesh and drinking the blood of the Son of Man, set in Galilee earlier in his Gospel. Some readers see a link, others see a separate teaching. || John 6:48–58 || text-card
In all these accounts, the physical objects are ordinary: a loaf, a cup of wine. The words transform them from the meal's background into the meal's meaning. That is how the practice would survive long after the table in Jerusalem was gone. || Chalice and paten, Antioch Chalice, Met Cloisters || met
How did the disciples hear these words that night? The Gospels do not tell us. Perhaps they did not fully understand, as the Gospels often say of them. The meaning would grow clearer, in the story, only after the events that followed. || Pilgrim ampulla Holy Land bread and wine || wikimedia
CHAPTER: Covenant Echoes
The word covenant carries a lot of weight in this passage. A covenant, in the Hebrew Bible, is a binding agreement between God and a people, often sealed with a ritual: an oath, a sacrifice, a meal. To speak of covenant is to speak of belonging. || Covenant at Sinai, Exodus 24 || text-card
In Exodus twenty-four, after the Law is given at Sinai, Moses takes blood and sprinkles it on the people, saying this is the blood of the covenant that the Lord has made with you. Then the elders go up the mountain and eat and drink in God's presence. || Exodus 24:3–11 || text-card
When Mark has Jesus say blood of the covenant, the phrase is nearly identical to Moses' words in Exodus. Many scholars hear a deliberate echo: at this table, a new moment of binding is being declared. || Moses sprinkling blood, Sinai, Gustave Dore || wikimedia
Luke and Paul use another phrase, new covenant, and this points to a different text. The prophet Jeremiah, in chapter thirty-one, promises a new covenant, written not on stone but on the heart, in which God's people will know him from the least to the greatest. || Jeremiah 31:31–34 || text-card
Jeremiah wrote in the time of crisis around the fall of Jerusalem to Babylon, in the early sixth century before the common era. His words about a new covenant were a promise to a broken people, not a replacement of Israel by someone else. || The Prophet Jeremiah, Michelangelo, Sistine Chapel || wikimedia
Here we need to be careful with a word that has sometimes been misused. Later Christian writers called the collection of Jewish scriptures the Old Testament and spoke of the new covenant as superseding it. That reading, called supersessionism, has been widely questioned by modern scholars. || Codex Leningradensis, Hebrew Bible || wikimedia
Many historians and theologians now emphasise that Jeremiah's promise is addressed to the house of Israel and the house of Judah. Within the first-century Jewish setting, it is better understood as a hope about renewal within the covenant people than as a break from them. || Dead Sea Scroll, Great Isaiah Scroll || wikimedia
Another echo comes from the Passover itself. The lamb's blood on the doorposts marked households for protection in Exodus twelve. Does Jesus' language connect his death to that lamb? Paul seems to think so, since he writes elsewhere that Christ our Passover has been sacrificed. || 1 Corinthians 5:7 || text-card
There is also the Servant of the Lord in Isaiah fifty-three, who is described as bearing the sins of many. Jesus' words about blood poured out for many may recall that song. The echo is plausible, though interpreters disagree on how direct it is. || Isaiah 53:10–12 || text-card
What we have, then, is a meal that draws on several streams at once: the Exodus, Sinai, the prophets, and the hope for a coming kingdom. Layers of Scripture sit beneath the words, which is how Jewish teachers regularly taught. || Great Isaiah Scroll column 53 || wikimedia
Jesus also looks forward. He says he will not drink the fruit of the vine until he drinks it new in the kingdom of God. A meal that begins in memory of the past ends with a glance toward a banquet still to come. || Isaiah 25:6–8 || text-card
That forward look has an echo of its own in Isaiah, who imagines a feast for all peoples on the mountain of the Lord. The image of a great shared table is deeply rooted in Jewish hope, and it shapes the way the Gospels talk about the kingdom. || Wedding Feast, Veronese, Louvre || wikimedia
So when we hear the bread and cup in this light, we are not hearing an isolated ritual. We are listening to a teacher who speaks in the language of covenant, Passover and promise, and who gives it a new, urgent centre. || Last Supper, Fra Angelico, San Marco || wikimedia
CHAPTER: Was It a Passover Meal?
Here we reach one of the great puzzles of the Last Supper. The Synoptic Gospels say clearly that it was a Passover meal. John seems to say something different. This difference has occupied scholars for centuries. || Mark 14:12; John 18:28 || text-card
In Mark, Matthew and Luke, the disciples prepare the Passover on the day when the lamb is sacrificed. The meal is eaten that evening, and Jesus is arrested, tried and crucified the next day. In this chronology, the Last Supper is a Passover. || Luke 22:7–8 || text-card
John, however, tells a different story. When Jesus is taken to Pilate, the Jewish leaders do not enter the governor's headquarters, in order to avoid ritual defilement, so they can still eat the Passover. That suggests the Passover meal was still to come. || John 18:28 || text-card
Later in John, as Pilate hands Jesus over for crucifixion, the text says it was the day of Preparation for the Passover, about noon. In this outline, Jesus dies at the very hour when the Passover lambs are being slaughtered in the Temple. || John 19:14 || text-card
So in John the supper is held the evening before Passover, and Jesus dies on the day itself. In the Synoptics, the supper is the Passover, and Jesus dies the following day. The two outlines cannot both be literally right about the date. || The Crucifixion, Mantegna, Louvre || wikimedia
Scholars have proposed many solutions. One suggestion is that different Jewish groups used different calendars, so that some celebrated the festival on a different day. The Dead Sea Scrolls community, for instance, followed a solar calendar. || Dead Sea Scrolls, Qumran Cave 4 || wikimedia
Others suggest that John is making a theological point. By placing Jesus' death at the moment of the lambs' slaughter, he highlights a symbolic connection between Jesus and the Passover lamb, an image he introduces early with John the Baptist's words. || John 1:29 || text-card
Another view holds that the Synoptic accounts are historically right and that John adjusted the chronology for theological reasons. Joachim Jeremias argued strongly for the Passover character of the meal, listing features such as the evening setting, the wine and the hymn. || Joachim Jeremias, The Eucharistic Words of Jesus, 1966 || text-card
Raymond Brown, in his commentary on John, argued that the difficulty is real and that we may not be able to settle it. He thought both traditions preserved authentic memories and that the shape of each account served the author's purposes. || Raymond E. Brown, The Gospel According to John XIII–XXI || text-card
What does this mean for how we understand the supper? If it was a Passover meal, then its symbols of bread and cup echo the festival's own symbols. If it was a meal on the eve of Passover, it is still a festival-time meal, charged with the same atmosphere. || Passover Haggadah, Sarajevo Haggadah || wikimedia
Either way, the Gospels agree that this meal took place in the days of Passover, in Jerusalem, at a time of heightened memory about liberation. The date question changes the exact outline of the week, but not the central setting. || Jerusalem at Passover, 19th century engraving || loc
It is a good moment to notice how historians work. Where sources conflict, they do not simply pick the one they prefer. They ask what each source wants to do, and which details are least likely to have been invented. That patience is worth carrying with us. || Ancient scrolls in a library, Qumran || wikimedia
For our story, we will hold both possibilities open. We will not insist that the Last Supper was a Seder in the later sense, since the Seder as we know it took shape after the Temple's fall. But we will keep in mind that it unfolded in Passover time. || Seder plate, 20th century || wikimedia
