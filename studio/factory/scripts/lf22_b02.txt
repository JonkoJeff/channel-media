@chapter A12 cafe_sunset | Myth two: love at first sight
It's one of the oldest stories we tell. Two strangers' eyes meet across a crowded room, and in that instant they just know. Poets have written about it for centuries. Many couples say it happened to them. But is it really possible to fall in love in a single glance? Or does something else happen in those first seconds, something we later remember as love?

@char A10 living_room_day | Fast judgments | The mind works quickly
To start answering that, we need to know how quickly the mind forms impressions of a new face. The answer turns out to be astonishingly fast. Faster than a blink.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Willis & Todorov | Psychological Science, 2006 | First impressions: making up your mind after a 100-ms exposure to a face
In 2006, Janine Willis and Alexander Todorov at Princeton showed people photographs of unfamiliar faces for different amounts of time, some for just a tenth of a second. Then they asked people to judge each face on traits like attractiveness, likeability, trustworthiness and competence.

@stat A12 cafe_sunset | Time needed for a first impression | 100 ms | judgments after a tenth of a second closely matched judgments made with no time limit
Judgments made after just a tenth of a second were closely related to judgments made by other people who could look at the faces for as long as they liked. Giving people more time mostly made them more confident, without changing their impressions very much. In other words, the mind decides quickly, and then mostly just becomes surer of what it already decided.

@char A03 cafe_sunset | Not the same as accuracy | Fast is not always right
Now, an important point. These studies show that our impressions are fast and fairly consistent between people. They don't show that those impressions are correct. A face can look trustworthy without the person being trustworthy. Speed and accuracy are different things.

@chapter A13 rooftop_dusk | Thin slices
Still, there are situations where very brief glimpses do seem to carry real information. A famous study showed this with teachers.

@study A04 attic_rain | Ambady & Rosenthal | Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 1993 | Half a minute: predicting teacher evaluations from thin slices of nonverbal behavior and physical attractiveness
In 1993, Nalini Ambady and Robert Rosenthal showed people silent video clips of university teachers, each lasting just a few seconds, up to half a minute. With no sound, viewers rated the teachers on qualities like confidence, warmth and enthusiasm. Then the researchers compared those ratings with the evaluations given by the teachers' actual students at the end of the semester.

@char A12 living_room_day | The surprise | Seconds predicted a semester
The ratings from the brief silent clips were strongly related to the students' end-of-semester evaluations. A few seconds of body language predicted much of what students thought after months of classes. Ambady called these brief glimpses thin slices of behaviour.

@char A10 cabin_fireplace | Why it matters here | First impressions carry something
So when we meet someone and feel an immediate warmth, or an immediate unease, we may be picking up on something real. How they move, how they smile, how they respond. We take in a great deal in a very short time. But that's still a long way from love.

@chapter A15 cafe_sunset | What love at first sight really is
So what are people actually feeling when they say it was love at first sight? A group of researchers in the Netherlands decided to find out.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Zsok, Haucke, De Wit & Barelds | Personal Relationships, 2017 | What kind of love is love at first sight? An empirical investigation
In 2017, Florian Zsok and his colleagues asked people to report their feelings shortly after meeting potential partners, in online surveys, in a lab and at dating events. Occasionally, people said they had experienced love at first sight with someone they had just met.

@char A03 cafe_sunset | What it was made of | Mostly attraction
But when the researchers looked at what those feelings contained, they found that love at first sight was strongly linked to how physically attractive people found each other. It rarely included the other parts of love that people describe in long relationships, like deep intimacy or commitment. It looked more like a powerful first attraction than love in the full sense.

@char A13 rooftop_dusk | Memory rewrites | The story we tell later
They also noticed something else. People who were already in relationships sometimes remembered their first meeting as love at first sight, even if it may not have felt quite so certain at the time. Our memories have a way of polishing the beginning of a story once we know how it turned out.

@list A12 living_room_day | Love at first sight, according to the research | It does feel real to people ;; It is closely tied to physical attraction ;; It rarely includes intimacy or commitment yet ;; Memories of it may grow stronger later
So, love at first sight. It's a real feeling, and people do experience it. But it's mostly a strong attraction, the spark at the start, not the full fire of love. The intimacy and commitment come later, if they come at all, and our memories may later blend them all together.

@char A10 cabin_fireplace | Slow beginnings | Love that grows
And it's worth remembering that many lasting connections begin without any spark at all. Plenty of people say they hardly noticed their partner or best friend at first. The feeling grew slowly, through shared time and small moments, until one day they realised how much this person had come to matter. A quiet beginning is not a lesser one.

@chapter A10 attic_rain | A single word
Before we leave first impressions, there's one more classic finding that shows just how much a first moment can shape everything that follows.

@study A04 attic_rain | Solomon Asch | Journal of Abnormal and Social Psychology, 1946 | Forming impressions of personality
In 1946, the psychologist Solomon Asch gave people a short list of traits describing an imaginary person. Intelligent, skilful, industrious, warm, determined, practical, cautious. Others got exactly the same list, except that the word warm was replaced by cold.

@char A03 cafe_sunset | One word changed everything | Warm versus cold
That one word changed the whole picture. The warm person was seen as generous, happy and good-natured. The cold person, with all the other traits the same, was seen much less kindly. Asch argued that some traits act as central organising points, colouring how we interpret everything else about someone.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Harold Kelley | Journal of Personality, 1950 | The warm-cold variable in first impressions of persons
A few years later, in 1950, Harold Kelley tried this with a real person. Students were told a guest lecturer was coming, and given a short written note about him. For some, the note described him as rather warm. For others, rather cold. Everything else in the note was the same, and so was the lecture.

@char A12 living_room_day | The same lecture | Different people saw different men
After the class, the students who had read rather warm rated the lecturer more positively than those who had read rather cold. And in Kelley's study, students who expected warmth were more likely to join in the class discussion. A single word before they even met him seemed to shape how they saw him, and how they behaved towards him.

@photo lf22_b02_01.jpg | A first meeting
That's worth remembering about our own first meetings. What we expect to see in someone shapes what we notice. And how we behave towards them in turn shapes how they respond to us. A first impression isn't just something we receive. It's something we help create, in both directions.

@char A10 cabin_fireplace | So, is it real? | A spark, not a story
So, is love at first sight a myth? Not quite. The spark is real, and the mind forms impressions incredibly fast. But what we feel in that first glance is mostly attraction and expectation. Love, in the deeper sense, tends to be built afterwards, in all the moments that come next.
