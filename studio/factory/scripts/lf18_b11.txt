@chapter A12 living_room_day | Faces in clouds
Have you ever looked at an electrical socket and thought that it looked surprised? Or at the front of a car and seen a face with headlights for eyes? Or at the surface of the moon and seen someone looking back? You are not alone, and there is nothing wrong with you. It's one of the most common things that human brains do.

@char A01 cafe_sunset | The habit | Seeing faces where there are none
The experience is called face pareidolia, from Greek words meaning something like a false image next to the real one. It happens when the brain finds a familiar pattern in a random or unrelated shape. Faces are the most common example, but people also see animals in clouds and figures in the grain of wood.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Liu et al. | Cortex, 2014 | Seeing Jesus in toast: neural and behavioral correlates of face pareidolia
In 2014, a team led by Jiangang Liu at Beijing Normal University, working with Kang Lee, scanned the brains of people looking at images of random noise. When people reported seeing a face in the noise, a part of the brain specialised for faces, the fusiform face area, became more active, even though no face was present.

@char A04 attic_rain | What it suggests | A face detector that's eager
It seems that the brain has a sensitive face detector, set to trigger early. It's cheap to mistake a bush for a face, and costly to miss a real one. Being quick to see a face, even a false one, is the safer bet. A smoke alarm that sometimes sounds when you burn the toast is better than one that never sounds at all.

@list A03 living_room_day | Why a quick face detector helps | Faces tell you who is near ;; Expressions tell you how they feel ;; Missing a face can be costly ;; A false alarm usually costs very little
This kind of reasoning is sometimes described as an error management idea in psychology. If one kind of mistake is much worse than the other, a system tuned for survival will lean towards the cheaper one. The extra faces in your clouds are the price of never missing a real one.

@chapter A13 rooftop_dusk | Born for faces
How early does this start? In 1991, Mark Johnson and colleagues at the University of London tested newborn babies, only a few minutes old in some cases. They moved cards past the babies' faces, some with a pattern of three dots arranged like a face, and some with the dots jumbled. The babies turned their heads further to follow the face-like pattern.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Johnson, Dziurawiec, Ellis & Morton | Cognition, 1991 | Newborns' preferential tracking of face-like stimuli and its subsequent decline
This was one of the first results to suggest that humans are born with some kind of bias towards faces. It has been debated and refined over the years, and some researchers argue that the preference is for a general kind of pattern, with more dots at the top, and not for faces as such. But the basic finding has been repeated in different forms.

@char A12 living_room_day | A famous upside-down face | The Thatcher illusion
Faces are processed in a special way, and one of the best demonstrations is the Thatcher illusion, discovered by Peter Thompson at the University of York in 1980. Take a photo of a face, and flip the eyes and the mouth upside down inside the face. Turn the picture the right way up, and it looks grotesque. Turn it upside down, and it looks almost normal.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Thompson | Perception, 1980 | Margaret Thatcher: a new illusion
Thompson used a photograph of the then British prime minister, which is how the illusion got its name. The point is that when a face is upside down, the brain has a harder time processing the way its features relate to each other. The sense of a whole face is tuned to the upright orientation, and so the oddity goes unnoticed.

@char A10 attic_rain | The inversion effect | Faces are special
Another study, by Robert Yin in 1969, found that people were much worse at recognising upside-down faces than they were at recognising upside-down houses. This was taken as a sign that faces are handled by a particular set of mechanisms, which rely on seeing the face as a whole.

@list A12 living_room_day | Evidence that faces are special | Newborns prefer face-like patterns ;; A dedicated area responds strongly to faces ;; Upside-down faces are hard to read ;; We see faces in clouds and sockets
These are four lines of evidence pointing in the same direction. The brain treats faces as a kind of privileged class of objects. That makes sense for a species that depends so heavily on other people.

@chapter A06 cafe_sunset | The face on Mars
In July 1976, the Viking 1 orbiter took a photograph of a region of Mars called Cydonia. In one of the images, a mesa looked remarkably like a face staring up at the sky, with eyes, a nose and a mouth. NASA released it with a note that it was a trick of light and shadow, but many people believed that it was a monument built by an alien civilisation.

@char A01 cafe_sunset | A closer look | Better pictures
In later years, better cameras took more detailed pictures. In 1998 and 2001, the Mars Global Surveyor and then other probes photographed the same spot, in much higher resolution. The face disappeared into a hill, and the shadows, which had made the eyes and the mouth, were gone. It was a natural landform, seen at low resolution and under a particular light.

@stat A06 cafe_sunset | Resolution of the original photo | About 50 metres per pixel | later images showed the hill at a much finer scale
That's a useful number. The original photo had a resolution of roughly fifty metres per pixel, which is coarse enough that a few dark pixels could make an eye. As soon as the detail improved, the face dissolved. It's a perfect example of the brain filling in a pattern from sparse information.

@char A04 attic_rain | The bigger term | Apophenia
The general tendency to see meaningful patterns in random data is called apophenia, a word introduced in 1958 by the German psychiatrist Klaus Conrad. It's usually a harmless quirk. But the same tendency, in other settings, can lead us to see conspiracies in coincidences, or reasons in random events.

@chapter A10 attic_rain | When we feel out of control
Interestingly, our tendency to see patterns changes depending on how we feel. In 2008, Jennifer Whitson and Adam Galinsky ran a series of experiments suggesting that when people feel a lack of control, they see more patterns in noise.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Whitson & Galinsky | Science, 2008 | Lacking control increases illusory pattern perception
In one experiment, volunteers were asked to recall a time when they had felt a lack of control, such as being caught in a situation that they couldn't change. Others recalled a time when they'd been in control. Afterwards, those who recalled a lack of control were more likely to see images in random visual static, where none had been placed.

@char A12 living_room_day | Why it makes sense | Order is comforting
The authors argued that seeing patterns is a way of regaining a sense of order. If the world feels unpredictable, a hidden pattern, even a false one, is more comforting than pure chance. It's a plausible idea, and it's supported by several experiments, though like many studies in this area, it has been tested mainly in university students.

@list A03 living_room_day | What the research suggests | Feeling out of control can increase pattern seeing ;; People may then see links that aren't there ;; Restoring a sense of control can reduce it ;; This applies to all of us, in different moments
It's worth keeping in mind during stressful times. When life feels chaotic, we are more likely to look for explanations, and less likely to check them. Knowing that can help us to slow down before we decide that something is a sign.

@char A19 cabin_fireplace | A classic | Superstition in pigeons
In 1948, the behaviourist B. F. Skinner reported something rather charming. He placed hungry pigeons in a box and fed them at regular intervals, whatever they did. After a while, most of the pigeons had developed odd routines, such as turning in circles or bobbing their heads, as though these actions brought the food.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Skinner | Journal of Experimental Psychology, 1948 | Superstition in the pigeon
Skinner argued that this was the pigeon version of superstition. The bird happened to do something just before the food arrived, and it mistook the coincidence for a cause. Later researchers have suggested other explanations for the behaviour, but the idea of accidental pairing remains a popular way of thinking about human rituals.

@char A04 attic_rain | A fair note | Not always a mistake
It's important to say that finding patterns is not a defect. It's the foundation of learning, language, science and music. The trouble comes when we're too sure of a pattern that has too little evidence behind it. The skill is to enjoy the pattern, and then to test it.

@list A12 living_room_day | A way to test a pattern | Ask how many examples you have ;; Ask what would count against it ;; Check if the pattern appears again ;; Ask whether a simple chance explanation would do
These are the habits of good scientists, and of good friends in a late-night conversation. They don't ruin the magic of a pattern. They simply help us to tell between a discovery and a cloud that looks like a dog.

A knot in the wood, a swirl of paint, and there it is, a face. It's a small gift of a mind that evolved to be sensitive to people, and it's a reminder of how little the brain needs in order to find something it recognises.

@list A03 living_room_day | Pattern or coincidence? | Count how many examples you really have ;; Look for examples that don't fit ;; Ask whether chance could explain it ;; Sleep on it before deciding
When something looks like a sign, a pause helps. Count your examples honestly, including the misses. Ask whether chance could produce the same thing. And if the feeling is strong, sleep on it. The brain's pattern finder is wonderfully fast and sometimes over-eager, and a little time gives the sceptical part of the mind a chance to catch up.

@list A03 living_room_day | Words for what you just learned | Pareidolia ;; Apophenia ;; Inversion effect ;; Illusory pattern perception
Four terms from the faces chapter. Pareidolia is seeing faces in things. Apophenia is seeing patterns in noise. The inversion effect is the difficulty of reading upside-down faces. And illusory pattern perception is the tendency to see more patterns when we feel out of control.

@char A15 cabin_fireplace | A gentle thought | A little friendly company
There's something rather lovely about a brain that sees faces in everything. It means that we are made for company. The next time a house, a cloud or a mug seems to be smiling at you, you can let it. A bit of imagined company is a very human way of making the world feel less empty.

@char A08 cabin_fireplace | Looking back | What the faces taught us
So what did the faces in clouds teach us? That the brain has an eager face detector. That we seem to be born with a bias towards faces. That we see more patterns when we feel out of control. And that the trick is to enjoy a pattern, and then to check whether it is real.
