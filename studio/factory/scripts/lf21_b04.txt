@chapter A13 rooftop_dusk | A brain that keeps learning
You've probably seen the phrase somewhere. Rewire your brain. It appears on book covers, in apps and in advertisements, usually next to a promise that sounds a little too good. Rewire your brain in twenty-one days. Rewire your brain for happiness. It makes the brain sound like a house where you can simply call an electrician.

@char A10 attic_rain | Behind the slogan | Something real, and something oversold
Behind the slogan, there is something real, and it is genuinely remarkable. The adult brain does change with experience. But the real story is slower, more specific and, honestly, more interesting than the slogans. So let's walk through it gently, starting with an idea from the middle of the twentieth century.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Donald Hebb | The Organization of Behavior, 1949 | Proposed that connections between neurons strengthen when the cells are repeatedly active together
In 1949, the Canadian psychologist Donald Hebb published a book called The Organization of Behavior. In it, he proposed that when one brain cell repeatedly helps to fire another, the connection between them grows stronger. Decades later, the neuroscientist Carla Shatz summed up the idea in a phrase that stuck. Cells that fire together, wire together.

@char A12 living_room_day | A path through grass | How practice leaves a trace
A helpful picture is a path through a field of tall grass. The first time you walk across, it's hard going and you barely leave a mark. But if you take the same route every day, a path appears. It becomes easier to walk, and soon it's the way you go without thinking. Much of learning, and much of habit, works a little like that.

@chapter A15 cafe_sunset | The taxi drivers of London
For a long time, this was mostly theory and animal research. Then, around the year 2000, a group of neuroscientists in London found a beautiful way to study it in people.

@photo lf21_b04_01.jpg | Thousands of streets to learn
To earn a licence to drive the famous black cabs of London, drivers must pass a test known as the Knowledge. They have to learn the layout of thousands of streets and landmarks in the city, and be able to plan routes between them from memory. Preparing for it typically takes years.

#ai lf21_b04_01.jpg | Editorial illustration of a winding old city street at dusk with wet cobblestones reflecting warm street lamps, a folded paper street map on a cafe table in the foreground, soft painterly documentary style, no people, no text, no logos

@study A04 attic_rain | Maguire, Gadian, Johnsrude, Good, Ashburner, Frackowiak & Frith | PNAS, 2000 | Navigation-related structural change in the hippocampi of taxi drivers
In a study published in 2000, Eleanor Maguire and her colleagues scanned the brains of licensed London taxi drivers and compared them with men who didn't drive taxis. In the taxi drivers, the back part of the hippocampus, a region involved in memory and navigation, was larger. The front part was somewhat smaller.

@char A03 cafe_sunset | Years on the job | A link with experience
And there was a telling detail. Among the taxi drivers, the longer someone had been doing the job, the larger that back part of the hippocampus tended to be. That hinted that the experience itself might be changing the brain. But it couldn't prove it. Perhaps people with a larger hippocampus were simply more likely to become taxi drivers in the first place.

@chapter A12 living_room_day | Following the trainees
To answer that properly, you would need to scan people before they started training and again afterwards. That's exactly what happened next.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Woollett & Maguire | Current Biology, 2011 | Acquiring the Knowledge of London's layout drives structural brain changes
Katherine Woollett and Eleanor Maguire followed seventy-nine trainee taxi drivers over several years as they studied for the Knowledge, along with a comparison group who didn't. At the start, there was no difference in the hippocampus between the groups.

@char A13 rooftop_dusk | Who passed, who didn't | The change followed the learning
Thirty-nine of the trainees eventually qualified. In those who passed, the grey matter in the back of the hippocampus had increased. In the trainees who didn't qualify, and in the comparison group, there was no such change. The learning came first, and the brain change followed.

@char A10 cabin_fireplace | Why this is lovely | Even adults
This is one of the clearest pieces of evidence we have that sustained learning can physically reshape part of the adult brain. These weren't children. Many were well into adulthood. And the change didn't come from a slogan or a weekend course. It came from years of patient, daily effort.

@chapter A15 cafe_sunset | Three months of juggling
Learning the Knowledge takes years. Can the brain change faster than that, with something simpler?

@study A04 attic_rain | Draganski, Gaser, Busch, Schuierer, Bogdahn & May | Nature, 2004 | Neuroplasticity: changes in grey matter induced by training
In 2004, Bogdan Draganski and his colleagues in Germany taught a group of adults to juggle three balls. They scanned their brains before training, after about three months of practice once the volunteers could juggle for at least a minute, and again after another three months in which they mostly stopped.

@char A12 living_room_day | Grew, then faded | Use it or lose it
After the three months of juggling, the jugglers showed a temporary increase in grey matter in areas involved in processing visual motion. Then, after they stopped practising, those areas shrank back towards where they started. A comparison group who didn't juggle showed no such change.

@char A03 cafe_sunset | Two lessons | Fast, and fragile
There are two lessons in that. The first is encouraging. Measurable change can appear within months. The second is just as important. Without continued use, it fades. The path in the grass grows back over if nobody walks it.

@char A10 cabin_fireplace | Think of a skill | Something you used to do
Think of a skill you once had and let slip. A language from school, an instrument, a sport you played as a teenager. If you picked it up again, it would probably feel clumsy at first. But it would likely come back faster than it took to learn the first time. Something of the old path is still there, under the grass, waiting to be walked again.

@chapter A13 cabin_fireplace | Fingers and keys
Here's one more study, and it holds a surprise about the power of imagination.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Pascual-Leone and colleagues | Journal of Neurophysiology, 1995 | Modulation of muscle responses evoked by transcranial magnetic stimulation during the acquisition of new fine motor skills
In 1995, Alvaro Pascual-Leone and his colleagues asked volunteers who had never played the piano to practise a simple five-finger exercise for two hours a day, over five days. Using a technique that maps the movement areas of the brain from the outside, they found that the area controlling those finger muscles expanded over the days of practice.

@char A10 attic_rain | And the surprise | Practising in your head
And here's the surprise. Another group only imagined playing the exercise, without moving their fingers. They showed changes too, though smaller ones than real practice. Imagining the movement in detail seemed to rehearse some of the same circuits. Real practice still worked best, but the mind alone was not nothing.

@chapter A12 living_room_day | What the slogans get wrong
So the brain is changeable. Doesn't that mean the slogans are right? Not quite. Let's look at what they leave out.

@list A13 rooftop_dusk | What plasticity really means | Change is specific to what you practise ;; It usually takes weeks, months or years ;; Without continued use, it can fade ;; A bigger brain area is not a better person ;; Scientists still debate whether adults grow many new neurons
Brain change is specific. Juggling changed areas involved in seeing motion, not some general happiness centre. It takes time and repetition. It fades without use. And a change in the size of a brain region doesn't automatically mean a change in your character.

@char A04 attic_rain | An open question | New neurons in adults?
There's even a live scientific debate about one of the most famous claims, that adults keep growing new neurons in the hippocampus. In 2018, two studies came out within weeks of each other. One, led by Shawn Sorrells in the journal Nature, found very little sign of new neurons after childhood. Another, led by Maura Boldrini in Cell Stem Cell, reported that new neurons continued to form even in older adults. Researchers are still working out why the results differ.

@char A12 cafe_sunset | The honest version | Slow, specific, real
So here's the honest version, the one without the hype. Your brain is not fixed. It responds to what you do, again and again, over time. But it responds to repetition, not to wishes. It isn't rewired by deciding. It's reshaped by doing.

And that leads us neatly to the next chapter. Because the most powerful kind of repetition in our lives isn't the kind we choose each morning. It's the kind we no longer notice at all. Habits. How do they really form, how long do they take, and why is the famous twenty-one day rule probably wrong? Let's find out.
