@chapter A13 rooftop_dusk | Myth three: opposites attract
Think of the couples you know, or the couples in your favourite films. There's often a pair who seem like complete opposites. One is loud, the other quiet. One is tidy, the other chaotic. And people say, well, opposites attract. It's such a common saying that most of us never stop to question it.

@char A10 attic_rain | A satisfying story | Two halves of a whole
It's also a satisfying story. It suggests that each of us is missing something, and that the right person will fill in the gaps. But psychologists have tested this idea many times, over many decades. And the results point fairly clearly in one direction.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Donn Byrne | Journal of Abnormal and Social Psychology, 1961 | Interpersonal attraction and attitude similarity
In 1961, the psychologist Donn Byrne gave students a questionnaire about their attitudes on various topics. A couple of weeks later, he showed them a questionnaire supposedly filled out by another student, and asked how much they thought they would like this person. In fact, the stranger's answers had been arranged to be either very similar to the student's own answers, or very different.

@char A12 living_room_day | The result | Similarity won
The more similar the stranger's attitudes were, the more the students expected to like them. Byrne and others went on to repeat versions of this many times, and the basic pattern held. People tend to be drawn to those who share their views and values, not those who oppose them.

@study A04 attic_rain | Montoya, Horton & Kirchner | Journal of Social and Personal Relationships, 2008 | Is actual similarity necessary for attraction? A meta-analysis of actual and perceived similarity
In 2008, Matthew Montoya, Robert Horton and Jeffrey Kirchner combined more than three hundred studies on similarity and attraction. They found something interesting. In studies where people hadn't actually met, real similarity predicted attraction. But in existing relationships, what mattered more was perceived similarity, how similar people believed they were.

@char A03 cafe_sunset | Feeling alike | The belief matters
That's a subtle and rather lovely point. In a close relationship, feeling that someone understands you and shares your outlook may matter more than whether your answers on a questionnaire would actually match. We tend to feel similar to the people we feel close to, and close to the people we feel similar to.

@char A13 rooftop_dusk | So what about opposites? | Complementing, not opposing
So why do opposites seem to attract in stories? Partly because those couples are memorable. Nobody tells a story about two quiet bookworms who fell in love over their shared love of quiet books. And partly because couples can differ in small, complementary ways, like one enjoying cooking and the other enjoying washing up, while still sharing the values that matter most.

@chapter A12 cafe_sunset | The power of being nearby
If similarity is one key ingredient, there's another that's even simpler, and it's almost embarrassingly ordinary. It's just being nearby.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Festinger, Schachter & Back | Social Pressures in Informal Groups, 1950 | Friendship formation in a student housing development
In 1950, Leon Festinger, Stanley Schachter and Kurt Back studied friendships in a housing development for married students at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology. The students had been assigned to their apartments more or less at random, so they hadn't chosen their neighbours.

@char A12 living_room_day | Next door | Distance shaped friendship
When the researchers asked residents to name their closest friends in the development, a clear pattern appeared. People were much more likely to be friends with those who lived next door, or very close by, than with those a few doors further away. Even small details, like whose door was near the stairs or the post boxes, seemed to influence who became friends.

@char A10 cabin_fireplace | Ordinary moments | Small encounters add up
It sounds almost too simple. But think about how many of your own friendships began because you happened to sit near someone, work near someone, or live near someone. Repeated small encounters, saying hello, chatting in a corridor, give a connection the chance to grow.

@chapter A15 cafe_sunset | Simply seeing someone
And there's a reason those repeated encounters matter. It's one of the most robust findings in social psychology.

@study A04 attic_rain | Robert Zajonc | Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 1968 | Attitudinal effects of mere exposure
In 1968, Robert Zajonc published a series of studies showing that simply being exposed to something repeatedly tends to make people like it more. Made-up words, unfamiliar symbols, photographs of strangers. The more often people saw them, the more positively they tended to rate them. He called it the mere exposure effect.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Moreland & Beach | Journal of Experimental Social Psychology, 1992 | Exposure effects in the classroom: the development of affinity among students
In 1992, Richard Moreland and Scott Beach tested this in a real university lecture course. Four women, chosen because they looked fairly similar, attended the lectures different numbers of times over the semester. One attended no lectures, one attended five, one ten and one fifteen. They never spoke to the other students. They just sat in the room.

@bars A12 living_room_day | Lectures attended by each woman | lectures | Woman A:0 ;; Woman B:5 ;; Woman C:10 ;; Woman D:15
At the end of the semester, students were shown photographs of the four women and asked to rate them. The more lectures a woman had attended, the more attractive and likeable students rated her, even though most of them didn't remember ever having seen her. Simply being present had made her seem more appealing.

@char A03 cafe_sunset | Familiar feels safe | Why exposure works
Why would this happen? One explanation is that familiar things are easier for the brain to process, and that ease feels pleasant. Another is that, over most of human history, something we've seen many times without harm is probably safe. Either way, familiarity tends to breed fondness, at least when the first impressions aren't negative.

@photo lf22_b03_01.jpg | Familiar faces
So some of the connections that feel most natural might have grown out of simple familiarity. The barista you see every morning. The colleague at the next desk. The neighbour you nod to. Each small encounter adds a little warmth, often without either person noticing.

@char A10 attic_rain | A caution | Familiarity can cut both ways
One caution. Mere exposure seems to work best when the first impression is neutral or positive. If you already dislike something, seeing it over and over doesn't reliably make you like it more. And a little novelty matters too. Familiarity builds comfort, but sparks often come from something unexpected within that comfort.

@list A13 rooftop_dusk | What brings people together | Shared values and outlook ;; Feeling understood and similar ;; Being nearby ;; Seeing each other often ;; Small, repeated, positive moments
So, do opposites attract? Mostly, no. People tend to be drawn to those who share their values and outlook, who are nearby and whom they see often. What looks like opposites attracting is often two people who share what matters deeply, while differing in ways that complement each other.

@char A12 living_room_day | A comforting thought | Connection grows in ordinary places
There's something comforting in that. Connection isn't only about finding a rare, perfect opposite somewhere out in the world. Very often, it grows quietly in the ordinary places we already go, with people we see again and again.

@list A15 cabin_fireplace | Places where connection quietly grows | The same café each morning ;; A regular class or club ;; A walk at the same time each day ;; The people next door
Perhaps that's a reason to keep a few regular places and routines in your life. The same café on a Saturday. A weekly class. A walk at the same time each day. The people next door. These ordinary repetitions are exactly where familiarity, and then fondness, have the chance to grow.
