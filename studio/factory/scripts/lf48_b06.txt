@chapter N19 street_day | Learning the rules
Every expectation has to come from somewhere. When Ines hears a key change and feels a lift, her brain is drawing on a huge, quiet store of knowledge about how music of her culture tends to go. Nobody sat her down and taught it to her. So how did she get it?

@char N02 living_room_day | Learning without trying | Statistical learning
Researchers call it statistical learning. The brain notices which sounds tend to follow which, over and over, and it builds up a feel for what's likely. It doesn't need effort, or attention, or instruction. It just keeps score, quietly, all the time.

@study N07 cafe_sunset | Jenny Saffran, Richard Aslin and Elissa Newport | Science, 1996 | Eight-month-old babies heard two minutes of nonsense syllables and picked up which ones went together
In 1996, three researchers at the University of Rochester published a now-famous study with babies. Eight-month-olds listened for two minutes to a stream of nonsense syllables with no pauses, like a recording of a made-up language. Afterward, the babies could tell the difference between syllable groups that had always gone together and groups that hadn't. They had picked up the pattern, from two minutes of listening.

@char N18 autumn_overlook | Not just for speech | Tones, too
Later work by Saffran and others showed that infants can do the same with sequences of musical tones. They track which notes follow which, and they notice when a pattern is broken. That suggests that a very basic skill, noticing what tends to come next, is already at work in the first year of life.

@char N10 cabin_fireplace | Growing up inside a musical culture | Years of listening
Over the years, every child absorbs thousands of hours of the music around them. Lullabies, songs on the radio, hymns, jingles, playground chants. By the time they start school, they've built up a detailed, unspoken map of what sounds right.

@study N07 street_day | Corrigall and Trainor | Music Perception, 2010 | Preschoolers showed sensitivity to keys and chord patterns of Western music by about age five
In a study from 2010, the Canadian researchers Kathleen Corrigall and Laurel Trainor tested young children on whether they could tell when a tune ended on a note that didn't fit. By about the age of five, the children showed they had absorbed key patterns in the music of their culture, even though they were far too young to know any music theory.

@char N23 living_room_day | Why foreign music can sound odd | A different map
This helps explain a familiar experience. Music from another culture can sound strange, or even confusing, at first. It isn't worse or better. It's simply following a different set of rules, and your brain hasn't learned them yet. Listeners who grow up with other scales and rhythms have maps of their own, and music that fits their map gives them the same kind of pleasure.

It's a little like learning your native language. You aren't taught the grammar, you just hear enough sentences that the grammar settles in. And then, when someone uses a sentence in a clever or surprising way, you notice, because you know what a normal sentence feels like.

@char N29 street_day | Some things may be shared | Across cultures
Even so, there may be some features that people everywhere find easy to recognize. In 2019, a team led by Samuel Mehr at Harvard collected songs from dozens of societies around the world and played them to tens of thousands of listeners online. Listeners could guess, well above chance, whether a song was a lullaby, a dance song, or a healing song, even when it came from a culture they'd never met.

@char N20 bedroom_night | What that suggests | Some shared habits
That doesn't mean all music is the same. It suggests that certain things, like slow, soft, gentle sounds for soothing a baby, and fast, strong, steady beats for dancing, are widely shared. Our learned maps are layered on top of a small number of more basic habits of the ear.

@char N02 cafe_sunset | The point for chills | Learning shapes the shiver
For chills, the lesson is this. A shiver isn't only a reaction to the sound. It's a reaction to the sound measured against what your brain has learned. That's one reason chills are so personal, and so often tied to music you've known for years. Your history with a piece changes how it lands.

@char N18 rooftop_dusk | Why unfamiliar music can still do it | A surprise
That said, chills don't require a piece to be familiar. People can get them from a song they're hearing for the first time, especially if the harmony or the voice does something striking. The brain's general knowledge of music, built up over years, is enough to recognize a surprise even in a new piece.

@char N10 bedroom_night | Ines and her song | An old favorite
Ines has known her song since she was a teenager, and she remembers exactly where she was the first time she heard it. And that leads naturally to the next question. If chills depend on what you've learned, then why do some people get them and some don't?
