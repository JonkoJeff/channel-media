@stat A13 rooftop_dusk | Cells in a human brain | About 86 billion | neurons, estimated by counting a few brains
A human brain holds roughly 86 billion nerve cells, neurons. That figure comes from a 2009 study by Suzana Herculano-Houzel and colleagues, who had the careful idea of dissolving brain tissue and counting the cell nuclei. Each of those cells connects to thousands of others. And somewhere in that web, your Tuesday dinner is either stored, or it isn't.

@char A10 bedroom_night | Not in one place | A pattern, not a file
Here's the interesting part. There isn't a drawer in there labelled Tuesday. A memory isn't a file sitting in a spot. It's a pattern, a particular set of connections between cells that were active together, and that became a little easier to activate together again.

@char A15 autumn_overlook | Remembering is rebuilding | Every single time
That has a surprising consequence. If a memory is a pattern of connections, then bringing it back means switching that pattern on again, and putting the scene together once more, right now, with whatever you know right now. Remembering is less like replaying a film, and more like retelling a story.

@quote A02 living_room_day | Remembering is not the re-excitation of innumerable fixed, lifeless and fragmentary traces. | Frederic Bartlett, Remembering, 1932
The British psychologist Frederic Bartlett put it beautifully almost a hundred years ago. In his book Remembering, in 1932, he wrote that remembering is not the re-excitation of innumerable fixed, lifeless and fragmentary traces. It is, he said, an imaginative reconstruction. You might wonder why a brain would work like that. Wouldn't an exact recording be better? Well, think about what a memory is actually for. You don't need to relive last Tuesday. You need to know how to act next Tuesday.

@study A12 rooftop_dusk | Daniel Schacter & Donna Rose Addis | Published in 2007 | Argued that remembering the past and imagining the future draw on much the same brain machinery
That idea was developed by Daniel Schacter and Donna Rose Addis, in a 2007 paper. They pointed to brain scans and to patients with memory loss, who often struggle to imagine future scenes as well as to recall past ones. Their suggestion was that memory is a toolkit for building scenes, and the past is only one of the things it builds.

Before we begin the journey, there's one popular idea worth a gentle look. Photographic memory. The notion that some lucky people can look at a page, and later read it off the inside of their heads, like a photograph. Does that exist?

@study A20 autumn_overlook | Charles Stromeyer & Joseph Psotka | Published in Nature, 1970 | Tested a woman who said she could hold an image in her mind in perfect detail
The best case for it is a single one. In 1970, Charles Stromeyer and Joseph Psotka reported a woman, known as Elizabeth, who seemed able to combine two patterns of random dots, seen on different days, into a three-dimensional picture, as if she were seeing both at once. That would take a truly photographic image. But no one has been able to repeat the result with her, or with anyone else.
