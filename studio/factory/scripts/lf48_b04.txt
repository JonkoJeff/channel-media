@char N02 living_room_day | A thought from 1956 | Leonard Meyer
In 1956, a music theorist and philosopher named Leonard Meyer published a book called Emotion and Meaning in Music, and in it he made an idea famous. Music, he argued, gets its emotional power from expectation. As we listen, the mind is constantly guessing what's coming next. When the music delays or twists what we expected, we feel tension. When it finally gives it to us, we feel release.

@char N18 street_day | A simple example | The end of a phrase
Think of a tune you know very well, and imagine it stopping a note before its last note. You'd feel it hang in the air, almost uncomfortably, waiting. The tune has told you where it's going, and it hasn't gone there yet. That small ache of waiting is exactly the kind of thing Meyer was talking about. Composers have known how to use it for centuries, long before anyone wrote it down as a theory.

@char N10 cabin_fireplace | The build-up | Holding back on purpose
Many pieces that give people chills use this on a large scale. A quiet start, a slow rise in volume, a melody climbing step by step, and a long approach to a high note or a big chord. The music keeps making a promise, and keeps delaying it. The longer and more skillfully it delays, the bigger the arrival feels when it comes.

@study N07 cafe_sunset | David Huron | Sweet Anticipation, MIT Press, 2006 | Described five steps of expectation: imagination, tension, prediction, reaction, appraisal
In 2006, the music cognition researcher David Huron, then at Ohio State University, built on Meyer's idea in a book called Sweet Anticipation. He described a model with five steps, with the memorable abbreviation I, T, P, R, A. Imagination, tension, prediction, reaction and appraisal. In his view, the pleasure of music comes from a whole chain of small mental events, not from any single one.

@char N29 autumn_overlook | The five steps | In plain words
In plain words, it goes like this. Before a moment arrives, you imagine what might happen, and your body gets a little tense in preparation. You make a prediction. Then the moment arrives and you have a quick, automatic reaction, followed by a slower, more thoughtful appraisal of whether it was good. All of this happens in a second or two, and mostly without any awareness at all.

@photo lf48_b04_01.jpg | A road winding toward a hill
Think of walking along a winding road toward a hilltop you can't quite see. Part of the pleasure is the not seeing. The road bends, the trees thin, the sky grows brighter, and you know the view is coming. When you finally round the last corner, the reward isn't only the view. It's the whole walk toward it.

@char N23 living_room_day | The surprise that isn't surprising | A paradox
There's a curious paradox in this. Many people who get chills from a piece of music have heard it dozens of times. They know the moment is coming. And yet it still works. Huron's explanation is that the brain keeps two sets of expectations running at once. One is the learned, specific expectation of this exact piece. The other is a general, built-in expectation of how music tends to go.

@char N19 cafe_sunset | Two kinds of knowing | Schemas and memory
When a piece does something that fits your memory but breaks the general pattern, the general part of your brain reacts as though it were a surprise, even while the specific part knows exactly what's coming. It's a bit like watching a magic trick you've seen before. You know how it works, and it still delights you.

That fits neatly with what we saw in the scanner. The first dopamine hot spot, the caudate, rose in the seconds when listeners were anticipating a peak. The second, the nucleus accumbens, rose at the peak itself. The brain was running Huron's chain, tension and prediction first, and then reaction and reward.

@stat N11 cabin_fireplace | Anticipation | seconds | the build-up that comes before the peak
How long is the build-up? In the Montreal studies, the anticipatory phase lasted for several seconds before a chill, and the pleasure itself was brief. It's a bit like the shape of a wave. A long, slow swell, and then a crest that lasts a moment.

@char N10 rooftop_dusk | Delay as a gift | Why waiting helps
This is worth thinking about in everyday life, too. Waiting for something good is itself a pleasure. A meal you can smell from the next room. A friend's train arriving at the platform. A message you know is coming. Music happens to be an art that works almost entirely with that feeling, shaping it moment by moment, and handing it to you in a few minutes.

@quote N27 cabin_fireplace | Part of the pleasure is the walking toward it. | the idea behind the build-up
It's why a piece that starts right at its loudest, with no climb, rarely gives anyone chills. The shiver seems to need a road behind it.

@char N18 autumn_overlook | The role of silence | The pause before
Composers also use silence. A brief pause before a big chord, or a held rest before a singer enters, makes the listener lean forward. It's a way of raising the stakes, and the stakes are entirely in the listener's own expectation. Nothing has happened yet, and already the body is waiting.

@char N02 cabin_fireplace | Not only classical | Every kind of music
None of this is limited to any style. Rock songs with a quiet verse and a loud chorus, dance tracks with a long build and a drop, folk songs where everyone joins in on the last refrain, hymns with a rising final verse. They all use the same trick, which suggests that the trick is about brains and not about any particular genre.

@char N10 bedroom_night | And now a twist | What about surprise?
But waiting isn't everything. Some of the strongest chills come from something the listener didn't see coming at all. And the science of surprise turns out to be very interesting, and a little bit counter-intuitive.
