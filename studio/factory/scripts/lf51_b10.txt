@chapter N27 rooftop_dusk | Can boredom make you creative?
Let's look at the first of boredom's supposed gifts. You may have heard that boredom is good for creativity, that it's the soil in which ideas grow. It's a lovely claim, and like many lovely claims, it deserves a careful look.

@char N10 cabin_fireplace | A popular claim | Let kids be bored
Parents are often told to let their children be bored, because boredom leads to imagination. Writers talk about long, empty afternoons that gave them their best stories. Some people swear that their best ideas arrive in the shower or on a long walk, when nothing else is going on. Is there any evidence behind it?

@char N19 living_room_day | A study | Mann and Cadman, 2014
The best-known experiment comes from the psychologist Sandi Mann at the University of Central Lancashire, in England, with her colleague Rebekah Cadman. Their paper, Does being bored make us more creative?, appeared in 2014 in the Creativity Research Journal. It has been cited many times, and it's a good place to start.

@study N09 cafe_sunset | Mann and Cadman, 2014 | University of Central Lancashire, published in the Creativity Research Journal | Participants copied numbers from a phone directory, then tried to think of uses for plastic cups
In the first experiment, some participants were asked to spend fifteen minutes copying numbers from a phone directory, a famously dull task. Others did nothing beforehand. Then everyone was asked to come up with as many uses as they could for a pair of plastic cups, a classic test of creative thinking.

@char N02 rooftop_dusk | The result | Bored minds had more ideas
The people who had copied numbers came up with more uses for the cups than the people who hadn't. In a second experiment, the researchers made the dull task even more passive, by asking people simply to read the numbers aloud, and the creative effect was stronger still. The authors suggested that boredom, especially of a mind-wandering kind, may help creativity.

@char N10 living_room_day | Another study | Boredom and associations
A related line of work was published in 2014 by Karen Gasper and Katherine Middlewood in the Journal of Experimental Social Psychology. Their paper explored what kinds of feelings encourage people to link ideas together in unusual ways. They found that both elation and boredom promoted this kind of associative thinking, more than distress or relaxation did.

@char N19 cabin_fireplace | Why might that be? | A wandering mind
One reason may be what we've already met, the default network, and the wandering mind. When a dull task keeps the surface of attention occupied, the rest of the mind can roam, linking ideas that don't usually meet. And in a bored state, the wish for something new may nudge it to explore more widely.

@char N09 rooftop_dusk | A further study | Incubation
There's also research on what psychologists call incubation. In 2012, Benjamin Baird and colleagues, including Jonathan Schooler, published a paper in Psychological Science with the title, Inspired by distraction. People who worked on a creative problem, then did an easy, undemanding task, then returned to the problem, did better than those who did a demanding task, took a rest, or went straight on.

@char N10 cafe_sunset | The easy task | Undemanding, not empty
That's a useful detail. The undemanding task, not an empty mind, was what seemed to help. Something simple enough to run on autopilot lets the mind wander toward the earlier problem, without the pressure of focus. A walk, a simple chore, a doodle or a drive may be the real-life versions.

@char N02 living_room_day | Resting brains | Not idleness
This fits with a 2012 paper by Mary Helen Immordino-Yang, Joanna Christodoulou and Vanessa Singh, in Perspectives on Psychological Science, called Rest is not idleness. They argued that quiet, reflective time supports the brain's default network, and may be important for things like understanding ourselves and others, and for making sense of our experiences.

@char N23 autumn_overlook | A sceptical note | Handle with care
Now for the careful part. The boredom and creativity story is not as settled as it sounds. The original experiments used small groups and a particular kind of test, with a narrow measure of creativity. Replications and extensions have sometimes found weaker effects, and there are many kinds of creativity, from solving puzzles to writing a novel.

@char N19 living_room_day | The honest summary | Suggestive, not proven
The fair summary is that there's suggestive evidence that bored, wandering minds can produce more unusual associations, and that undemanding time may help ideas incubate. But boredom is no guarantee of creativity, and a bored person staring at a screen isn't automatically brewing the next great idea.

@char N10 rooftop_dusk | A condition | What you do with it
It may depend on what you do with the boredom. If it pushes you toward a blank page, a sketchbook, a walk or a conversation, it can turn into something. If it pushes you toward a feed that fills every gap, there's less room for the mind to wander. The same itch, answered differently, has different consequences.

@char N09 cabin_fireplace | Sandi Mann's view | Downtime has value
In her 2016 book, The Upside of Downtime, Mann argues that we have squeezed boredom out of our days, and that we may be losing some of the benefits of an unfilled mind. Whether or not every claim in the book holds up, the main point is a reasonable one. A mind that is never allowed to wander has little chance to make surprising connections.

@char N02 living_room_day | A friendly experiment | Try it yourself
There's an easy, gentle experiment you can try yourself. The next time you face a small, dull task, like washing up, folding clothes or a long walk, try doing it without any audio or screen. Notice what your mind does. It might drift to a problem you've been mulling over, and an idea might turn up unexpectedly.

@char N19 rooftop_dusk | A realistic hope | Small and ordinary
Don't expect a flash of genius every time. Most of what turns up will be ordinary, a half-remembered errand, a tune, a plan for dinner. But every so often, something useful floats up, and that's how a lot of creative ideas have always arrived, in the margins of an ordinary day.

@char N10 living_room_day | Back to Mira | A sketch
In the waiting room, Mira finds herself thinking about a bicycle problem that has been bothering her for a week, a rattling noise she couldn't trace. Without trying, she notices that the sound appeared after she changed the bottom bracket. It's not a brilliant discovery, but it is a quiet little click, and it happened because she had nothing else to do.

@char N27 cabin_fireplace | A reminder | That click
That little click is the kind of benefit that downtime might offer. It isn't dramatic. It's a mind with spare room quietly tidying itself. And it might be worth keeping a bit of that room in our lives, instead of covering every empty moment.

@char N19 autumn_overlook | A bridge | Beyond ideas
Creativity is only one of the things boredom might do for us. There's a deeper idea, which is that boredom raises a question about meaning, and that how we answer it can shape a good deal of what we do next.
