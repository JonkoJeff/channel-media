@char A04 attic_rain | The quiet voice | Is it true?
So let's take that quiet voice seriously for a moment. The one that says, this is just who you are. Is it right? Is personality fixed, the way eye colour is fixed? Or is it something softer, something that moves with time and effort? Psychologists have argued about this for more than a century, and the answer turns out to be more interesting than either side expected.

@chapter A13 rooftop_dusk | Set like plaster
Let's begin with one of the most famous sentences ever written about character. It comes from William James, the American philosopher and psychologist, in his huge book The Principles of Psychology, published in 1890.

@quote A10 attic_rain | In most of us, by the age of thirty, the character has set like plaster, and will never soften again. | William James, The Principles of Psychology, 1890
James wrote this in his chapter on habit. He wasn't being gloomy for the sake of it. He was making a point about how much of our lives runs on routines we built when we were young. Once those routines are laid down, he argued, they carry us along, whether we like them or not.

@char A03 cabin_fireplace | A powerful image | Plaster, not clay
It's a powerful image, isn't it? Wet plaster can be shaped. Dry plaster can only be broken. And for most of the twentieth century, a version of this idea was taken seriously by many personality researchers. The thinking went like this. Children and teenagers are still forming. But by early adulthood, the basic shape of a person is done.

@char A12 living_room_day | The big five | A shared language for personality
To test an idea like that, you first need a way to measure personality. Over the twentieth century, researchers settled on a set of five broad traits that show up again and again in questionnaires across many countries. They're often called the Big Five.

@list A13 rooftop_dusk | The Big Five traits | Openness: curiosity and love of new ideas ;; Conscientiousness: organisation and self-discipline ;; Extraversion: sociability and energy around others ;; Agreeableness: warmth and cooperation ;; Neuroticism: how strongly you feel worry and stress
Openness is about curiosity, imagination and a taste for new experiences. Conscientiousness is about being organised, reliable and self-disciplined. Extraversion is about how much energy you get from other people. Agreeableness is warmth and cooperation. And neuroticism, which some researchers prefer to call low emotional stability, is about how strongly you tend to feel worry, sadness and stress.

@char A03 living_room_day | Each one a dial | Not boxes, but ranges
None of these are boxes you're either in or out of. Each one is more like a dial, and most people sit somewhere in the middle. With these five dials, researchers could ask a clear question. If we measure the same people years apart, do the dials stay where they were?

@chapter A15 cafe_sunset | What the long studies found
The best way to answer that is with longitudinal studies, which follow the same people over many years. One study of this kind is useful. Dozens are much better. So in 2006, Brent Roberts, Kate Walton and Wolfgang Viechtbauer gathered them together.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Roberts, Walton & Viechtbauer | Psychological Bulletin, 2006 | Patterns of mean-level change in personality traits across the life course: a meta-analysis of longitudinal studies
They combined the results of ninety-two longitudinal studies, covering people from adolescence into old age. Instead of asking whether one person changed, they asked how the average person changed over the years. If James was right, the lines should go flat after thirty.

@char A12 cafe_sunset | Not flat | Change keeps going
They didn't go flat. People, on average, kept changing well past thirty. And the direction of the change was remarkably consistent. As people moved through adulthood, they tended to become more conscientious, more emotionally stable, and in some ways more socially confident. Agreeableness also tended to rise, especially later in life.

@char A13 rooftop_dusk | The busiest decade | Twenty to forty
Interestingly, the biggest changes didn't happen in adolescence, as many people had assumed. Much of the change happened in young adulthood, roughly between twenty and forty. That's the stretch of life when many people take on new roles, like steady work, long relationships and caring for others.

@char A10 cabin_fireplace | The maturity principle | Growing into steadier versions of ourselves
Researchers have a name for this pattern. They call it the maturity principle. Over time, most people tend to grow into calmer, more responsible and more caring versions of themselves. Not everyone, and not in a straight line, but on average, that's the drift.

@chapter A12 living_room_day | A hundred thousand people
One meta-analysis is strong evidence. But here's another angle on the same question, from a very large sample collected in a very modern way.

@study A04 attic_rain | Srivastava, John, Gosling & Potter | Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 2003 | Development of personality in early and middle adulthood: set like plaster or persistent change?
In 2003, Sanjay Srivastava and his colleagues published a study using personality questionnaires completed online by more than a hundred and thirty-two thousand adults, aged twenty-one to sixty. Notice the title they chose. Set like plaster, or persistent change? They were testing William James directly.

@char A03 living_room_day | Their answer | Persistent change
This was not a longitudinal study. It compared people of different ages at one moment in time, which has limits. But the pattern was clear and it matched the long studies. Conscientiousness and agreeableness kept rising through the twenties, thirties and beyond. Neuroticism tended to fall, especially in women. The plaster, it seemed, was still soft.

@char A13 cabin_fireplace | Two kinds of stability | The rank and the level
Now, there's a subtle point here that's worth slowing down for. Researchers talk about two different kinds of stability. One is about the average level. Do people in general become more conscientious over time? The other is about rank order. Does the most organised person in a group of twenty-year-olds tend to still be among the most organised at fifty?

@char A10 attic_rain | Both can be true | Moving together
And here's the thing. Both can be true at once. Imagine a group of runners all moving forward along a track. Everyone is going somewhere new, but the runner in front often stays near the front. That's roughly what personality looks like in adulthood. The whole group moves, and individual positions shift a little, but slowly.

@photo lf21_b01_01.jpg | A slow drift over many years
So William James was partly right. Habits and routines do carry us. But the idea that character sets solid at thirty, never to soften again, doesn't hold up. Personality keeps moving, slowly, for most of our lives.

And yet, if personality really keeps changing, why does it feel so fixed from the inside? Why do so many of us believe that the person we are today is, more or less, the person we'll always be? That's where we're going next, because psychologists have measured that feeling too. And it turns out to be one of the most reliable illusions they've ever found.
