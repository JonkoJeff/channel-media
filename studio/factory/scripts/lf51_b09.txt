@chapter N10 cabin_fireplace | A signal, not a flaw
Let's ask the most interesting question of all. If boredom feels so bad, and has been with us for so long, why hasn't evolution removed it? Why do we have a feeling that seems to serve no purpose except to make us uncomfortable?

@char N19 living_room_day | A clue | Pain has a purpose
It helps to think about another unpleasant feeling, which is physical pain. Pain hurts, and yet it's one of the most useful things the body does, because it tells us that something is wrong and needs attention. People who can't feel pain are in constant danger. Many researchers now suggest that boredom may work in a similar way, as a mental alarm rather than a bodily one.

@char N09 cafe_sunset | The idea | A regulatory state
In 2013, the researchers Shane Bench and Heather Lench published a review in the journal Behavioral Sciences with the title, On the function of boredom. Their proposal was that boredom is a kind of self-regulatory emotion. It signals that our current activity isn't satisfying, and it motivates us to pursue a different goal.

@char N02 rooftop_dusk | The philosopher's view | The bright side
The philosopher Andreas Elpidorou has made a similar case in several papers, including one in 2014 with the cheerful title, The bright side of boredom, in the journal Frontiers in Psychology. He argues that boredom is a kind of push. It pushes us away from a situation that isn't fulfilling and toward something that might be, and without that push, we might remain stuck.

@char N10 living_room_day | A simple picture | Alarm and compass
You can think of boredom as both an alarm and a compass. The alarm says, this isn't working. The compass says, look for something else. When we ignore the alarm, we're left in a state that's neither useful nor pleasant. When we listen, we move, and the discomfort fades.

@char N19 autumn_overlook | A useful idea | The cost of staying
A more mathematical version of this appeared in 2013, in the journal Behavioral and Brain Sciences. The psychologist Robert Kurzban and his colleagues proposed an opportunity cost model of mental effort. In everyday terms, the brain keeps track of what else you could be doing with your time and attention, and when the current task isn't paying off, it makes staying feel costly.

@char N09 cabin_fireplace | Foraging | Like an animal in a field
Animals do something very similar when they search for food. A bird in a bush full of berries stays and eats, but when the berries run low, it becomes increasingly worth flying to a new bush. In biology, this is a well-known idea, and some researchers think that boredom may be the mind's version of the feeling that it's time to move on.

@study N10 cafe_sunset | Geana, Wilson, Daw and Cohen, 2016 | Princeton University, presented at the Annual Meeting of the Cognitive Science Society | Participants who were made to feel bored by repetitive tasks were more willing to explore new options
A study from Princeton, led by Andra Geana and colleagues in 2016, tested this idea. They made some people bored by showing them a repetitive task, and then gave everyone choices in a game. The bored participants were more likely to explore new options, rather than sticking with the familiar ones.

@char N23 living_room_day | Another study | Bored people seek novelty
That fits with Bench and Lench's 2019 findings, which we met earlier, that bored people seek out novel experiences. And it fits with a more recent theory, published in 2022 by Mayank Agrawal, Marcelo Mattar, Jonathan Cohen and Nathaniel Daw in the journal Psychological Review, that links boredom and mental fatigue to the changing opportunity cost of staying with a task.

@char N10 cabin_fireplace | What it means | Boredom as direction
So what does all this suggest? That boredom isn't a sign that something is wrong with you. It's a sign that the present activity is no longer paying off, and that the system responsible for choosing what to do next has started to look around. It's your mind's way of saying, let's try something else.

@char N19 rooftop_dusk | A careful note | Not a perfect system
It's only fair to point out that the alarm isn't perfect. Boredom can go off when we're doing something that is valuable but slow, like learning an instrument or writing a long report, and responding by quitting every time would be a mistake. Part of wisdom is telling apart a task that's truly pointless from one that's just temporarily dull.

@char N02 living_room_day | A difference | Dull and worthwhile
This is where the meaning part of the MAC model comes in. If you can remind yourself why the dull task matters, the alarm quietens, because the opportunity cost no longer seems as high. That's why a student who loves the subject can sit through a long lecture, and a student who doesn't is checking the time.

@char N09 cafe_sunset | The risk | A noisy alarm
There is also a risk when the alarm is too sensitive. People who are very prone to boredom may find that nothing holds their interest for long. A book from the library, a film, a hobby, even a conversation can start to feel flat, and they may jump from one thing to the next without staying long enough to enjoy any of it.

@char N10 cabin_fireplace | Research on this | Boredom proneness
Studies of boredom proneness, including a short version of the scale published in 2017 by Andriy Struk, Jonathan Carriere, J. Allan Cheyne and James Danckert in the journal Assessment, have linked high proneness with a number of difficulties, though the evidence is correlational. It doesn't tell us that boredom causes these problems, only that they tend to go together.

@char N19 living_room_day | A distinction | Boredom is not a diagnosis
It's important to say that being easily bored is not an illness. It's a feature of how some people respond to monotony. But if a person feels bored and empty almost all the time, for weeks, or if everything seems flat and pointless, it's worth talking to a doctor or a mental health professional. That might be something else, and it deserves proper care.

@char N23 rooftop_dusk | A wider view | Why it still matters
Danckert and Eastwood, in their 2020 book, Out of My Skull, make a thoughtful case that boredom is a signal we should take seriously, neither ignoring it nor numbing it. They argue that it points toward something we want, and it's worth asking what.

@char N09 cabin_fireplace | Back to Mira | What does she want?
Mira, in her plastic chair, asks herself that very question. What does she want right now? She wants to be home, with the lamp on and her bike stand in front of her. She wants to be working on something with her hands. That insight doesn't end the wait, but it does turn the restlessness into a direction.

@char N10 living_room_day | The first step | Listen to it
That may be the best first step with boredom, before anything else. Instead of rushing to silence it, ask what it's pointing to. It might say, I want something new, or I want to do something that matters, or I want company. Often the answer is surprisingly clear.
