@chapter A12 living_room_day | The gentle illusions
Early in this video, we met Shelley Taylor's idea that healthy minds hold mildly positive illusions about the world. That idea was born in a study of people at one of the hardest moments of life: a diagnosis of cancer. It's one of the most moving studies in the psychology of adjustment, and it adds something important to our picture of how people rebuild after a shock.

@char A04 attic_rain | The study | Seventy-eight women
In the early 1980s, Taylor and her colleagues at the University of California, Los Angeles, interviewed seventy-eight women who had been treated for breast cancer. They wanted to understand how people live with a threat that may or may not return. They expected to find people struggling with fear and gloom. What they found was more varied, and more surprising.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Taylor | American Psychologist, 1983 | Adjustment to threatening events: a theory of cognitive adaptation
Taylor proposed that people adjust to a threat through three linked kinds of thinking. A search for meaning, an effort to understand why it happened and what it means. An attempt to regain mastery, a feeling of having some control over the event or one's life. And an effort to restore self-esteem, through self-enhancing thoughts, often by comparing oneself with others who are worse off.

@list A03 living_room_day | Three themes of adjustment | Meaning: what does this mean for my life? ;; Mastery: what can I do to keep it from happening again? ;; Self-enhancement: I'm coping better than most
These three themes echo the ones we have seen throughout. Meaning is the search we talked about in the meaning chapter. Mastery is the belief that we can influence what happens, which was one of the shattered assumptions. And self-enhancement is the quiet inner voice that says, I am still worth something.

@chapter A13 rooftop_dusk | Beliefs that weren't true
Here's what made the study so interesting. Many of the women held beliefs about control that couldn't be literally true. Some thought that their own thoughts or habits had caused the cancer, or could prevent its return. Others believed that a particular diet, a mood or a routine would protect them. And many believed their prospects were good, even when the facts were uncertain.

@char A12 living_room_day | The surprise | Illusions that helped
These beliefs didn't match the evidence, and yet the women who held them often seemed to be coping well. Taylor suggested that these were not signs of denial, but of adaptive illusions, optimistic beliefs that helped people to take their treatment, to keep up their relationships and to go on with life. They weren't harmless to everyone, and they weren't a general rule. But in this situation, they often served people well.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Taylor, Lichtman & Wood | Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 1984 | Attributions, beliefs about control, and adjustment to breast cancer
In a follow-up study of seventy-eight women, the team found that those who believed that they had some control over the course of their illness showed better adjustment. This held even when the beliefs were not accurate. What mattered for their wellbeing was the feeling of mastery, whether or not they could prove it.

@char A04 attic_rain | A careful note | Not all control beliefs are good
This doesn't mean that all illusions are good for us. Later research has shown that blaming oneself for a loss can be harmful, particularly when it's unwarranted. A belief such as, I could have prevented this, can feel like control, but it often leads to guilt and distress. The helpful kind of control belief looks forward, such as, there are things I can do now, and not backward, such as, it was my fault.

@list A12 living_room_day | Helpful and unhelpful control beliefs | Helpful: there are things I can do now ;; Helpful: I can influence how I handle this ;; Unhelpful: I should have prevented it ;; Unhelpful: it happened because I was bad
It's an important distinction. Believing that you have some control over the future can be a source of strength. Believing that you're to blame for the past is much more likely to be a source of pain. If you notice yourself blaming yourself for something that was out of your hands, it's worth talking to someone, since that kind of thinking rarely helps and often hurts.

@chapter A06 cafe_sunset | Comparing downward
One of the three themes, self-enhancement, deserves another look. Many of the women compared themselves with others who were doing worse, and found comfort in doing so. This is called downward comparison. It sounds a little unkind, but it appears to be a common and useful way to restore a sense of worth when something has knocked it.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Wills | Psychological Bulletin, 1981 | Downward comparison principles in social psychology
Thomas Wills proposed that when people feel threatened, they often compare themselves with others who are worse off, in order to feel better. He suggested that this is a normal way of coping, and that it can be quite gentle. In practice, it usually means something like, at least I can still do such and such, or, others have it harder.

@char A01 cafe_sunset | A reminder | Use comparison with care
Downward comparison can help in the short run. But it can also lead to guilt or to a feeling of having no right to be upset. If you find yourself thinking, other people have it worse, so I shouldn't complain, that's a signal to be kinder to yourself. Your pain doesn't have to win a contest to count.

@list A03 living_room_day | Words to keep from this chapter | Cognitive adaptation ;; Mastery ;; Self-enhancement ;; Downward comparison
Four terms from this chapter. Cognitive adaptation is the mind's way of adjusting to threat. Mastery is the sense that we have some control over what happens. Self-enhancement is the effort to keep a positive view of ourselves. And downward comparison is looking at people who are worse off in order to feel better. Picture this scene. A woman waiting for test results tells her friend that she is sure she will be fine, and that she has been eating well and sleeping early. Her friend, a nurse, knows that these habits do not decide the outcome, but she does not correct her. She simply says that those sound like good things to be doing. Later, whatever the results, the woman will not have been harmed by her small, hopeful routine. It gave her something to hold. The research suggests that her friend chose wisely: to leave a harmless hope in place, and to be there when it is tested.

@list A12 living_room_day | Things you can still control | How you spend today ;; Whom you ask for help ;; What you say to yourself ;; One small thing you finish before bed
Here's a small exercise inspired by this research. Take a sheet of paper and list the things you can't control in your situation: the past, what other people choose, what the future will bring. Then make a second list, with the things you can still influence. How you spend today. Whom you ask for help. What you say to yourself when it gets late. One small thing that you finish before bed. The first list may be long, and that's okay. The point of the second list is not to deny the first. It's to remind you that, even after a shock, you are not powerless, and that a small amount of control, well placed, can carry a surprising amount of weight.

A ship's wheel on a quiet sea. You can't control the weather. But you can still hold the wheel. That small sense of steering, the research suggests, can be one of the most protective feelings of all.

@char A15 cabin_fireplace | A gentle thought | Hope is allowed
There's a quiet permission in Taylor's work. It says that hope, even imperfect hope, can be healthy. You don't have to be certain, and you don't have to be right about the future. A mild optimism is part of how minds survive. If you can find a little of it, hold on to it. It's doing its job.

@char A08 cabin_fireplace | Looking back | What the gentle illusions taught us
So what did we learn? That women facing cancer adjusted through meaning, mastery and self-enhancement. That mildly positive beliefs about control often helped. That blaming yourself for the past tends to hurt. And that comparison with others can comfort, but should be used with care.
