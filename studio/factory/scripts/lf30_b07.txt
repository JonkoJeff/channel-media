@chapter A02 autumn_overlook | When the world changes
The gorilla was something new walking into a scene. But here's a slightly different belief, and it feels even safer. If something you were looking at changed, you would notice the change. A wall turning from blue to green. A building vanishing from a photo. A stranger turning into a different stranger.

@char A13 living_room_day | It feels obvious | We'd spot the difference
After all, spotting differences is something we enjoy. Children's puzzle books are full of two nearly identical pictures, and we happily hunt for the seven small changes. But those puzzles give us a big advantage. Both pictures sit side by side, and we can look back and forth as long as we like. What happens when the change happens in time instead, with a tiny interruption in between?

@study A14 cafe_sunset | Ronald Rensink, Kevin O'Regan & James Clark | Psychological Science, published 1997 | A photo and an edited version alternated with a brief blank between them; people often took many seconds to spot even a large change
In 1997, Ronald Rensink, Kevin O'Regan and James Clark published a study in the journal Psychological Science called To see or not to see. They showed people a photograph, then a brief blank grey screen, then an edited version of the same photograph, then the blank again, round and round.

@list A03 cafe_sunset | The flicker method | Original picture ;; A brief blank ;; Edited picture ;; A brief blank, and repeat
That's why it's called the flicker method. Original, blank, edited, blank, over and over. The change might be large. An engine missing from the wing of a plane, for example. Something you would spot instantly if the two pictures were side by side.

@char A04 attic_rain | The surprise | So slow to see it
But with that brief blank in between, people often took many seconds, sometimes far longer, to find the change. They would stare at the flickering scene, sure that nothing was different, until suddenly they saw it. And once they had seen it, it was impossible to miss. Many laughed, embarrassed that it had taken so long.

@char A19 cabin_fireplace | Why the blank matters | Hiding the motion
The blank is the key. Normally, when something changes, it creates a little flicker of motion or a sudden flash, and that grabs your attention automatically. The blank screen hides that signal. Without it, your brain has to compare what's there now with what was there before. And it turns out that the brain keeps a surprisingly small record of what was there before.

@curve A10 living_room_day | Spotting a change over time | seconds of flicker | chance of having spotted it | 0.85 | Slow at first, then suddenly obvious
If you plotted it, the pattern often looks a bit like this curve. For a while, nothing. Your eyes wander across the scene, and the change hides in plain view. Then your attention happens to land on the right spot at the right moment, and the change leaps out. The difficulty isn't in seeing. It's in knowing where to look.

@chapter A16 street_day | A stranger who changed
Pictures on a screen are one thing. But surely, in real life, face to face with a real person, things would be different. Two researchers decided to test that in the most charming way.

@study A11 street_day | Daniel Simons & Daniel Levin | Psychonomic Bulletin & Review, published 1998 | A researcher asked a pedestrian for directions; two people carrying a door passed between them, and the researcher was swapped for someone else
In 1998, Daniel Simons and Daniel Levin published a study in the journal Psychonomic Bulletin and Review. The setting was a university campus, out in the open. A researcher, holding a map, walked up to a passer-by and asked for directions.

@list A15 autumn_overlook | How the swap worked | A stranger asks for directions ;; Two workers carry a door between them ;; The first stranger walks off behind the door ;; A different person takes their place
While the passer-by was explaining the way, two men carrying a large wooden door walked right between them. For a moment, the door blocked the view. In that moment, the researcher asking for directions grabbed the back of the door and walked away with it, and a different researcher, who had been carrying it, stepped into their place and carried on the conversation.

@char A06 rooftop_dusk | The question | Would you notice?
The two people looked different. Different heights, different builds, different voices, different clothes. The passer-by had been talking with the first one, face to face, for several seconds. So, when the door passed and a different person was suddenly standing there, did they notice?

@stat A12 living_room_day | Noticed the swap | 7 of 15 | people asked for directions (Simons & Levin, 1998)
Only seven of the fifteen people noticed. Fewer than half. The rest simply carried on giving directions to a completely different person. When the researchers asked afterwards whether they'd noticed anything unusual, many said no, and were astonished when told what had happened.

It's a gentle reminder of something we rarely think about. When you're focused on a task, like explaining the way to the library, you may not be storing much about the person in front of you. You see a stranger, a person asking directions, and that may be most of what your brain keeps.

@chapter A05 cabin_fireplace | The world as its own memory
So why does this happen? Why doesn't the brain keep a detailed copy of everything we see?

@study A18 cafe_sunset | Kevin O'Regan | published 1992, Canadian Journal of Psychology | Proposed that the brain doesn't store a detailed picture of the world, because the world itself is always there to look at again
In 1992, the psychologist Kevin O'Regan published a paper with a beautiful idea in its title. He described the world as an outside memory. The brain, he suggested, doesn't need to keep a detailed internal copy of the scene, because the scene itself is right there. Whenever you need a detail, you can simply look again.

@char A20 living_room_day | Why it makes sense | Saving effort
When you think about it, this is a clever design. Storing every detail of every moment would take enormous effort, and most of it would never be used. Why memorise the exact pattern of the curtains when the curtains will still be there in a second? So the brain keeps the gist, the meaning, and a few details it cares about, and trusts the world to hold the rest.

@char A13 attic_rain | The catch | When the world cheats
The catch is that this design depends on the world being honest. Usually it is. Curtains don't change colour while you blink. Strangers don't swap places behind doors. But when the world does something sneaky, the brain has no copy to compare against, and the change slips through.

@list A03 autumn_overlook | Change blindness in daily life | Film errors few viewers spot ;; A friend's new haircut you missed ;; A sign that changed on your daily route ;; Small changes during a blink
And this happens in everyday life more than we think. Films are full of small continuity errors, a glass that's suddenly full again, a scarf that changes sides between shots, and most viewers never see them. Maybe a friend has had a new haircut, and it took you a whole evening to notice. The world changes, and much of the time, we just carry on.

#ai lf30_b07_02.jpg | Editorial still-life of a half-full glass of water and a folded scarf on a wooden cafe table, warm afternoon window light, cozy calm mood, no people, no text, no logos

@photo lf30_b07_02.jpg | Was the glass always full?
Film editors know this well. They even have people whose whole job is checking that the glass on the table stays at the same level from shot to shot. Still, errors slip through, because most of us are watching the story, not the glass.

@char A19 cabin_fireplace | It feels complete | The great illusion
And here, once again, is the most surprising part. Even though we store so little, it doesn't feel that way. Look around the room you're in. It feels fully present, richly detailed, all of it seen at once. That feeling of completeness is itself something the brain creates. It's less a photograph and more a promise: if you look, the detail will be there.

@list A12 cafe_sunset | What we've learned so far | Eyes see sharply in a small patch ;; Attention chooses what you experience ;; Little is stored from moment to moment ;; The world holds the details for you
So let's put the pieces together. Your eyes see sharply only in a small patch. Attention selects what reaches your experience. From moment to moment, you store only a little. And the world itself holds most of the details, waiting for you to look again.

@char A10 rooftop_dusk | Another belief | Separate senses
So far, we've been talking almost entirely about seeing. But we have other senses, too. Hearing, touch, taste, smell. And most of us assume each of them works on its own, like separate channels on a radio. Your ears hear, and your eyes see. But what if your eyes could change what you hear?
