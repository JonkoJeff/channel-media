@chapter A02 autumn_overlook | What fills a dream
If only a little of a dream comes from the room around you, then most of it must come from somewhere else. So what is it made of? You might guess that dreams are full of flying, falling and fantastic creatures. The answer, when researchers actually counted, turned out to be a good deal quieter, and in some ways more interesting.

@char A12 living_room_day | Counting dreams | A strange idea
Counting a dream sounds almost rude, like measuring a poem with a ruler. But it is the only way to find out what dreams are really like, and not what we remember as the most dramatic ones. We tend to retell the odd nights. A fair picture needs the ordinary nights as well, the thousands of them, written down and sorted.

@study A03 cafe_sunset | Hall & Van de Castle | The Content Analysis of Dreams, Appleton-Century-Crofts, 1966 | A scoring system for dream reports
Calvin Hall began collecting dream reports in the 1940s, at Case Western Reserve University in Cleveland, and he gathered thousands of them. With Robert Van de Castle, he turned them into a scoring system, published as a book in 1966. Treat each report like a short story, they said, and count what is in it, in the same way for every dreamer.

@list A10 attic_rain | What the system counts | Characters ;; Friendly and aggressive acts ;; Emotions ;; Settings ;; Good fortune and misfortune
Notice how plain the categories are. Who is in the dream? What do they do to each other? How does the dreamer feel? Where is it set? What goes right, and what goes wrong? There is no hidden symbol to decode. The system simply asks what a dream says on its surface, and it turns out that the surface has a lot to say.

@char A13 rooftop_dusk | The norms | Hundreds of ordinary dreams
The best known figures from the system are called norms. They come from 500 dreams from 100 men and 500 from 100 women, collected from college students in the late 1940s and early 1950s. Later researchers repeated the work in other places and decades, including the University of Richmond in 1979, with 263 dreams from 53 men and 340 from 69 women, and found much the same patterns.

@stat A03 cafe_sunset | Negative emotions among all emotions in dream reports | 80% | in the Hall and Van de Castle norms
Here is the first surprise. In those norms, about 80 percent of all the emotions people reported in their dreams were negative. Fear, worry, anger and sadness far outnumbered joy. And this is not only an American pattern. Studies in many countries, and in small societies without writing, showed more aggression than friendliness, more misfortune than good fortune, and more negative than positive feeling.

@char A19 attic_rain | The dream's tilt | Worry is the default
It's tempting to read this as a sign that something is wrong, but it isn't. It seems to be how dreaming tends to work, in people everywhere. Dreams lean toward trouble: a missed train, an unprepared test, a door that will not open. If you have woken up unsettled, you are in very large company. Most of us are visited by uneasy dreams far more often than by delightful ones.

@bars A03 attic_rain | Aggressive share of aggressive and friendly acts | percent | Men:59 ;; Women:51
The system also compares unkind acts with kind ones. Of all the aggressive and friendly interactions coded in the norms, 59 percent in men's dreams and 51 percent in women's were aggressive. So kindness appears in dreams, but unkindness appears a little more often. The scale tips toward trouble, in both sets of dreams, though not by a great deal.

@list A12 living_room_day | Dream reports with at least one of these | Aggression: about 45% ;; Misfortune: about a third ;; Negative element of any kind: nearly 80%
The numbers make the same point. About 47 percent of men's dream reports and 44 percent of women's contained at least one act of aggression. Around a third contained a misfortune, such as an accident or a loss. And once you count aggression, misfortune, failure and negative emotion together, roughly four in five dreams had at least one of them.

@chapter A01 rooftop_dusk | The dream is where you live
Now for the second surprise, and it's the one that I find most touching. The negative tilt of dreams is real, but the stage on which they happen is not exotic at all. It's usually somewhere you know.

@bars A03 living_room_day | Settings that were familiar | percent | Men:62 ;; Women:79
In the norms, about 62 percent of the settings in men's dreams were familiar places, and about 79 percent in women's. Home, school, a street you know, a room you have stood in a hundred times. The fantastic landscapes we tend to retell are the exception. Most dreams take place on ordinary ground.

@char A05 cabin_fireplace | Indoors, among the familiar | Everyday ground
Indoor settings were common too, about 48 percent of the coded settings for men and 61 percent for women. And the people in dreams are often people you know. Roughly 45 percent of the characters in men's dreams, and 58 percent in women's, were familiar. Animals made up only a small share, about 6 percent for men and 4 for women, in spite of how often they are retold.

@photo lf28_b08_01.jpg | An ordinary room
This could be the setting for many a dream. A table, two cups, the light coming in from a window. Nothing about it is strange, and that's rather the point. When the dreaming brain looks for a place to stage its story, it reaches for the places it knows best, the ones stored most deeply, and it fills them with the people you see most.

@char A15 autumn_overlook | Who is in the room | The cast
The cast is more varied than you might expect. In the norms, the dreams of men contained about 67 percent male characters, while the dreams of women were closer to an even split, 48 percent male and 52 percent female. In other words, your dream cast tends to resemble the cast of your life. The people who matter most to you, or who worry you most, have the leading parts.

@char A12 living_room_day | Friends in the cast | The company you keep
Friends are a large part of that cast. About 31 percent of the characters in men's dreams and 37 percent in women's were coded as friends. That is the company you keep in daily life, turning up on the dream stage. They may be friendly or difficult, helpful or absent, but they are rarely strangers, and they are rarely monsters.

@char A02 cabin_fireplace | A victim, not a villain | Who gets hurt
Another detail from the norms is quietly telling. The dreamer was involved in about 80 percent of the aggressions in the dream reports, and more often as the target than as the one doing the harm. About 60 percent of the time for men, and 67 percent for women, the dreamer was the victim.

@list A03 attic_rain | The typical bad dream | Being chased ;; Being blamed ;; Being left out ;; Being caught unprepared
So the typical bad dream is not a night of violence. It is a small, human worry, played out in a familiar place. Nothing grand, nothing mysterious, and nothing that needs a symbol to explain it. Just the ordinary fears of a person who has things to do and people to care about.

@chapter A10 rooftop_dusk | The continuity hypothesis
All of this leads to an idea that psychologist G. William Domhoff has argued for many years. It's called the continuity hypothesis, and it is very simple. Dreams are not a separate world. They are a continuation of the concerns that fill your waking life.

@char A13 cabin_fireplace | Continuity | What you think about is what you dream about
The idea is that the things you care about, worry about and think about by day are the things that appear at night. A person who is anxious about work dreams of work. A person who is missing someone dreams of that person. That sounds almost too obvious to count as a theory, but it makes a testable prediction: different lives should produce different dreams, in patterns that we could measure.

@study A03 cafe_sunset | Domhoff | Perchance to Dream: The Frontiers of Dream Psychology, Nova Science Publishers, 2009 | Gender Differences in Dreams
Domhoff tested exactly that, using the same scoring system. The gender differences in the norms, such as the mix of male and female characters, and the share of indoor settings, fit with differences in daily life. And when later studies of women's dreams were collected, including 500 dreams from 100 women in the late 1980s, they found very few differences from the original norms.

@char A10 cabin_fireplace | It held up | Repeated in other places
The reassuring part is that the basic findings were checked again and again. The original sample was replicated at the University of Richmond in 1979, at the University of California, Berkeley in 1986, and at Salem College in 1987. The details shifted a little from group to group, but the shape stayed. More aggression than friendliness, more misfortune than good fortune, and more negative than positive feeling.

@list A12 living_room_day | What the continuity idea predicts | Dreams reflect current concerns ;; Familiar people and places dominate ;; Different lives, different dreams ;; Patterns stay stable over decades
Four predictions, then, and the dream reports broadly agree. The stability is the remarkable part. The patterns hold for students in the 1940s and for students decades later. The surface of a dream, it seems, reflects something steady about being a person, as much as it reflects the particular day you have had.

@char A18 attic_rain | A fair caution | Reports are not dreams
We should add a few honest limits. These figures come from dream reports, which are memories of dreams told afterwards, and memory is selective. Many of the samples were college students in a few places. And the continuity idea describes a broad pattern; it doesn't promise that any single dream will mirror your day. Plenty of dreams contain something you have never thought about at all.

@char A19 rooftop_dusk | If a dream unsettles you | Nothing to decode
If a dream leaves you uneasy, the research offers a gentle comfort. Worry and misfortune are normal ingredients of dreaming, not a message that something is wrong with you. You are not required to decode them. And if bad dreams are frequent and truly distressing, that is a good reason to talk with someone you trust.

@char A05 living_room_day | The surprising ordinariness | Most nights are small
Perhaps the most striking thing about all this counting is how ordinary it makes dreams look. The famous strange dreams do happen, and we will meet the machinery behind them soon. But a typical dream is closer to a quiet, slightly worried scene in a familiar place, with people you know. It's the kind of thing you might forget by breakfast.

@list A13 living_room_day | A quiet exercise for tomorrow morning | Where was the dream set? ;; Who was in it? ;; How did you feel? ;; Was it a place from your life?
If you are curious, you can try the Hall and Van de Castle way of looking at your own last dream. Ask where it was set, who was there, and how you felt. Then ask whether any of it came from your recent days. You may find the setting is a room you know, the cast is people you see, and the feeling is a worry you carried to bed. It's a gentle way of seeing the norms in your own sleep.

@list A03 cabin_fireplace | Things to take with you | Dreams lean toward worry and misfortune ;; Settings and people are usually familiar ;; Waking concerns seep into dreams ;; The norms come from reports, not from dreams themselves
Let's gather those threads. Dreams lean toward worry. Their settings and people are usually familiar. And the concerns of the day tend to follow us into the night. None of this says what dreams are for, or why the brain bothers. That is the next question, and scientists have offered some very different answers.

@char A15 autumn_overlook | And so, the question | Why do we dream at all
We now know a good deal about what a dream looks like from the inside. But the harder question is why we do it. Why does the brain spend so many hours of the night building scenes that we mostly forget by morning? There are two famous answers, and they pull in opposite directions.
