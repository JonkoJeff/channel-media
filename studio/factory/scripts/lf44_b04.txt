@chapter A19 cafe_sunset | What embarrassment actually is
Let's look at the feeling that came with Ivy's memory. Embarrassment is one of those emotions everyone knows and almost no one can define. We know it when it hits. The heat in the cheeks, the urge to look away, the sudden wish to vanish. But what is it actually about? And why does it need other people to exist?

@char A12 living_room_day | A social emotion | It needs an audience
Here's the first clue. You can be frightened alone in a forest. You can be sad alone in a room. But it's quite hard to be embarrassed if no one, real or imagined, could possibly see you. Embarrassment is about how we appear to others. It's a feeling that lives in the space between us and other people.

@char A03 attic_rain | Even imagined eyes | The audience in our head
And notice the word imagined. Ivy is completely alone in her flat. Nobody can see her. And yet she's embarrassed, because in her memory, the lecture hall is still full. The audience is in her head now, frozen at the exact moment she lowered her hand. The feeling doesn't need the people to be present. It only needs us to picture them watching.

@quote A13 rooftop_dusk | Embarrassment lives in the space between us and other people. | A thought to keep
That's a good way to hold it. Embarrassment lives in the space between us and other people. It's not a sign that something is wrong inside you. It's a sign that you care about that space, about how you come across, about being part of the group. And, as we'll see later tonight, caring about that space is one of the things that makes people trust us.

@char A15 cafe_sunset | Everyone knows it | Across lives and places
It's also one of the most shared feelings there is. Ask almost anyone for an embarrassing story and, after a small groan, they'll have one ready. Often several. It's one of the few topics where people compete to tell the worst one, laughing as they go. That alone tells us something. A feeling we can laugh about together is rarely as terrible as it feels alone.

@chapter A10 cabin_fireplace | A family of feelings
Psychologists put embarrassment into a small family of emotions they call self-conscious emotions.

@list A18 living_room_day | The self-conscious emotions | Embarrassment ;; Shame ;; Guilt ;; Pride
They're called self-conscious because they all involve looking at ourselves, often through other people's eyes. The family includes embarrassment, shame, guilt, and on the brighter side, pride. They all need a sense of self, and a sense that others might be judging us. That's part of why young children only start showing some of them as they grow older.

@study A03 cafe_sunset | June Tangney and colleagues | United States | Compared people's own stories of embarrassment, shame and guilt
In 1996, June Tangney and her colleagues published a study in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology. They asked people to describe real experiences of embarrassment, shame and guilt from their own lives, and then compared them. Were these really three separate feelings, or just one feeling with different names?

@list A19 rooftop_dusk | What embarrassment tended to be | Sudden and surprising ;; About lighter, smaller slips ;; More often in front of others ;; More often funny later
They turned out to be different in telling ways. Compared with the other two, embarrassment tended to come from sudden, surprising moments. It tended to be about lighter, smaller slips, rather than serious wrongs. It more often happened in front of other people. And it was more often linked with humour, the kind of story people can later laugh about.

@char A12 living_room_day | That's Ivy's wave | Small, sudden, public
Look at Ivy's wave with that in mind. It was sudden. It was small. It happened in front of people. And, if she's honest, it's the kind of story that's actually quite funny when told over dinner. That's what makes it embarrassment, rather than something heavier. It was never about doing anything wrong. It was simply a little slip in front of an audience.

@char A15 autumn_overlook | A lighter feeling | Even when it burns
That's an important difference, especially at night. Embarrassment can burn very hot, but it's usually about something light. It says, that looked a bit silly, not, I am a bad person. When the night makes an embarrassing memory feel enormous, it can help to remember which feeling it actually is. This one belongs to the lighter end of the family.

@char A13 bedroom_night | Night changes the size | Not the feeling's type
The night doesn't change what kind of feeling it is. It changes the size. In the middle of the afternoon, Ivy might tell the lecture hall story as a funny anecdote. At one in the morning, the same story feels like a small catastrophe. Same memory, same facts, different size. Keeping that in mind can be a quiet comfort.

@chapter A13 bedroom_night | Predicaments
Another psychologist who spent years studying embarrassment is Rowland Miller, who wrote a whole book about it.

@study A10 attic_rain | Rowland Miller | United States | Book: Embarrassment, Poise and Peril in Everyday Life, 1996
His book, published in 1996, is called Embarrassment: Poise and Peril in Everyday Life. Miller describes embarrassment as the flustered, awkward feeling that comes when something happens that makes us look different from how we'd like to be seen, in front of others. A trip, a slip of the tongue, a misunderstanding, a moment of being noticed when we didn't want to be.

@list A18 cafe_sunset | Everyday predicaments | Physical slips, like tripping ;; Social slips, like a wrong name ;; Being suddenly noticed ;; Watching someone else's slip
In everyday life, embarrassment tends to come from a few kinds of small predicaments. Physical slips, like tripping on a step or spilling a drink. Social slips, like using the wrong name or waving at someone who wasn't waving at you. Being suddenly noticed, like being sung to on your birthday. And sometimes, simply watching someone else's slip, which we'll come back to.

@char A12 living_room_day | Both of Ivy's moments | Two classic predicaments
Ivy's evening has two of the most classic predicaments in the book. The wave meant for someone else, and the thank you, you too, to a waiter. Both are tiny misreadings of a social script. The world handed her a line, and she answered it a fraction too quickly. Nearly everyone has done one or both of these. Many of us have done them more than once.

@quote A19 rooftop_dusk | A tiny misreading of a social script. | What most embarrassing moments are
That's what most embarrassing moments are. A tiny misreading of a social script. We live inside thousands of small scripts every day. Greetings, goodbyes, thank yous, how are yous. We run them automatically, without thinking, and that's what makes them so efficient. Every now and then, the automatic part fires a little too early, and out comes, you too.

@char A15 cafe_sunset | Automatic is good | Mostly
And that tells us something kind about these slips. They happen because the automatic system works well, not because it's broken. If Ivy had to think carefully about every greeting and every thank you, she'd be exhausted by lunchtime. Running on autopilot is how the brain saves effort. The occasional misfire is the small price of a system that, the rest of the time, runs beautifully.

@quote A10 attic_rain | The occasional misfire is the price of a system that mostly runs beautifully. | A kinder view of slips
The occasional misfire is the price of a system that mostly runs beautifully. Think of how many thank yous Ivy says in a week at the library desk. Hundreds, probably, all perfectly timed. One, you too, at dinner doesn't undo any of them. It just stands out because it's the odd one.

@chapter A03 cabin_fireplace | Why the feeling exists at all
So we know what embarrassment is. A light, sudden, social feeling about how we appeared to others.

@char A13 living_room_day | A bigger question | Why have it at all?
But that leaves a bigger question. Why would the brain bother with such a feeling at all? Why make a moment in a lecture hall burn so hot that it still glows ten years later? Feelings that stay with us through evolution usually do so because they were useful. So what could be useful about a face going red in the dark?

@char A10 attic_rain | A clue in the heat | It points to people
The clue is in what the feeling is about. It's about other people. About fitting in, being accepted, staying part of the group. For most of human history, being part of a group wasn't just pleasant. It was how people survived. So a feeling that pays close attention to how we come across might have been far more important than it looks.

@char A19 cafe_sunset | A guess worth testing | Researchers have looked
That's a guess, of course, and good scientists don't just leave guesses lying around. They test them. And over the past few decades, researchers have looked closely at what embarrassment does, what the brain does with social mistakes, and why bad moments seem to stick. What they found is, in many ways, quite kind to people like Ivy.

Let's pause and gather what we have. Memories arrive on their own, called up by small cues. The night gives them room to be noticed. Embarrassment is a social feeling about how we appear to others. It's usually about lighter, smaller slips. And many of those slips come from the brain's handy autopilot, misfiring just a little.
