@chapter A13 cabin_fireplace | When a memory feels certain
Earlier tonight, I promised to come back to a puzzle. If memory is rebuilt, and can change, why do some memories feel as solid as stone? Why can you see them so clearly, sure of every detail, and still be wrong? Let's look at the strongest case there is, the moments you were sure you would never forget.

@study A15 autumn_overlook | Roger Brown & James Kulik | Published in Cognition, 1977 | Asked people where they were when they heard about a shocking public event
In 1977, Roger Brown and James Kulik coined the term flashbulb memory. They asked people about the moment they heard that President Kennedy had been shot, in 1963. Almost everyone could say where they were, who told them, and what they were doing. The memories felt like photographs, taken in a flash, and kept for good.

@char A02 cafe_sunset | A natural belief | Big moments, big memories
It seems natural. Something enormous happens, your emotions are running high, and the moment is stamped into your mind. We feel sure that it's more accurate than an ordinary memory. But there was a catch, and it took a tragedy to find it.

@study A20 rooftop_dusk | Ulric Neisser & Nicole Harsch | Published in 1992 | Asked students to write down how they heard about the Challenger explosion, and again two and a half years later
The morning after the space shuttle Challenger exploded in January 1986, the psychologist Ulric Neisser asked students in his class to write down exactly how they had heard the news. Two and a half years later, he and Nicole Harsch asked them to describe it again. About a quarter of the accounts were wrong on nearly every point, and almost none were entirely accurate.

@char A12 living_room_day | The confidence | Still sky high
And yet, the students were confident. One student, shown their own handwriting from the day after, said, that's my handwriting, but that's not what happened. The new memory was so vivid that it overruled the written record. How sure someone felt told very little about how right they were.

@study A05 rooftop_dusk | Jennifer Talarico & David Rubin | Published in Psychological Science, 2003 | Compared memories of the September 11 attacks with everyday memories, over several months
In 2003, Jennifer Talarico and David Rubin ran a similar test, with the attacks of September 11, 2001. The day after, students described how they had heard the news, and also described an ordinary event from that week. Weeks and months later, they were tested again. The accuracy of both kinds of memory dropped at about the same rate.

@char A15 autumn_overlook | The difference | Vivid, not accurate
What was different was how it felt. The memories of September 11 stayed vivid, and people remained confident in them, while confidence in the everyday memories faded along with their accuracy. A flashbulb memory isn't more accurate. It just feels more accurate. Their paper was named, Confidence, not consistency, characterizes flashbulb memories.

@char A19 cabin_fireplace | Not a failure of character | A feature of the system
This isn't carelessness, or a lack of honesty. Emotion tends to give the central fact a firm hold, that something terrible happened. It also gives the whole memory a feeling of clarity. But the surrounding details, where you were, who told you, are rebuilt like any other, and they drift, while the feeling of certainty stays.

@chapter A03 living_room_day | How we judge what's real
So how do we decide that a memory is true? Mostly, we don't decide at all. We feel.

@study A12 cafe_sunset | Marcia Johnson and Carol Raye | Published in Psychological Review, 1981 | Proposed that we judge whether a memory is real partly by how vivid and detailed it feels
In 1981, Marcia Johnson and Carol Raye proposed what they called reality monitoring. When something comes to mind, we use clues to decide whether it was a real event or something we imagined. Is it vivid? Is it detailed? Does it have sounds and smells? Imagined things tend to be fainter. Usually that's a pretty good guide. But it can be fooled.

@study A01 autumn_overlook | Maryanne Garry, Charles Manning, Elizabeth Loftus & Steven Sherman | Published in 1996 | Asked people to imagine childhood events, then rated how likely they were to have happened
In 1996, Maryanne Garry and colleagues asked people how likely it was that certain childhood events had happened, such as breaking a window with a hand. Some were then asked to imagine one of those events in detail. Later, they rated that event as more likely to have happened. The imagining made it feel more real. It's called imagination inflation.

It's a little like a crystal ball. The more clearly you can see in it, the more sure you feel about what it shows. But the clarity belongs to the glass, not necessarily to the past. Your mind can polish the picture until it gleams.

@chapter A19 cabin_fireplace | Witnesses, and certainty
This matters most in the courtroom, where a confident witness can decide a person's fate.

@study A10 rooftop_dusk | Gary Wells & Amy Bradfield | Published in 1998 | Told witnesses they had picked the right person, after they had chosen from a lineup
In 1998, Gary Wells and Amy Bradfield showed volunteers a video of a crime, and asked them to pick the culprit from a set of photos. The culprit wasn't in the set. Afterwards, some were told, good, you identified the suspect. Those people became more confident, and said they'd had a better view, than people who got no comment. Confidence can be inflated by feedback.

@study A02 living_room_day | John Wixted & Gary Wells | Published in Psychological Science in the Public Interest, 2017 | Reviewed the evidence on how confidence relates to accuracy for eyewitness identifications
But the picture isn't all dark. In 2017, John Wixted and Gary Wells reviewed decades of research, and reached a more balanced view. When a witness makes a first identification, under fair conditions, with no hints, high confidence can be a good sign of accuracy. It's confidence that grows later, after feedback and repeated retelling, that can mislead.

@char A05 cafe_sunset | The thing to take away | When, and how
So what should we do with this? A useful rule of thumb is this. A memory is most trustworthy when it is recorded early, comes from a clean first attempt, and has not been retold and discussed many times. And a feeling of vividness is not a guarantee. It just tells you how well your mind has painted the scene.

@list A15 rooftop_dusk | A kinder way to hold your memories | Treat vivid memories as testimony, not video ;; Check important details against records ;; Be gentle when two people remember it differently ;; Write down key things early
So here's a gentler way to hold your memories. Treat vivid memories as testimony, not video. Check important details against records, if there are any. Be gentle when two people remember the same event differently, because both may be sincere. And if something really matters, write it down early.

@chapter A12 living_room_day | Where the eyes go
Confidence isn't the only thing that shapes what we remember about an event. What we looked at while it happened matters too.

@study A02 rooftop_dusk | Elizabeth Loftus, Geoffrey Loftus & Jane Messo | Published in Law and Human Behavior, 1987 | Showed people a scene where a person held either a gun or a cheque, and tracked their eyes
In 1987, Elizabeth Loftus, Geoffrey Loftus and Jane Messo showed volunteers a series of slides in which a person at a restaurant counter held either a gun or a cheque. People's eyes went to the gun more often, and for longer. Afterwards, they were worse at remembering what the person looked like. The weapon had drawn attention away.

@char A15 autumn_overlook | Weapon focus | Attention is limited
It's a vivid example of the first stop on our journey. What you pay attention to is what you remember, and a threat tends to take all the attention. A person who remembers a frightening moment very clearly may remember the frightening thing clearly, and the face behind it poorly.

@char A19 cabin_fireplace | Faces are harder | Than we think
Faces are surprisingly hard to hold. Reviews of many studies find that people are better at recognising faces from their own group than from another, a pattern called the own-race bias. It's one more reason that a confident identification, from a stranger, can be wrong, and why the courts have been learning to treat it with care.

@chapter A15 autumn_overlook | Ten years later
There's a tender sequel to the story of vivid memories, which looks at how they change over a decade.

@study A12 cafe_sunset | William Hirst and colleagues | Published in the Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, 2015 | Followed more than three thousand people for ten years after the September 11 attacks, asking them about the day
In 2015, William Hirst, Elizabeth Phelps and colleagues reported a study of more than three thousand people, who had been asked about the attacks of September 11, 2001, at several points over ten years. Most of the forgetting happened in the first year. After that, the accounts stayed fairly steady from one occasion to the next.

@char A20 living_room_day | A memory that settles | Though not always correct
That fits what we know about consolidation. The story you tell after the first year tends to be the story you keep telling. But being steady is not the same as being right. A memory that has stopped changing may simply have settled into its final form, mistakes and all.

@list A12 living_room_day | The eleventh stop | Vivid and confident does not mean accurate ;; Flashbulb memories feel better, not necessarily are ;; Imagining can make something feel real ;; Confidence is most informative early and unprompted
So that's the eleventh stop. Vivid and confident doesn't mean accurate. Flashbulb memories feel better, they aren't necessarily better. Imagining can make something feel real. And confidence is most informative when it's early and unprompted.
