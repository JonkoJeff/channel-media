@chapter A19 bedroom_night | The night before
Let's go back to last night. Sam went to bed later than he meant to. There was a show he wanted to finish, and then a few messages, and then that strange, wide-awake feeling that sometimes arrives right when you finally switch off the light. By the time he fell asleep, it was late. And the alarm didn't care.

@char A10 bedroom_night | About five hours | Not a disaster
About five hours of sleep. Not a disaster. Most of us have done it, many times, and got through the next day just fine. We drink a little more coffee, we yawn in the afternoon, and we tell ourselves we'll catch up at the weekend. But there's one thing about a short night that's easy to miss. It doesn't only make us tired. It may make us shorter-tempered too.

@char A13 cabin_fireplace | Tired and touchy | A common feeling
You've probably noticed this in yourself. On a tired day, small things feel bigger. A slow queue, a loud phone call on the train, someone who walks just a little too slowly in front of you. On a rested day, you'd barely notice. On a tired day, it feels almost personal. But noticing something in yourself isn't the same as knowing it's true. So researchers put it to the test.

@study A12 living_room_day | Zlatan Krizan and Garrett Hisler | Iowa State University | published in 2019
In 2019, the psychologists Zlatan Krizan and Garrett Hisler at Iowa State University published a study in the Journal of Experimental Psychology: General. Its title was short and rather vivid. Sleepy anger. They wanted to know whether losing sleep actually causes more anger, or whether angry people just happen to sleep less.

@char A03 cafe_sunset | Cause or coincidence? | The tricky question
That second possibility matters. If you simply ask people how well they slept and how angry they feel, you might find a link. But you wouldn't know which came first. Maybe stress keeps people awake and also makes them irritable. To know whether sleep itself was doing the work, the researchers needed to change people's sleep on purpose. Not a lot, and not for long. Just enough to see whether a small, everyday kind of sleep loss would show up in how people felt the next day.

@list A15 cafe_sunset | How the study worked | Two groups, chosen at random ;; One slept normally ;; One slept 2 to 4 hours less ;; For two nights
So that's what they did. Participants were randomly assigned to one of two groups. One group kept their normal sleep. The other group cut their sleep by two to four hours a night, for two nights in a row. Random assignment is the important part. It means the two groups started out alike, so any difference afterwards is likely to come from the sleep.

@list A12 living_room_day | Sleep per night in the study | Normal sleep: about 7 hours ;; Restricted sleep: about 4.5 hours
On average, the normal-sleep group slept about seven hours a night. The restricted group slept about four and a half. That's not extreme. It's the kind of night many of us have before an early flight, or after a late evening out, or during a busy week, or with a newborn in the house. Very ordinary tiredness. The kind most of us would shrug off and say, I'm fine, I just need a coffee.

And then came the test. The researchers wanted to see how people felt when something mildly annoying was happening around them. Not a fight, not an insult. Just the kind of low, grinding discomfort that fills an ordinary day.

@char A10 attic_rain | A little bit of noise | Brown noise, white noise
So participants were asked to rate everyday products while listening to noise. Sometimes it was brown noise, a low, rumbling sound, and sometimes it was white noise, a harsher hiss. Unpleasant, but nothing dramatic. Rather like sitting on a warm, crowded bus at the end of a long day.

@stat A13 cabin_fireplace | Krizan and Hisler, 2019 | More anger | reported by the sleep-restricted group than the normal-sleep group
The result was clear. The people who had slept less reported more anger. Just two short nights, and the same mild noise felt more irritating. Their tempers were thinner. And this wasn't because they were angry people to begin with. They'd been chosen at random. The only thing that had changed was their sleep.

@char A18 living_room_day | The surprising part | Why it happens
What I find especially interesting is the next part. The researchers also asked people how sleepy they felt. And that sleepiness turned out to explain a large share of the effect. When people felt sleepier, they also tended to feel angrier.

@stat A12 cafe_sunset | Krizan and Hisler, 2019 | 50% | of the extra anger was explained by how sleepy people felt
About half of the effect of sleep loss on anger could be explained by sleepiness itself. So it isn't only that short sleep changes something hidden in the body. It's also that the feeling of being sleepy, heavy and foggy and worn, is itself an unpleasant feeling. And, as we've seen tonight, unpleasant feelings like company.

@char A03 living_room_day | Fewer resources | Less room to adapt
One way to picture it is this. Being well rested gives us a kind of cushion. When something annoying happens, we have room to absorb it, to shrug, to adapt, to let it pass. A short night takes some of that cushion away. The annoying thing is the same. There's just less padding between it and us. And when the padding is thin, even a soft knock can feel like a blow. A tired parent snaps at a spilled drink. A tired driver honks a second too soon. A tired brother reads three short words and hears an accusation. None of these people are bad. They're simply running with less cushion than usual.

@quote A19 rooftop_dusk | The noise was the same. The sleep was different. | What the sleepy anger study showed
I think that's worth holding onto. The noise in that study didn't get louder for the tired group. The products didn't get worse. The world was the same. What had changed was the person meeting it.

@char A10 bedroom_night | Back to Sam | Hungry and tired
Now think about Sam again. He skipped lunch. He slept about five hours. And he's sitting on a bus that's warm and noisy, with a message on his phone that could be read in more than one way. That's not one reason to snap. That's two or three small reasons, quietly stacked on top of one another.

@list A15 cafe_sunset | Sam's quiet stack | A short night ;; No real lunch ;; A long day of small frustrations ;; A noisy bus
And none of them has anything to do with his brother. That's the part that's so easy to miss in the moment. When we're angry, it feels as if the anger is entirely about the person in front of us. Very often, a good part of it was already there, waiting for somewhere to land.

@char A12 living_room_day | Not a judgement | Just useful to know
I don't say this to make anyone feel guilty about a late night. Life gets busy. Sleep gets squeezed. Sometimes there's a baby, or a deadline, or a worry that won't let go. The point isn't to blame ourselves for being tired. The point is to know that tiredness can quietly change the volume of our feelings.

@char A13 cabin_fireplace | A small habit | Asking one more question
So here's a second small question for Sam, to sit next to the first one. When did I last eat? And how did I sleep last night? If the answers are not well, and not much, then perhaps the message from his brother deserves a second reading, after a meal, or after a night of proper rest.

@quote A18 rooftop_dusk | Rest doesn't erase real problems. It helps us see which ones are real. | Still angry tomorrow? That's allowed too
And if he reads it again tomorrow, well fed and rested, and it still bothers him, that's worth knowing too. Then it really might be about something between him and his brother. Rest doesn't erase real problems. It just helps us see which problems are real.

@char A19 bedroom_night | The bus window | Warm and crowded
But for now, Sam is still on that bus. And there's one more thing in the air around him that we haven't talked about yet. The heat. The windows are fogged, the heater is on too high, and somebody's bag keeps pressing against his knee. It sounds like a small detail. But some of the most surprising evidence about anger comes from exactly this kind of small detail, measured on a very large scale.
