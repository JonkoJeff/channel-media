@chapter A12 rooftop_dusk | Who decides
Here's a small experiment you can try, lying wherever you are right now. At some moment of your own choosing, whenever you feel like it, gently move one finger. Don't plan it. Just let the urge arrive, and then move. Did you choose that moment? It certainly feels as though you did. But in the early 1980s, a scientist in California set out to test exactly that feeling.

@study A01 cafe_sunset | Benjamin Libet and colleagues | University of California, San Francisco, 1983 | A brain signal rose before people felt they had decided to move
His name was Benjamin Libet. In a study published in 1983, he asked volunteers to flex their wrist whenever they felt like it, while watching a special clock, a dot moving quickly around a circle. They had to note where the dot was at the moment they first felt the urge to move. Meanwhile, electrodes on their scalp recorded their brain activity.

@char A13 cabin_fireplace | Before the urge | A slow build up
Scientists already knew that, before a voluntary movement, a slow build up of electrical activity appears in the brain. It's called the readiness potential. Libet wanted to know whether that build up came before, or after, the moment people felt they had decided to move.

@stat A10 living_room_day | The brain signal came first | 0.35 s | before people reported the urge to move
The answer surprised him. The readiness potential began, on average, about a third of a second before people reported feeling the urge to move. In other words, the brain seemed to be preparing the movement before the person was aware of deciding to make it.

@char A15 autumn_overlook | A storm of debate | Do we have free will
You can imagine the storm this caused. Some people concluded that free will was an illusion, that our brains decide and our conscious minds simply find out a moment later. Libet himself didn't go that far. He suggested that we might still have a kind of veto, a last moment chance to stop a movement before it happens.

@study A03 rooftop_dusk | Chun Siong Soon, John-Dylan Haynes and colleagues | Max Planck Institute, Leipzig, 2008 | Brain scans predicted a left or right choice seconds early, a little better than chance
In 2008, a team led by John-Dylan Haynes went further. Volunteers lay in a brain scanner and freely chose to press a button with their left hand or their right. From patterns of activity in certain brain areas, the researchers could predict which hand a person would choose up to several seconds before the person felt they had decided.

@char A18 cafe_sunset | How well | Only a little
That sounds dramatic. But it's worth knowing how well the prediction worked. It was right about sixty percent of the time, when guessing would give you fifty. Better than chance, clearly, but far from reading anyone's mind. Something in the brain leaned one way, a little, before the choice felt made.

@chapter A16 autumn_overlook | A different reading
And then, in 2012, came a very different way of reading Libet's signal. Aaron Schurger, Jacobo Sitt and Stanislas Dehaene proposed that the readiness potential might not be a decision at all.

@study A20 cafe_sunset | Aaron Schurger, Jacobo Sitt and Stanislas Dehaene | PNAS, 2012 | The early brain signal may be ordinary background noise drifting towards a threshold
Their idea was this. The brain is never perfectly still. Its activity drifts up and down all the time, like small waves. When you've been asked to move at some point, any moment when the drift happens to rise towards a threshold becomes a likely moment to move. Average many such moments together, and you get something that looks like a slow build up, even though nothing was decided early.

@char A13 cabin_fireplace | A quieter picture | Not a hidden decider
In this view, there's no hidden decider getting in first. There's a general readiness, and a mind that waits for a good moment, and random little fluctuations that tip the timing one way or another. For tiny, meaningless choices, like when to move a finger, that's perhaps not surprising at all.

@char A10 living_room_day | Fingers and lives | Very different choices
It's also worth remembering what these experiments studied. When to move a finger, or which button to press, with nothing at stake and no reasons either way. Mara's decision is the opposite. It has reasons on both sides, consequences for years, and people she loves involved. What's true for a finger may tell us very little about a life.

@photo lf33_b10_01.jpg | Waves that rise and fall
Picture the brain's quiet background activity as small waves of light, rising and falling all the time. Now and then, a wave happens to rise a little higher than the rest. And sometimes, that's simply the moment a finger moves.

@chapter A05 cabin_fireplace | Sleeping on it
There's another popular idea about the hidden mind and decisions. That for complicated choices, it's better not to think consciously at all. Let it go, distract yourself, and let the unconscious mind quietly work on the problem. Then the answer will come.

@study A01 rooftop_dusk | Ap Dijksterhuis and colleagues | Science, 2006 | Distracted choosers seemed to pick better from complex options
In 2006, the Dutch psychologist Ap Dijksterhuis and his colleagues published a study in Science suggesting just that. People read about four imaginary cars, each with twelve good or bad features. Some chose right away. Some thought carefully for a few minutes. And some were distracted with puzzles before choosing. For complicated choices, the distracted group seemed to pick the best car most often.

@char A04 attic_rain | A lovely idea | Hard to repeat
It's a lovely idea, and it spread quickly. Don't think, just let it simmer. But other researchers struggled to find the same effect. In 2008, Femke Acker combined the existing studies and found little reliable support. And in 2015, Michael Nieuwenstein and his colleagues ran a large, careful replication, and found no advantage for the distracted choosers at all.

@char A12 cabin_fireplace | Something still true | Time helps in other ways
So unconscious thought, in that strong form, doesn't seem to hold up. But there's still something in the old advice to sleep on it. Time lets the first rush of feeling settle. It lets new thoughts and memories arrive. And it gives you the chance to imagine your choice on a different day, in a different mood.

@chapter A19 bedroom_night | So who decides
So, who is doing the deciding? Perhaps the best answer is that there isn't a single decider at all. There are fast processes and slow ones. Feelings and memories. Patterns and habits. Some of it reaches awareness, and much of it doesn't. You are not only the part that watches. You are the whole system.

@char A15 cafe_sunset | Mara's whole self | All of it hers
When Mara finally decides, perhaps tomorrow, perhaps next week, it won't be a little judge in her head that does it. It will be all of her. Her memories of her sister. Her love of her city. Her tiredness tonight, and her hopes for the morning. The fast parts and the slow parts, together. And all of that is hers.

@char A20 living_room_day | Not an illusion | Just not simple
That's perhaps a more comforting way to see it. Your choices aren't fake because some of the work happens out of sight. Your heart beats out of sight too, and it's still your heart. Most of your mind works below the surface. Deciding is simply the moment when some of that work becomes visible.

@char A18 autumn_overlook | A strange twist | We explain afterwards
But there's a strange twist still to come. If much of the work happens out of sight, how well do we really know why we chose what we chose? We certainly feel that we know. We can always give a reason. The question is whether those reasons are the real ones.
