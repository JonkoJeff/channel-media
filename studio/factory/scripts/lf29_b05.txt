@char A10 cabin_fireplace | A book in your hands | Do you hear it?
Think about the last time you read a good book. Not a menu or a message, but a story you sank into. When you read the words on the page, did you hear them? Was there a voice, reading along inside your head? Many people say yes, of course. Others pause, and say, actually, I'm not sure.

@char A13 attic_rain | A common belief | The reading voice
It's often said that reading is a kind of silent speech, that we all quietly say the words to ourselves as our eyes move along the line. It feels obvious to many readers. But as we saw with the beepers, what feels obvious about our own minds isn't always what's there when someone checks carefully.

@chapter A03 autumn_overlook | An old story about silent reading
Before we look at the science, here's a story that people have told about reading for a very long time.

@study A18 living_room_day | Augustine of Hippo | Confessions, around 400 | Described his teacher Ambrose reading with his eyes only, his voice and tongue at rest
In his Confessions, written around the year 400, Augustine of Hippo describes watching Ambrose, the bishop of Milan, reading. His eyes moved over the page and his heart searched out the meaning, Augustine writes, but his voice and tongue were at rest. Augustine seems to have found this worth remarking on, and for a long time the passage was taken as evidence that silent reading was rare in the ancient world.

@char A19 cabin_fireplace | A second look | Maybe not so rare
Historians have since questioned that. In 1968, the classicist Bernard Knox published an article arguing that silent reading was not as unusual in antiquity as people had assumed, and others have added evidence since. So the story may tell us more about Augustine's curiosity than about how everyone read. But it does show that people have long noticed the strange relationship between reading and the voice.

Picture an old book open on a desk by lamplight, and a reader leaning over it in silence. Nothing moves except their eyes. And yet, somewhere inside, the words are becoming a story, with people and places and feelings. The question is whether a voice is doing that work, or something else.

@chapter A12 cafe_sunset | Catching readers in the act
In 2018, Russell Hurlburt's group turned their beepers on exactly this question.

@study A03 autumn_overlook | Brouwers, Heavey, Hurlburt and colleagues | Journal of Consciousness Studies, 2018 | Beeped students reading short stories, and rarely found an inner voice narrating the text
Vincent Brouwers, Christopher Heavey, Russell Hurlburt and their colleagues published their study in the Journal of Consciousness Studies. Sixteen college students read short stories by F. Scott Fitzgerald and Ernest Hemingway, while a beeper sounded at random moments. At each beep, the students noted what was in their experience, and were interviewed carefully afterwards, just as in the earlier studies.

@stat A12 cafe_sunset | Moments with inner narration of the text | 3% | of samples while reading stories (Brouwers et al., 2018)
The result surprised many people. An inner voice narrating the text, saying the words of the story along with the eyes, showed up in only about three percent of the samples. The title of the paper said it plainly: silent reading is not silent speaking of the text.

@list A15 living_room_day | What readers did experience | Pictures of the scene, often ;; Some words, about a quarter of samples ;; Focus on a single word ;; Their own comments on the story
So what was going on instead? Inner pictures were frequent. People saw the scene, the room, the characters. Words did appear in roughly a quarter of the samples, but often not as a narration. Sometimes a reader was focused on one particular word from the text. Sometimes they were silently commenting on the story, in their own words. And sometimes the words had nothing to do with the book at all.

@char A10 cabin_fireplace | Why we overestimate | Expectations shape reports
The authors suggested that one reason people overestimate the reading voice is that they expect it to be there. If you've always been told that reading is silent speech, then when you ask yourself whether you hear the words, you check, and in that moment of checking, you probably do. The act of looking may switch it on.

@chapter A05 autumn_overlook | But voices do show up
That doesn't mean the reading voice is a myth. Other studies suggest that, at least some of the time, our brains do bring voices into reading, especially when a text asks for it.

@study A13 living_room_day | Jessica Alexander & Lynne Nygaard | Emory University, 2008 | Readers seemed to read a text at the pace of the person they imagined saying it
In 2008, Jessica Alexander and Lynne Nygaard at Emory University published a study about this. Participants first got to know two talkers, one who spoke quickly and one who spoke slowly. Later, they silently read passages that were said to have been written by one of them. The reading times suggested that people were, in a sense, hearing the writer's voice. They tended to read more slowly when the passage came from the slow talker, especially when the text was hard.

@study A03 cabin_fireplace | Bo Yao, Pascal Belin & Christoph Scheepers | University of Glasgow, 2011 | Reading quoted speech activated voice-sensitive parts of the brain more than reported speech
And in 2011, Bo Yao, Pascal Belin and Christoph Scheepers at the University of Glasgow published a brain imaging study in the Journal of Cognitive Neuroscience. People silently read stories that contained either direct quotations, like, she said, I'm leaving, or indirect reports, like, she said that she was leaving. The direct quotations produced more activity in voice-sensitive areas of the auditory cortex.

@list A12 cafe_sunset | Putting it together | Readers don't narrate every word ;; Pictures and meaning often lead ;; Voices appear when the text invites them ;; People differ a great deal
So the picture is richer than either a yes or a no. Most of the time, readers probably don't narrate every word in their heads. Pictures and meaning often seem to lead. But voices can appear, especially when the text invites them, with a character speaking, or a writer whose voice you know. And, as always, people differ a great deal.

@char A20 living_room_day | Try it later | A small experiment
If you'd like, you can try a small experiment the next time you read. Don't check constantly, because checking changes things. Just once or twice, stop suddenly and ask yourself, what was there, just then? A voice? A picture? A feeling? Nothing in particular? You might be surprised by what you find.

@char A19 bedroom_night | A gentle point | Your way of reading
And whatever you find, it's fine. Some people hear every line of dialogue in a different voice, like a radio play. Some see a film. Some simply absorb the meaning, with very little inner sound at all. Each of these is a way of reading, and each of them gets the story from the page into the mind.

@chapter A12 cabin_fireplace | Back to the inner voice
For our story tonight, reading offers one more lesson about the inner voice.

@char A13 attic_rain | Less than we think | More varied too
The inner voice may be less constant than we believe, even in the places where we're most certain it lives. And it seems to come and go depending on what the mind needs, appearing when a voice is useful, and stepping back when pictures or meaning can do the job better.

But when the voice does appear, where does it actually happen? Is it all in the brain, or does the body join in? A century ago, a few researchers tried to answer that by attaching sensors to the lips and throat of people who were only thinking.
