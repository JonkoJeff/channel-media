After a restless hour, Nora sleeps. And then, at around four, her eyes open again, for no reason that she can name. The house is silent, her mind is clear and a little too lively, and a familiar thought arrives. I've woken up again, so something must be wrong. @chapter N27 rooftop_dusk | The architecture of a night
To see why, we need a quick tour of what a normal night of sleep actually looks like, because it's not the flat, unbroken stretch that most of us imagine.

@char N19 living_room_day | A discovery in 1953 | Rapid eye movements
In 1953, a graduate student named Eugene Aserinsky, working with Nathaniel Kleitman in Chicago, noticed that sleeping people's eyes sometimes darted rapidly under their lids, and that those periods came with brain activity that looked like waking. They had discovered what we now call REM sleep. It was a surprise, because sleep had been thought of as a simple switch.

@char N09 cafe_sunset | The cycles | About ninety minutes
Sleep researchers now describe the night as a series of cycles, each lasting roughly ninety minutes, though they vary from person to person and from night to night. Each cycle moves through lighter sleep, deeper sleep, and REM sleep, and then back towards the surface. In a typical night, an adult goes through about four to six of them.

@char N02 cabin_fireplace | Deep early, dreamy late | A shifting balance
The mix changes over the night. The deepest sleep tends to come in the first few hours, while REM sleep takes up more and more of each cycle toward morning. That's why the early morning hours are so full of dreams, and also why they're lighter. Near the end of each cycle, you are very close to the surface.

@char N13 attic_rain | The surfacing | Waking is part of the pattern
And right at the end of a cycle, many people briefly wake up. Most of the time, they don't remember it, because the memory doesn't form. Sleep laboratories have recorded that even good sleepers wake up several times a night, usually for a few seconds to a minute or two. We only notice the ones that last long enough to leave a trace, or the ones when we're worrying.

@char N10 living_room_day | A yardstick | What counts as normal
How much waking is normal? In 2017, a panel assembled by the National Sleep Foundation, led by Maurice Ohayon, reviewed the evidence and published recommendations for what counts as good sleep quality in adults. They suggested that falling asleep in about fifteen minutes or less, waking at most once a night, and being awake for no more than about twenty minutes in total during the night, are signs of good sleep.

@char N19 autumn_overlook | A caution | Averages, not rules
Of course, those are rough guides, and many healthy people fall slightly outside them from time to time. But they do show that a wake-up or two isn't a symptom of anything by itself. If Nora wakes at four, lies quietly for ten minutes, and dozes off again, she's within the range of ordinary sleep, even though the memory of it feels like a very long time.

@chapter N20 cabin_fireplace | The two sleeps
There's an old idea about night-time waking that has charmed many people, and it's an idea that comes from history.

@char N02 living_room_day | A historian's discovery | First sleep and second sleep
In 2001, the American historian Roger Ekirch published a paper called "Sleep We Have Lost", based on years of reading diaries, court records, medical books and literature from Britain and other parts of Europe before the Industrial Revolution. He noticed that people kept referring, casually, to a first sleep and a second sleep, as though it were ordinary.

@char N09 rooftop_dusk | What people did | The hour in between
According to those sources, people would sleep soon after dusk, wake some time after midnight for an hour or so, and then sleep again until morning. In the middle, they might pray, talk quietly, tend the fire, or simply lie in a state of relaxed rest. Ekirch collected hundreds of references, and his work has been widely discussed.

@study N07 cabin_fireplace | Thomas Wehr | American Journal of Physiology, 1992 | Eight healthy men were kept in darkness for fourteen hours each night for about a month, and their sleep was recorded
In 1992, Thomas Wehr, a psychiatrist at the National Institute of Mental Health, kept volunteers in darkness for fourteen hours each night for several weeks. After a period of adjustment, many of them began to sleep in two main bouts, with a quiet period of wakefulness in between, typically lasting an hour or two.

@char N13 attic_rain | A caution | The picture is debated
It would be easy to tell this as a tidy tale in which our ancestors slept in two parts and we've broken it. But scientists are cautious. The historical sources are patchy, and the laboratory conditions in Wehr's study were unusual. Meanwhile, studies of people in traditional societies, such as the one by Gandhi Yetish and colleagues in 2015, found that they mostly slept in a single main period.

@char N02 cabin_fireplace | Why four a.m.? | The early morning rise
There may also be a biological reason that so many people wake in the small hours. Late in the night, sleep pressure has been mostly used up, because you've slept for several hours, while the body clock is starting to push alertness upwards towards the morning, and stress hormones such as cortisol naturally begin to rise. The balance of the two clocks we met earlier shifts, and sleep becomes lighter.

@char N09 bedroom_night | A quiet reminder | Light sleep is still sleep
At that stage, sleep is simply easier to disturb. A car passing, a pipe settling, a pang of hunger, or the pressure of a full bladder can pull a sleeper to the surface, where a worried mind is waiting. It's another reason why a dark, quiet, cool room can matter, and why it is worth removing the little things that nudge a light sleeper awake.

@char N19 living_room_day | When waking is a signal | Things worth checking
That said, there are times when night waking is a sign of something specific. Loud snoring with gasping or pauses can point to sleep apnoea, which affects many adults and is often undiagnosed. Restless legs, pain, acid reflux, and certain medicines can all break up sleep. If you wake often and feel unrefreshed, or a partner notices breathing pauses, a doctor can look into it, and many causes are treatable.

@char N10 cafe_sunset | What we can safely say | Waking is not new
So what can we say? That waking in the night is probably very old, that it isn't automatically a disorder, and that how we respond to it may matter more than the wake-up itself. Where people accepted the middle of the night as a quiet part of life, they weren't alarmed by it. And a body that isn't alarmed has a far better chance of returning to sleep.

@char N19 living_room_day | Practical echoes | Rest without alarm
Sleep specialists today often say much the same thing in different words. If you wake in the night, try not to treat it as an emergency. Lie quietly, keep the lights low, and let your body do what it can. Many people find that the wakefulness passes more quickly when they stop fighting it, much as the Wehr volunteers seemed to treat their quiet hour as part of the night.

@char N10 bedroom_night | Back to Nora | A quieter four a.m.
So the next time Nora opens her eyes at four, she might try thinking something different. Not I've woken up again, so something must be wrong, but I'm in the middle of a long, ordinary night, and this is one of its pauses. It's a small reframe, but it takes the alarm out of the moment, and without the alarm, there's room for sleep.
