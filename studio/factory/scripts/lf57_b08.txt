Researchers have tried to find out whether that's a general rule.

@char A10 living_room_day | An apology without words | The remedial idea
The idea goes like this. When you make a small mistake in front of others, like knocking over a glass, or forgetting someone's name, a blush might work as a kind of apology without words. It shows that you know something went wrong, that you care what others think, and that you're not going to brush it off. Psychologists call this the remedial function of blushing.

@char N20 cabin_fireplace | A signal that's hard to fake | Why that matters
For a signal like that to be trusted, it helps if it's difficult to fake. And a blush is very difficult to fake. You can force a smile, you can fake a frown, but almost nobody can blush on purpose. That makes a blush rather like an honest message from the body, one that the person sending it can't easily control.

@study A13 autumn_overlook | Peter de Jong, Madelon Peters and David De Cremer | Motivation and Emotion, 2003 | Volunteers judged people in short stories who blushed, or didn't, after mishaps and in unclear situations
In 2003, Peter de Jong, Madelon Peters and David De Cremer ran two experiments, with forty-nine and then fifty-eight volunteers. People read short descriptions of someone in an awkward situation. Sometimes the person blushed, and sometimes they didn't. Then the volunteers judged what they thought of that person.

@list N09 attic_rain | What the 2003 study found | After an obvious mishap, blushing helped ;; After a deliberate wrong, it helped too ;; In an unclear situation, it hurt trust
The results were more interesting than a simple yes or no. After an obvious accident, blushing helped: the person who blushed was judged more kindly. After a clear, deliberate wrong, blushing also helped, as a sign of regret. But when the situation was unclear, when it wasn't obvious whether the person had done anything wrong at all, blushing undermined how trustworthy they seemed.

@char A03 living_room_day | Blushing may signify guilt | When the story is unclear
The paper's title says it all: Blushing May Signify Guilt. If nobody knows what happened, and you go red, people may fill in the gap with the worst explanation. So the blush seems to work as a kind of amplifier. It makes whatever people already believe about the situation a little stronger, for better or worse.

@char N10 bedroom_night | Other studies agree | Blushing after a mishap
Other researchers have found similar things. A study by Corine Dijk, Peter de Jong and Madelon Peters, published in 2009, found that people who blushed after a mishap or a small wrong were judged more positively than those who stayed pale. Psychologists who study embarrassment, like Dacher Keltner in America, have also argued that the whole embarrassed display works to calm others down after a social slip.

@char A19 cabin_fireplace | Darwin, revisited | Maybe it is useful
So maybe Darwin was wrong on this one point. He thought a blush was no use to anyone. Modern research suggests it may be quite useful after all. Not always, and not in every situation. But in the everyday business of living among other people, where we're constantly making little mistakes, a blush may help smooth things over.

@char N29 rooftop_dusk | Elena's audience | Warmth returned
Back in the church hall, Elena's situation wasn't ambiguous at all. She'd done something brave, it went well, and her blush said, I care about this, and I'm a little shy. @char A12 street_day | A sharper test | A game with real stakes
But reading stories about imaginary people is one thing. What happens when real people are face to face, with something actually at stake? In 2002, the same Dutch research group set up an experiment with a well-known game from economics, where people have to decide whether to trust each other. They wanted to see what a real blush does in a real moment of letting someone down.
