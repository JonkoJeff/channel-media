@chapter A14 conspiracy_board | Can adults grow new brain cells?
Many of us were taught, at school or by someone's grandmother, that we're born with all of the brain cells that we'll ever have. Every glass of wine, every knock on the head, every year that passes, is a cell lost for ever. It's a gloomy thought, and for most of the twentieth century, it was what neuroscientists believed as well.

@char A12 living_room_day | The old doctrine | No new neurons
The idea was spelled out by the great Spanish neuroscientist Santiago Ramón y Cajal, who wrote in 1913 that in the adult brain, everything may die, nothing may be regenerated. For decades, that was treated as a law. A few researchers, notably Joseph Altman in the 1960s, reported new neurons in adult rats, but their findings were largely ignored.

@char A04 attic_rain | The turn | Birds, then rats, then people
In the 1980s, Fernando Nottebohm at Rockefeller University found that songbirds grew new neurons in the part of the brain that learns songs. That made people look again at mammals. By the 1990s, the evidence was growing that adult rodents made new neurons in a region of the brain called the hippocampus, which is vital for memory.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Eriksson et al. | Nature Medicine, 1998 | Neurogenesis in the adult human hippocampus
In 1998, a team led by Peter Eriksson in Sweden, working with Fred Gage in California, studied the brains of cancer patients who had been given a chemical marker for dividing cells, as part of their care. After death, the marker was found in new neurons in the hippocampus. It was the first strong evidence that adult human brains might produce new cells.

@list A03 living_room_day | Why this mattered | It overturned a century-old belief ;; It suggested the adult brain can renew itself ;; It pointed to the hippocampus, a centre of memory ;; It opened up the idea that exercise and learning could help
It was a thrilling finding, and for twenty years, many people took it as settled. Books, talks and magazine covers announced that you can grow new brain cells. But, as so often, the story turned out to be more complicated.

@chapter A13 rooftop_dusk | Counting by carbon
In 2013, Kirsty Spalding and Jonas Frisén at the Karolinska Institute in Sweden used a clever trick to date brain cells. Nuclear weapon tests in the Cold War had pumped a spike of radioactive carbon-14 into the atmosphere, which was taken up by every living thing. The level of carbon-14 in a cell's DNA tells you roughly when that cell was born.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Spalding et al. | Cell, 2013 | Dynamics of hippocampal neurogenesis in adult humans
Using brain tissue donated after death, they found that people did indeed make new neurons in the hippocampus throughout their lives. They estimated about seven hundred new neurons per day in each hippocampus, which amounts to a renewal of a small proportion of the cells each year. It's a modest rate, but a real one.

@stat A14 conspiracy_board | New neurons per day | About 700 | in each hippocampus, according to the carbon-dating estimate
That figure sounds large, until you remember that the hippocampus contains millions of neurons. The estimate implies that only a small fraction, around one or two percent of a certain group of cells each year, are replaced. It's more of a trickle than a flood, but it's far from zero.

@char A10 attic_rain | A challenge | Not everyone agrees
Then, in 2018, a team led by Shawn Sorrells and Arturo Alvarez-Buylla at the University of California, San Francisco, published a very different result in Nature. They examined brain tissue from people of all ages, and found that new neurons were abundant in the hippocampus of babies, dropped sharply in childhood, and were almost undetectable in adults.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Sorrells et al. | Nature, 2018 | Human hippocampal neurogenesis drops sharply in children to undetectable levels in adults
This was a striking contradiction. The authors argued that the earlier findings might have been errors, caused by the way the tissue was treated or by cells that looked like new neurons but weren't. The result caused a storm in the field, and it's fair to say that it has not been entirely resolved.

@char A12 living_room_day | The reply | Evidence from older brains
Other groups answered with their own studies. In 2018, Maura Boldrini and colleagues at Columbia University reported new neurons in the hippocampus of people up to the age of seventy-nine. And in 2019, Maria Llorens-Martín and her team in Madrid found immature neurons in healthy people, and fewer in those with Alzheimer's disease.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Moreno-Jimenez et al. | Nature Medicine, 2019 | Adult hippocampal neurogenesis is abundant in neurologically healthy subjects and drops sharply in patients with Alzheimer's disease
Their paper used tissue that had been prepared with great care, and the authors argued that the difference between studies came down to the quality of the tissue. The field remains divided, with careful scientists on both sides. It's an honest, live scientific argument, and not a settled case.

@list A12 living_room_day | Where the evidence stands | Adult human neurogenesis probably occurs at a low level ;; Some studies find it, others do not ;; Tissue preparation may explain part of the difference ;; Its importance for memory in humans is not yet clear
That's the careful summary. The old doctrine that adults can never make new neurons is almost certainly too strong. But the popular claim, that you can easily grow a lot of new cells by eating blueberries or doing puzzles, goes far beyond the evidence.

@chapter A06 cafe_sunset | The brain still changes
Even if new cells are rare, there's no doubt that the adult brain changes. It does so by reshaping the connections between cells, strengthening some and pruning others. This is called plasticity, and it is among the best-established findings in neuroscience.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Maguire et al. | Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 2000 | Navigation-related structural change in the hippocampi of taxi drivers
Eleanor Maguire and her colleagues at University College London scanned the brains of sixteen licensed London taxi drivers, who had spent years memorising the layout of thousands of streets. They found that the back part of the hippocampus was larger in the taxi drivers than in a comparison group, and that it was larger the more years they had been driving.

@char A01 cafe_sunset | The taxi drivers | Learning the Knowledge
In London, taxi drivers must pass a notoriously difficult test called the Knowledge, which involves learning around twenty-five thousand streets. In a later study in 2011, Katherine Woollett and Maguire followed trainee drivers, scanning them before and after the training. Those who qualified showed an increase in the same part of the hippocampus, while those who failed did not.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Woollett & Maguire | Current Biology, 2011 | Acquiring the Knowledge of London's layout drives structural brain changes
This is a pleasing finding because it followed the same people over time, which makes it much more convincing. Learning something hard seems to reshape the structure of the brain, even in adulthood. We don't know exactly what changes at the level of cells, but the effect on the size of the region is clear.

@char A04 attic_rain | A caution | Small samples, big headlines
It's worth keeping this in perspective. The taxi driver studies involved small groups, and the size of the changes was modest. They show that the adult brain is adaptable, and not that you can get a bigger brain by learning a few streets. A balanced view avoids both the old pessimism and the new hype.

@list A03 living_room_day | What's reasonably supported | The adult brain changes with learning ;; Exercise is linked to better memory and mood ;; Sleep supports the brain's maintenance ;; Staying socially and mentally active is associated with healthier ageing
Notice the careful language. These are associations and modest effects, not guarantees. But they point in the same direction, towards living in a way that keeps the brain engaged and rested.

A map, a compass and a pencil. For the taxi drivers, it was twenty-five thousand streets. For the rest of us, it might be a language, an instrument or a skill. The brain, it seems, is more like a city than a monument, always being rebuilt in small ways.

@char A15 cabin_fireplace | A gentle thought | It's never too late
There's a hopeful note to take from this. Whatever the final answer on new neurons, the broader message is clear. The adult brain isn't fixed. It responds to what you ask of it, and learning something new, at any age, leaves a trace.

@char A08 cabin_fireplace | Looking back | What the brain cells taught us
So what did we learn? That the old doctrine of no new neurons has been overturned, but that the extent of adult neurogenesis in humans is still debated. That the brain's connections change throughout life. And that learning something hard leaves measurable marks.

@char A12 living_room_day | Exercise and the brain | What the evidence says
One of the better-supported ideas about the brain is that regular aerobic exercise helps memory. In 2011, Kirk Erickson and colleagues at the University of Pittsburgh randomly assigned older adults to a year of walking or stretching. The walkers showed a small increase in the size of the hippocampus, while the stretchers showed a small decrease, and the walkers improved on a memory test.

@list A03 living_room_day | Sensible claims about the adult brain | It can change with learning ;; It benefits from exercise ;; It needs sleep to maintain itself ;; It does not need expensive gadgets
A sensible summary of the adult brain is a hopeful one. It can change with learning. It benefits from exercise and sleep. And it doesn't need expensive gadgets or miracle supplements to do so. Be wary of anyone who sells a quick way to grow new brain cells. The honest advice is older and cheaper, and consists of moving, resting and learning new things.

@quote A15 cabin_fireplace | Small steps count | A gentle thought
It doesn't take a marathon. The walking group in that study walked about forty minutes a day, three days a week. It's a modest routine, and it fits with the broader message of this chapter. The adult brain responds to how we live, and the changes that matter most are often made of small, repeated, ordinary things.

@char A19 cabin_fireplace | A reflective moment | Learn something hard
If the taxi drivers can teach us anything, it's that hard learning shapes us. Perhaps you'd like to learn a language, an instrument or a craft. The research can't promise a bigger hippocampus, but it does suggest that a mind that's kept busy with new and difficult things tends to stay flexible. And a more important benefit is the pleasure of doing something that you couldn't do before.
