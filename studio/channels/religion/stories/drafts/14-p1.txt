CHAPTER: A Letter Older Than the Gospels
The oldest written account of the Last Supper is not in any of the four Gospels. It sits in a letter that Paul wrote to the Christians of Corinth, probably in the mid-fifties of the first century. What did he already know about that final meal? || 1 Corinthians 11:23–26 || text-card
Picture a long upper room in Jerusalem, lamps burning low, a table with bread and a cup of wine. Thirteen people sit or recline around it. Outside, a city full of pilgrims is preparing for one of the great festivals of the Jewish year. || The Cenacle, Jerusalem || wikimedia
That meal has been painted thousands of times, and retold at altars and tables across the world. Yet the historical question begins with something plain: what do our earliest sources actually say, and how do they differ from one another? || The Last Supper, Leonardo da Vinci || wikimedia
Paul writes in First Corinthians that he is passing on what he received. He says that on the night Jesus was handed over, he took bread, gave thanks, broke it, and spoke words about his body. The wording is careful, almost formal. || Papyrus 46, First Corinthians || wikimedia
Notice what Paul does not say. He does not name the upper room, or describe the feet, or mention Passover. He gives the core of the meal, because the Corinthians are quarrelling over their own shared meals and he wants to remind them where the practice came from. || map: Roman Empire 54 CE Corinth || map
The Gospels came later. Mark is usually dated to around the year seventy, Matthew and Luke some years after, and John toward the end of the century. So the meal appears first as a remembered ritual, and only afterward as a scene in a longer story. || Codex Sinaiticus, Gospel pages || wikimedia
That order matters for how we read. When Christians gathered to share bread and a cup, they were already doing it before any Gospel was written down. The Gospel accounts, in part, record a practice that communities were living. || Dura-Europos house church, Syria || wikimedia
Today we will follow the evening step by step. We will begin with the festival that filled Jerusalem, then enter the room, and listen to what each account preserves, where they agree, and where the sources leave us with open questions. || map: Jerusalem first century CE || map
We will keep four layers apart. What the texts report. How early communities understood it. What historians can say about the setting. And what later traditions built on all of this, which is a very large story of its own. || The Last Supper, Juan de Juanes || wikimedia
Throughout, remember who sits at this table. Jesus and his disciples are Jews, keeping the rhythms of a Jewish festival in the Jewish capital. Everything that follows grows out of that world. || Western Wall Jerusalem || wikimedia
Let us begin where the city itself begins that week: with the crowds, the lambs, and the memory of an escape from Egypt. || Jerusalem pilgrims entering the city, David Roberts || wikimedia
CHAPTER: Passover in Jerusalem
Every spring, Jerusalem swelled far beyond its normal size. Pilgrims from Galilee, Judea and distant cities in the Roman world climbed the roads toward the Temple, carrying animals, bread, and the memory of a night long ago in Egypt. || David Roberts, Jerusalem from the Mount of Olives || wikimedia
The festival is described in Exodus twelve. A family takes a lamb, marks the doorposts, eats the meal in haste, and is spared as the last plague passes through Egypt. The next morning, Israel leaves slavery behind. || Exodus 12:1–14 || text-card
Passover was not only remembered, it was reenacted. Families were meant to retell the story to their children, eat unleavened bread and bitter herbs, and see themselves as people who had themselves been brought out of Egypt. || Passover Seder plate || wikimedia
In the first century, the festival had a Temple dimension too. Lambs were brought to the Temple and slaughtered in the afternoon, and the meat was then eaten that evening in groups, often within the city walls. || Model of Second Temple Jerusalem, Israel Museum || wikimedia
Jewish sources from the period, including the later rabbinic text called the Mishnah, describe how the meal developed: wine, bread, bitter herbs, the telling of the story, and the singing of Psalms. Some details come from later centuries, so historians use them with care. || Mishnah manuscript, Pesachim || wikimedia
The Mishnah was written down around the year two hundred, long after the Temple fell. It reflects a Judaism that had to continue without the sacrifice. So its picture of the meal cannot simply be laid over the first century, though it preserves older customs. || Kaufmann Manuscript Mishnah || wikimedia
Still, a few features seem to go back to Jesus' time. People ate in the evening, they reclined at table as free people did, and they drank wine. They also explained the meaning of what was on the table as part of the meal itself. || Reclining banquet scene, Roman relief || wikimedia
That last point is important. A Passover meal was already a meal full of words about the food. The bread was not simply bread: it was the bread of affliction. The cup was not simply wine: it recalled deliverance. || Matzah and wine cup || wikimedia
When the Gospels show Jesus speaking over bread and cup, they place him inside a pattern that his listeners would recognise. Teachers at a festival meal explained the symbols, and the symbols carried history. || Psalm 113–118 Hallel text-card || text-card
There was also political tension in the air. Jerusalem was under Roman governance, with a Roman prefect, Pontius Pilate, who normally stayed on the coast and came up to the city at festival time to keep order. || Pontius Pilate inscription, Caesarea || wikimedia
A festival that celebrated liberation from an empire, in a city under another empire, invited anxious attention from those in authority. Crowds were large, and memory of freedom was strong. || Roman soldiers, Trajan's Column relief || wikimedia
Into that setting, the Gospels place Jesus and his followers. Mark, Matthew and Luke describe a journey from Galilee, a triumphal arrival, days of teaching in the Temple area, and then the evening we are about to enter. || Entry into Jerusalem, Giotto Scrovegni Chapel || wikimedia
They set the meal on the first day of Unleavened Bread, when the Passover lamb is sacrificed. That wording, as we will see, raises a real question about dates, which we will return to later in this video. || Mark 14:12 || text-card
For now, picture simply a household of pilgrims looking for a place to eat. Where would thirteen Galileans find a room in a crowded city? The Gospels have a small and curious answer. || Jerusalem narrow street, Old City || wikimedia
CHAPTER: Preparing the Room
Mark tells the story with unusual detail. The disciples ask Jesus where he wants to eat the Passover. He sends two of them into the city and tells them they will meet a man carrying a jar of water. || Mark 14:12–16 || text-card
It is a curious instruction. In that culture, carrying water was usually women's work, so a man with a jar would have stood out. Some readers think this detail simply helps the two disciples spot him in a crowd. || Woman carrying water jar, Jerusalem, 19th century photograph || loc
They are to follow him and say to the owner of the house that the teacher asks where his guest room is. They will then be shown a large upper room, furnished and ready. There they are to prepare the meal. || Upper room stairway, Old City Jerusalem || wikimedia
Readers have asked whether this was a prearranged signal. Perhaps Jesus had friends in the city. Perhaps this is the Gospel's way of showing his foreknowledge. The text itself does not decide between these, and we should not claim to know. || Jerusalem Old City courtyard || wikimedia
Matthew shortens the story. In his account, Jesus tells the disciples to go to a certain man in the city and say that his time is near and he will keep the Passover at his house. The disciples do as they were told. || Matthew 26:17–19 || text-card
Luke names the two disciples. They are Peter and John. In his version, Jesus says they will meet the man with the water jar, and he adds a line about preparing the Passover for them. Luke's focus is on the sense of purpose in the preparations. || Luke 22:7–13 || text-card
John, interestingly, has no such scene. His Gospel moves straight from earlier events in Bethany to a supper that is simply described as taking place before the festival of Passover. That is the first sign of a different outline. || John 13:1 || text-card
Where was this house? The Gospels do not say. Later Christian tradition associated the upper room with a spot on the southwestern hill of Jerusalem, the area called Mount Zion, and a medieval building known as the Cenacle stands there today. || Cenacle, Mount Zion, Jerusalem || wikimedia
Historians are cautious about that identification. The current building dates mainly to the Crusader period, and the tradition linking it to the Last Supper developed over centuries. Whether it marks the real site is not something we can prove. || Crusader architecture, Cenacle interior || wikimedia
What the story does give us is a sense of the arrangements of the time. A large upper room, probably in a prosperous house, set with couches or low tables for a group meal. Hospitality, here, is part of the scene. || Roman triclinium, Pompeii || wikimedia
Preparing a Passover also meant practical work. Someone needed a lamb from the Temple, unleavened bread, bitter herbs, wine, and a place to roast and share the meat. Peter and John are therefore given real tasks. || Unleavened bread, matzah baking || wikimedia
By evening, everyone is gathered. In the Synoptic Gospels, the picture is of Jesus with the Twelve. Other followers, including women who had travelled with him, are present in Jerusalem, but the texts do not place them in the room. || Luke 8:1–3 || text-card
That detail has been much discussed. Later art, including Leonardo's famous painting, shows only men. The Gospel texts are silent about anyone else, so on this question, we can only say that we do not know. || Last Supper, Tintoretto, San Giorgio Maggiore || wikimedia
And now, the lamps are lit, the couches are filled, and the meal begins. In John's Gospel, the first thing that happens is not the breaking of bread. It is something much quieter, and more surprising. || Oil lamp, first century Judea, Israel Museum || wikimedia
CHAPTER: The Washing of the Feet
John tells us that before the meal, Jesus got up from the table, put aside his outer garment, and wrapped a towel around his waist. Then he poured water into a basin and began to wash his disciples' feet. || John 13:1–5 || text-card
Washing feet was a common courtesy in a world of dusty roads and open sandals. A host might offer water to his guests, or have a servant wash their feet. But it was a task of low status, usually done by the lowest member of a household. || Roman bronze basin and sandals, British Museum || wikimedia
By taking the towel, Jesus takes the place of the servant. John is careful to say that he did this knowing that the Father had given everything into his hands. The image is deliberately striking: the teacher kneeling before his students. || Christ Washing the Disciples' Feet, Ford Madox Brown || wikimedia
Peter objects. In the text, he asks whether Jesus will wash his feet, and protests that he never will. Jesus answers that unless he washes him, Peter has no share with him. Peter then asks for his hands and head as well. || John 13:6–10 || text-card
The scene is full of Peter's character: eager, impulsive, and quick to swing from refusal to excess. It is one of the places in the Gospels where a disciple's reaction teaches as much as the teacher's action. || The Washing of the Feet, Tintoretto || wikimedia
When Jesus has finished, he returns to the table and asks them if they understand what he has done. He calls himself teacher and lord, and says that if he has washed their feet, they should wash one another's feet. || John 13:12–15 || text-card
John calls this an example, a pattern for them to follow. Later Christians would take the command quite literally in some places and more symbolically in others. Some churches still have a foot-washing ceremony on Holy Thursday. || Holy Thursday foot washing ceremony, Rome || wikimedia
Now, here is something that surprises many readers. This scene appears only in John. The Synoptic Gospels, Matthew, Mark and Luke, do not mention it, and John, for his part, has no account of the bread and the cup. || Gospel of John manuscript, Papyrus 66 || wikimedia
That does not mean the authors disagreed. Each Gospel selects. John gives a long evening with a servant's gesture, a series of conversations and a prayer. The others give the bread, the cup and the foretelling of betrayal. || Papyrus 66 John 13 || wikimedia
Scholars have proposed different explanations for John's silence on the bread and cup. Some think the author assumed his readers already knew the meal. Others think he wanted to place his emphasis elsewhere. We simply cannot be sure. || Raymond E. Brown, Gospel According to John commentary || text-card
Luke does include something related. In his account, the disciples dispute about which of them is the greatest, and Jesus answers that he is among them as one who serves. It echoes John's image in a different form. || Luke 22:24–27 || text-card
So across two traditions, a similar message appears: at this table, greatness is redefined. The one who leads is the one who serves. The teacher is on the floor with a basin, not on the couch of honour. || The Last Supper, Duccio, Maestà || wikimedia
There is also a dark note in the scene. John says that Jesus knew who would hand him over, and that was why he said not every one of them was clean. Foot by foot, he works his way around the table. || John 13:10–11 || text-card
Think of what that means in the story. Jesus kneels before every person present, including the one who will betray him, and including the one who will deny him. John does not tell us what each one felt. The silence is part of the power. || Detail of feet washing, Last Supper cycle || wikimedia
This was a meal where the teacher gave an example in the form of an action. In a few moments, he will give another, with bread and a cup, and the table will fall quiet in a different way. But first, a shadow crosses the room. || Rembrandt, Supper at Emmaus study || rijksmuseum
