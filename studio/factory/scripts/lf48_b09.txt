@chapter N23 cafe_sunset | Chills beyond music
Ines read a poem aloud at her sister's wedding last spring, and she remembers that the room went quiet when she reached the last verse. Her sister was crying. Her own arms were covered in goosebumps. And no music was playing at all.

@char N02 living_room_day | Poems and prose | The Wassiliwizky studies
Researchers in Frankfurt have studied exactly this. In 2015 and 2017, Eugen Wassiliwizky and his colleagues at the Max Planck Institute for Empirical Aesthetics asked people to listen to or read poems, and then recorded when they got chills, what happened in their bodies, and what happened in their brains.

@study N07 street_day | Eugen Wassiliwizky and colleagues | Social Cognitive and Affective Neuroscience, 2017 | Listeners to poetry showed goosebumps and brain responses in reward regions during the most moving passages
The finding was that poetry could produce the same kind of response as music. Listeners reported chills and showed goosebumps, and in the scanner, regions of the reward system, including the striatum, became active during the passages that moved them most. The strongest moments weren't usually the most dramatic. They were often passages that were simple, well-turned and perfectly timed.

@char N19 living_room_day | Being moved | A related feeling
A related idea comes from psychologists who study the feeling of being moved. It's a warm, slightly tearful emotion that people describe when they witness an act of kindness, a reunion, or something beautiful. Chills seem to follow it closely. In studies of goosebumps, the most reliable link wasn't with fear or cold, but with being moved.

@char N18 cabin_fireplace | Films, speeches, nature | Many triggers
People report chills in a wide variety of settings. A scene in a film where a long-awaited reunion finally happens. A speech in which a leader's voice breaks. A choir in a cathedral. The first sight of a mountain range at sunrise. A crowd singing together. What these moments share is that something larger than the self comes into view.

@photo lf48_b09_01.jpg | A mountain range at sunrise
Researchers sometimes describe these moments as awe, the feeling of encountering something vast that stretches the way we normally see things. A mountain range, a night sky, a great piece of music. Different experiences, one family of feeling, and the body seems to answer them all with a similar shiver.

@char N20 bedroom_night | The senses beyond sound | Chills from touch, too
There's even evidence that chills can come from other senses. In a 2011 paper, Oliver Grewe and colleagues asked people about frisson triggered by sounds, by sights, by touch and by taste. Many said that they'd felt it in more than one way. A caress on the neck, or a beautiful view, could do something similar to a piece of music.

@char N02 rooftop_dusk | Not the same as ASMR | Another tingle
You may also have heard of ASMR, which stands for autonomous sensory meridian response. People who experience it describe a calming tingling on the scalp and neck, usually triggered by soft sounds like whispering, tapping or the gentle brushing of a surface. It feels similar to chills in some ways, but the feeling is different.

@study N07 cafe_sunset | Giulia Poerio and colleagues | PLoS ONE, 2018 | ASMR videos lowered heart rate and raised skin conductance in people who experience ASMR
In 2018, Giulia Poerio at the University of Sheffield and colleagues compared people who experience ASMR with people who don't. When they watched ASMR videos, those who experienced the tingling showed a small drop in heart rate and a rise in skin conductance, and they reported feeling calm and happy. A calm kind of arousal, quite different from the lift of music chills.

@char N23 living_room_day | Why mention it tonight? | Sleepy sounds
It's worth knowing about because many people use soft sounds and gentle voices to relax at bedtime, and some of them find that these sounds give them a pleasant tingling. If that's you, you're not strange. You're one of a good many people who've found that a quiet sound can make the body soften.

@char N10 autumn_overlook | A common thread | Meaning in the moment
Poems, films, mountain views, whispered sounds. The common thread, as far as researchers can tell, is that the body responds to moments that matter. It uses a similar set of tools, goosebumps and tingles and a catch in the throat, to mark them.

@char N29 cabin_fireplace | Back to music | Why it's so good at it
Music may be especially good at producing these moments because it can combine so many triggers at once. It builds anticipation and delivers surprise. It carries a voice. It touches memory. And it can be shared with other people. We'll look at the voice next, because it may hold the oldest clue of all.
