Imagine we could film Oskar's yawn and play it back very slowly. At first the jaw begins to drop. The breath starts to flow in. The gape grows wider and wider, the eyes narrow, and for a brief moment everything holds at its widest point. Then comes a shorter breath out, the jaw closes, and it's over. It feels like one smooth wave.

@study N22 attic_rain | Robert Provine | University of Maryland, Baltimore County | Years of watching ordinary yawns
One of the people who watched yawns most patiently was Robert Provine, a neuroscientist at the University of Maryland, Baltimore County. He was interested in everyday human behaviours that most researchers walked straight past, such as laughing, hiccupping and yawning. His idea was simple: if something happens to almost everyone, every day, it deserves to be measured properly.

@stat N14 bedroom_night | The length of a yawn | About 6s | on average, in Provine's summary
In an article summing up his work in 2005, Provine described the typical yawn as lasting about six seconds, with the shortest ones around three and a half seconds. That may sound brief, but try counting it out. Six seconds is a long time for your face to be doing something you didn't plan, in the middle of whatever else you were doing.

@char A03 cafe_sunset | They come in clusters | Roughly a minute apart
Provine also noticed that yawns often arrive in little runs. When someone is in a yawning mood, he reported, the gap from one yawn to the next is about sixty-eight seconds on average. Oskar knows this pattern well, even if he has never timed it. One yawn on the train, then another a minute or so later, as though the first had only been an introduction.

@quote N29 living_room_day | A yawn, once under way, tends to run to its end | Provine's description of a fixed pattern
Provine described yawning as a stereotyped action pattern. In plain words, that's a movement that unfolds in much the same way each time, and which tends to carry on to completion once it has started. It's a little like a sneeze. You may be able to disguise it or squeeze it, but stopping it cleanly halfway through is surprisingly hard to do.

@char A12 cabin_fireplace | Try a closed mouth | The hidden yawn
You've probably tried to hide a yawn in a meeting, keeping your lips sealed while the rest of your face struggles. Other people may not see it, but you know it's still happening inside. The throat opens, the nostrils flare, the eyes water. The outside can be disguised, yet the program running underneath seems determined to finish what it began.

@list A10 rooftop_dusk | Two little experiments you may know | Pinch your nose: the yawn usually completes ;; Clench your teeth: it often feels stuck
Provine described two simple tests. If you pinch your nose shut during a yawn, you can usually finish it perfectly well through your mouth. But if you clench your teeth and try to yawn through them, many people find that it feels strange and unfinished. The wide gape of the jaw seems to be a part of the yawn that really matters to the feeling of completing it.

@study N09 autumn_overlook | Timothy Corey, Melanie Shoup-Knox, Elana Gordis and Gordon Gallup | Frontiers in Evolutionary Neuroscience, published 2012 | What the body does around a yawn
What about the inside of the body? Researchers including Timothy Corey and Gordon Gallup looked at physiological recordings taken before, during and after spontaneous yawns. In one part of their work they examined thirty-one yawns that had been captured from twenty-seven people while they were wired up for a different study, so nobody was yawning to order.

@bars A12 street_day | Heart rate around a yawn | beats per minute | Before:81 ;; Peak:93
In those recordings, average heart rate stood at about eighty-one beats a minute before the yawn and rose to a peak of about ninety-three around it. The amount of air in a breath changed even more dramatically, to roughly four times its earlier size. So for those few seconds, a yawn is a small burst of activity, even when it comes over you at your sleepiest.

@char N10 cafe_sunset | A small wake-up inside | Then things settle
The recordings also showed that breathing slowed in the seconds afterwards. Picture a little swell: the body stirs, takes in a great volume of air, the heart quickens, and then everything eases back. Some researchers think this swell is exactly the point, that a yawn gives the system a brief jolt when it's drifting. We should hold that as an idea, not a proven fact.

@char N20 living_room_day | Stretching too | A close relative
Yawns often keep company with stretching, especially first thing in the morning. There's even a long word for that combined stretch and yawn: pandiculation. On the train, Oskar lifts his arms above his head and arches his back as the yawn peaks. It feels good, in a way that's hard to explain, and that pleasant sense of release is something many people report.

@char N01 cabin_fireplace | Why so satisfying? | An honest answer
Why does a full yawn feel satisfying, while an interrupted one feels frustrating? Part of it may simply be that an action which is built to finish feels uncomfortable when it can't. Beyond that, I'd be guessing, and I'd rather not. What we can say is that your body treats the yawn as a whole unit, with a beginning, a middle and an end it wants to reach.

@list N27 rooftop_dusk | What we've seen so far | About six seconds long ;; Often in runs, about a minute apart ;; Hard to stop once started ;; A brief rise in heart rate and breath size
So here's our picture of the thing itself. A yawn lasts around six seconds. It often comes in runs. Once under way, it tends to finish. And in the body, it's accompanied by a short rise in heart rate and a much larger breath. All of that is happening to Oskar as the train rattles over a set of points.

@char A03 autumn_overlook | The obvious explanation | All that air
Now, that huge breath leads almost everyone to the same explanation. You've surely heard it. We yawn, the story goes, because we need more oxygen. The air in a stuffy room runs low, the brain asks for a top-up, and the mouth opens wide to gulp it in. It sounds completely sensible.
