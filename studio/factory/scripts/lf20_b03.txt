@chapter A01 cafe_sunset | Why spacing beats cramming
Every student knows the night before an exam. The coffee, the highlighters, the desperate hours. And every student knows that, somehow, much of it will be gone a week later. It's a pattern as old as school itself. The psychology of learning has a name for the alternative, and a lot of evidence behind it. It's called distributed practice, or the spacing effect.

@char A12 living_room_day | The old finding | Ebbinghaus again
We met the spacing effect briefly in an earlier video, when we talked about Hermann Ebbinghaus and his nonsense syllables. He found, in the 1880s, that spreading study sessions over several days produced better memory than packing the same amount into one block. Since then, it's been tested more often than almost any other finding in psychology.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Cepeda, Pashler, Vul, Wixted & Rohrer | Psychological Bulletin, 2006 | Distributed practice in verbal recall tasks: a review and quantitative synthesis
In 2006, Nicholas Cepeda and colleagues combined the results of three hundred and seventeen experiments, from one hundred and eighty-four articles, published over a century. They found that spaced practice beat massed practice in the great majority of them. It's one of the most reliable effects that researchers have found.

@stat A14 conspiracy_board | Experiments in the review | 317 | from 184 articles, spanning more than a century of research
That's a staggering amount of evidence for a single idea. When so many studies, in different places, with different materials and different people, point the same way, we can be confident that it's real. And the next question is the practical one: how far apart should the sessions be?

@char A04 attic_rain | The practical question | What gap is best?
If spacing is good, would a longer gap always be better? Not quite. If you wait too long, you will have forgotten too much, and you'll have to relearn. If you wait too little, you get little benefit. The best gap depends on how long you need to remember the material.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Cepeda, Vul, Rohrer, Wixted & Pashler | Psychological Science, 2008 | Spacing effects in learning: a temporal ridgeline of optimal retention
Cepeda's team recruited more than thirteen hundred people over the internet, and tested them with different gaps between two study sessions, and different delays before the final test. They found that the best gap was roughly ten to twenty percent of the time until the test. For a test a week away, the best gap is about a day. For a test a year away, it's about three or four weeks.

@list A03 living_room_day | A rough guide to gaps | Test in a week: review after about a day ;; Test in a month: review after about a week ;; Test in a year: review after about a month ;; Longer gaps are better for longer memory
This is a rule of thumb, not a law. But it gives you a place to start. The further off the goal, the further apart your reviews can be, and each review makes the memory last longer.

@chapter A13 rooftop_dusk | A family experiment
One of the most remarkable studies of spacing was done by a family. In 1993, Harry Bahrick, a psychologist at Ohio Wesleyan University, and three members of his family, decided to test how well people could remember foreign vocabulary years later. The participants were the researchers themselves, and the language was Spanish.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Bahrick, Bahrick, Bahrick & Bahrick | Psychological Science, 1993 | Maintenance of foreign language vocabulary and the spacing effect
They learned hundreds of Spanish words, and then practised them in thirteen or twenty-six sessions, with gaps of fourteen, twenty-eight or fifty-six days between sessions. Then they were tested, again and again, for up to eight years afterwards. It's a rare experiment, and it asks for extraordinary patience.

@char A10 attic_rain | The result | Wider gaps, longer memory
The longer the gaps between sessions, the better the memory years later. The group who spaced their sessions fifty-six days apart remembered much more eight years later than the group who practised the same number of sessions at fourteen-day gaps. Wider spacing was a little slower at first, and much better in the long run.

@list A12 living_room_day | What the Bahrick study suggests | Spacing helps memory last for years ;; Longer gaps gave better long-term retention ;; The slower route felt harder but paid off ;; Fewer sessions, well spaced, can beat many crowded ones
There's a quiet encouragement in this. You don't need to study every day for ever. A well-spaced schedule, with long gaps, may preserve what you've learned for many years, with surprisingly little effort in total.

@chapter A12 living_room_day | Why does spacing work?
Researchers have several ideas about why. One is that forgetting is useful. When a gap lets you forget a little, the next review requires effort to retrieve, and that effort strengthens the memory, in the same way that testing does. Spacing and testing are, in a sense, partners.

@char A04 attic_rain | A second idea | Different contexts
Another idea is that, over days, the context changes. Your mood, your surroundings and your thoughts are slightly different each time, so the memory is linked to many cues instead of one. That makes it easier to retrieve in a new situation, such as an exam room, or a conversation with a stranger.

@char A01 cafe_sunset | A third idea | Attention returns
A third idea is that attention is fresher after a gap. In a long session, the mind wanders, and later minutes are less useful. Several short sessions, each with full attention, give you more good minutes than one long, tired one. Nearly all of us have had the experience of reading a page three times without taking anything in.

@list A03 living_room_day | Why spacing works | Effortful retrieval after a gap ;; Memory linked to varied cues ;; Fresh attention at each session ;; Sleep between sessions helps storage
That last one connects to what we've said in earlier videos about sleep. A night's sleep between sessions seems to help the brain to store what you've studied, which gives spaced practice another advantage over cramming.

@chapter A06 cafe_sunset | In the classroom and the app
Is spacing just a laboratory finding? Not at all. It's been tested in classrooms. In one well-known study, Kerry Sobel and colleagues, and later others, found that schoolchildren who reviewed material after a gap did better on later tests than those who reviewed it straight away. The findings in real schools have been encouraging, though less tidy than in the lab.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Carpenter, Cepeda, Rohrer, Kang & Pashler | Educational Psychology Review, 2012 | Using spacing to enhance diverse forms of learning: review of recent research and implications for instruction
This review summarised the evidence that spacing helps not only with word lists, but with mathematics, science concepts, skills and even complex material such as medical training. It also noted that the benefit appears in learners of different ages, from young children to older adults. It's a robust finding, with few exceptions.

@char A12 living_room_day | Apps and algorithms | Spaced repetition
Many language-learning and flashcard apps now use spaced repetition, a system that schedules each card to reappear just as you're about to forget it. The idea goes back to the Austrian educator Sebastian Leitner in the 1970s, who proposed a box system for flashcards. Cards you got right moved to a box reviewed less often, and cards you got wrong came back sooner.

@list A03 living_room_day | A simple Leitner box | Box one: reviewed every day ;; Box two: reviewed every three days ;; Box three: reviewed every week ;; A wrong answer sends the card back to box one
It's an elegant method, and you can do it with a shoebox and some cards. It builds both testing and spacing into one routine, which is probably why it has lasted for fifty years.

@char A04 attic_rain | A caution | Spacing feels worse
The one problem with spacing is that it feels worse. When you return to something after a gap, you've forgotten some of it, and it feels like you're failing. The temptation is to conclude that spacing isn't working, and go back to cramming. That feeling of difficulty is the very sign that it is.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Kornell | Applied Cognitive Psychology, 2009 | Optimising learning using flashcards: spacing is more effective than cramming
Nate Kornell tested this with students learning vocabulary on flashcards. Spacing was better in most cases, but more than half of the students believed that cramming was just as good or better. They'd experienced the short-term advantage, and couldn't see the long-term one. It's a sobering finding about how poorly we judge our own learning.

@list A12 living_room_day | Words to keep from this chapter | Distributed practice ;; Massed practice ;; Spaced repetition ;; Optimal gap
Four terms from this chapter. Distributed practice spreads learning over time, while massed practice packs it into one block. Spaced repetition is the app-based version. And the optimal gap is about a tenth to a fifth of the time until you need the memory.

A shoebox, some dividers and a calendar. It's one of the most low-tech learning systems you could build, and it rests on a century of research. Each card returns just as you're about to forget it, and that return is the lesson.

@char A15 cabin_fireplace | A gentle thought | Slow is fine
There's something comforting in the spacing effect. It says that there's no shame in slowness. A little today, a little in a few days and a little in a few weeks is better than a heroic night of cramming. The tortoise, in this case, has the evidence on her side.

@char A08 cabin_fireplace | Looking back | What spacing taught us
So what did we learn? That three hundred and seventeen experiments support spacing. That the best gap is about a tenth to a fifth of the time you need to remember. That a family of researchers found wide gaps best over eight years. And that spacing feels harder, which is why we resist it.
