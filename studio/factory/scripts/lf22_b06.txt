@chapter A13 rooftop_dusk | Myth six: a look is just a look
Think of a time someone held your gaze for just a little longer than usual. Maybe across a table, maybe across a room. Perhaps it felt warm. Perhaps it felt uncomfortable. Either way, you noticed it. It's remarkable how much a single look can carry, without a single word being spoken.

@char A10 cabin_fireplace | From the very start | Babies and eyes
Our sensitivity to eyes starts astonishingly early. In 2002, Teresa Farroni and her colleagues showed newborn babies, just a few days old, pairs of photographs of faces. In one, the eyes looked straight ahead at the baby. In the other, the eyes looked away. The newborns tended to look longer at the faces that seemed to be looking at them.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Farroni, Csibra, Simion & Johnson | PNAS, 2002 | Eye contact detection in humans from birth
The researchers published their findings in the journal PNAS. Within days of being born, before they had learned almost anything about the world, babies already seemed drawn to eye contact. It's as if we come into the world ready to look for someone looking back.

@char A12 living_room_day | A lifelong signal | Looking means attention
That early sensitivity stays with us for life. When someone looks at us, we know we have their attention. And attention, for a social species like ours, is precious. It can mean interest, warmth, curiosity, or sometimes threat. Which is why a look can feel so intense.

@chapter A15 cafe_sunset | Two minutes of gazing
So what happens when two strangers are asked to look into each other's eyes on purpose? A study from the late 1980s tried exactly that.

@study A04 attic_rain | Kellerman, Lewis & Laird | Journal of Research in Personality, 1989 | Looking and loving: the effects of mutual gaze on feelings of romantic love
In 1989, Joan Kellerman, James Lewis and James Laird paired up men and women who were strangers and asked them to spend two minutes doing different things. Some pairs gazed into each other's eyes. Others looked at each other's hands, or one person looked at the other's eyes while the other looked away.

@char A03 cafe_sunset | The result | Mutual gaze, warmer feelings
Afterwards, the pairs who had gazed into each other's eyes reported stronger feelings of affection and liking for their partner than the pairs in the other conditions. Two minutes of mutual eye contact with a stranger was enough to produce a small but noticeable shift in how they felt.

@char A13 rooftop_dusk | Holding it lightly | A small, older study
It's worth holding this lightly. It was a fairly small study, carried out in one place a long time ago, and the feelings it measured were modest and short-lived. Nobody fell in love in two minutes. But it fits a much broader pattern. Mutual gaze is a powerful social signal, and sharing it can create a feeling of closeness.

@chapter A12 living_room_day | How long feels right?
So if eye contact can bring people closer, more must be better, right? Not quite. Anyone who's been stared at for too long knows there's a limit. A group of researchers in London set out to measure where that limit is.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Binetti, Harrison, Coutrot, Johnston & Mareschal | Royal Society Open Science, 2016 | Pupil dilation as an index of preferred mutual gaze duration
In 2016, Nicola Binetti and his colleagues invited visitors to the Science Museum in London to watch short video clips of an actor looking straight out at them for different lengths of time. After each clip, the visitors said whether the eye contact had felt too short or too long.

@stat A12 cafe_sunset | Preferred length of eye contact | 3.3 seconds | the average comfortable duration, with most people preferring between about 2 and 5 seconds
On average, the duration people found most comfortable was a little over three seconds. Most people preferred somewhere between about two and five seconds. Beyond that, eye contact with a stranger started to feel uncomfortable for many people. And the researchers found that people's pupils tended to widen more quickly when they preferred longer eye contact.

@char A03 cafe_sunset | A delicate dance | Looking and looking away
So a comfortable gaze is a kind of dance. We look, we look away, we look back again. Holding someone's eyes for a few seconds says, I'm with you. Holding it much longer, with someone we don't know well, can start to feel like a challenge. With people we're close to, of course, those limits can relax a great deal.

@chapter A15 cabin_fireplace | What our pupils give away
And that brings us to one of the most curious findings about eyes. Our pupils, the dark centres of our eyes, may be giving away more than we realise.

@study A04 attic_rain | Eckhard Hess | Scientific American, 1965 | Attitude and pupil size
In the 1960s, the psychologist Eckhard Hess at the University of Chicago became fascinated by pupils. In one well-known demonstration, he showed men two photographs of the same young woman. The pictures were identical, except that in one of them, her pupils had been retouched to look larger.

@char A12 living_room_day | Bigger pupils | Seen as warmer
Many of the men described the woman with larger pupils as more attractive, softer or more feminine. Yet hardly any of them noticed that her pupils were different. Something about those wider pupils seemed to make her more appealing, without anyone consciously knowing why.

@char A10 attic_rain | Why it might matter | Interest shows in the eyes
Hess also reported that pupils tend to widen when people look at something they find interesting or appealing. Later research has shown that pupils respond to many things, including light, effort and emotional arousal, so they're not a simple attraction meter. But it's fascinating to think that when we look at someone we're drawn to, our eyes may be quietly signalling it, and they may be quietly picking up on that signal too.

@chapter A13 rooftop_dusk | A real smile
There's one more thing the eyes do that we notice without knowing it. They tell us whether a smile is real.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Ekman, Davidson & Friesen | Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 1990 | The Duchenne smile: emotional expression and brain physiology
In the 1860s, the French physician Guillaume Duchenne noticed that genuine smiles of enjoyment involve not just the mouth, but also the muscles around the eyes, creating the small creases sometimes called crow's feet. In 1990, Paul Ekman, Richard Davidson and Wallace Friesen found that these smiles, which they named Duchenne smiles, went along with patterns of brain activity linked to enjoyment, more than other smiles did.

@photo lf22_b06_01.jpg | Smiling with the eyes
It's why we sometimes say someone smiles with their eyes. A polite smile can come from the mouth alone. But a smile of real warmth tends to reach all the way up to the eyes. And most of us can sense the difference, even if we couldn't explain what we're noticing.

@list A12 living_room_day | What eyes tell us | Newborns already seek eye contact ;; Mutual gaze can create closeness ;; Around three seconds feels comfortable with strangers ;; Pupils may signal interest ;; Real smiles reach the eyes
So, is a look just a look? Not at all. From the first days of life, we're drawn to eyes that look back at us. A shared gaze can bring people closer. A few seconds feels right with someone new. Our pupils may give away our interest. And a real smile reaches all the way up to the eyes.

@char A13 rooftop_dusk | Not everyone finds it easy | And that's alright
It's also worth saying that not everyone finds eye contact comfortable, and that's completely alright. Some people, including many autistic people, find direct eye contact intense or even distracting, and listen best while looking elsewhere. Connection doesn't depend on any one signal. Attention can be shown in many ways, through words, through warmth, through simply being there.

@char A10 cabin_fireplace | A gentle invitation | Look a little longer
Perhaps the next time you're talking with someone you care about, you could try something simple. Look at them a little more while they speak. Not staring, just letting your eyes rest on them warmly. It's one of the quietest ways there is to say, I'm here, and I'm listening.
