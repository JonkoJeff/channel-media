@chapter A12 cafe_sunset | Do words colour your world?
So colour is something your brain makes, not something that sits on the surface of things. That raises a gentle, curious question. If the brain is doing the painting, could the words you know change the paint it uses?

@char A13 living_room_day | A common belief | Words are just labels
Most of us assume that colour words are simple labels. The colours are out there, already sorted, and every language just sticks its own name tags on them. Blue is blue, whether you call it blue, bleu or azul. It feels obvious. But some languages divide the colour wheel in surprising places.

@list A03 autumn_overlook | Two blues in Russian | Goluboy: lighter blue ;; Siniy: darker blue ;; Two separate basic words ;; Like red and pink for English speakers
Russian is a lovely example. Where English has one everyday word, blue, Russian has two. Goluboy means a lighter blue, and siniy means a darker blue. They aren't shades of one colour the way we might say light blue and navy. For a Russian speaker, they feel more like two different colours, a bit like red and pink feel to an English speaker.

@char A19 cabin_fireplace | The question | Does it change seeing?
So here is the question researchers asked. If your language insists on a line between two blues, does your brain actually see that line more sharply? Or is it only a matter of naming, with no effect on seeing at all?

@study A14 street_day | Winawer, Witthoft, Frank, Wu, Wade & Boroditsky | Stanford and MIT, published 2007 | Russian and English speakers matched blue squares; Russians were faster when the two blues fell on different sides of the goluboy-siniy line
In 2007, a team led by Jonathan Winawer and Lera Boroditsky published a study in the journal PNAS, called Russian blues reveal effects of language on color discrimination. They showed Russian and English speakers three blue squares at a time. The task was simple: which of the two bottom squares exactly matches the top one?

@char A10 rooftop_dusk | The twist | A faster eye
The clever part was in the choice of blues. Sometimes the two squares fell on the same side of the Russian line, both goluboy or both siniy. Sometimes they fell on opposite sides. Russian speakers were faster when the two blues crossed their language's line. English speakers, who have no such line, showed no such advantage.

@chapter A02 street_day | The disappearing advantage
And then came the part that tells us something about how the brain does this.

@list A18 cafe_sunset | When the advantage vanished | Repeating numbers silently ;; A verbal task keeps words busy ;; The Russian speed-up disappeared ;; A spatial task did not remove it
While doing the colour task, people sometimes had to hold a string of numbers in mind, silently rehearsing them. That keeps the language system busy. Under that verbal load, the Russian speakers' advantage disappeared. But when they did a spatial task instead, one that doesn't use words, the advantage stayed.

@char A13 attic_rain | What it means | Words help in the moment
That suggests the words were helping in real time. Somewhere in the fraction of a second it takes to compare two blues, the brain was quietly consulting its vocabulary. The name wasn't just a label stuck on afterwards. It was part of the seeing.

@quote A03 living_room_day | The words we know can lend a hand to the eyes we see with. | A gentle way to put it
It's worth being careful here. Russian speakers don't live in a different colour universe. They can see the same blues English speakers see. The effect is small and measured in fractions of a second. But it's real, and it hints that perception and language are not as separate as they seem.

@photo lf30_b05_01.jpg | Where does one blue end?
Picture a row of tiles, sliding from the palest sky blue to a deep navy. Where exactly does one colour stop and the next begin? Nature doesn't draw the line. Your brain does, and your language may be holding the pencil.

@chapter A05 autumn_overlook | Not everyone sees the same colours
So far we've talked about how the same eyes can be nudged by words. But there's an even simpler fact: not all eyes are built the same.

@char A19 cabin_fireplace | Three kinds of cones | The colour sensors
Most people have three kinds of colour-sensing cells in the retina, called cones. Roughly speaking, one kind responds best to longer wavelengths, one to medium wavelengths, and one to shorter ones. Your brain doesn't receive colours. It receives a comparison between how strongly these three kinds of cells are firing, and from that comparison, it builds every colour you've ever seen.

@stat A12 living_room_day | Colour vision deficiency | 1 in 12 | men (about 1 in 200 women)
When one kind of cone works differently, the comparison changes. Colour vision deficiency, often called colour blindness, affects about one in twelve men and about one in two hundred women. It's more common in men because the genes for the red and green sensitive cones sit on the X chromosome, and men have only one X.

@char A20 rooftop_dusk | Not black and white | A different palette
Most people with colour vision deficiency don't see in black and white at all. They see colour, but some pairs that look very different to others, a red and a green, say, can look surprisingly alike. Many only find out as children, when a teacher notices them colouring the grass brown or the sky purple.

@list A10 cafe_sunset | What a different palette can mean | Some reds and greens look alike ;; Ripe and unripe fruit harder to tell ;; Traffic lights read by position ;; Some camouflage easier to spot
A different palette can be tricky in some ways. Some fruit is harder to judge for ripeness. Traffic lights are read by position rather than colour. But there have also been reports that people with red green colour deficiency can be better at spotting some kinds of camouflage, because they aren't distracted by colour differences and notice texture and shape instead.

@study A11 street_day | Morgan, Adam & Mollon | Cambridge, published 1992 | People with red-green colour deficiency picked out shapes hidden by colour camouflage that most people missed
One of those reports came from Cambridge in 1992. Michael Morgan, Alison Adam and John Mollon published a study in the Proceedings of the Royal Society B. They made patterns where a shape was hidden by a confusing patchwork of colours. People with typical colour vision were thrown off by the colours. People with red green deficiency, who weren't distracted by them, were better at picking out the hidden shape. A different palette is not just a missing piece. Sometimes it's a different strength.

@chapter A16 autumn_overlook | A fourth kind of cone
And now for one of the most intriguing ideas in colour science. If some people see fewer distinctions, could some people see more?

@char A18 living_room_day | The genetics | A possible fourth cone
Because those cone genes sit on the X chromosome, and women have two X chromosomes, some women carry two slightly different versions of the gene for one kind of cone. In principle, that could give them four kinds of cones instead of three. Scientists have wondered for decades whether any of these women actually use that fourth cone to see extra colours.

@study A14 cafe_sunset | Jordan, Deeb, Bosten & Mollon | Newcastle and Cambridge, published 2010 | Tested 24 women who carry a gene for a fourth cone type; one of them behaved like a true tetrachromat
In 2010, Gabriele Jordan and her colleagues published a study in the Journal of Vision. They tested twenty-four women who carry this extra cone gene, using careful colour-matching tasks designed to reveal a fourth dimension of colour.

@char A06 rooftop_dusk | One in twenty-four | A rare finding
Most of the women didn't show it. Having the gene wasn't enough. But one woman, known in the paper only by a code, made colour matches that only make sense if her brain was really using four kinds of cones. She seemed to see differences between mixtures of light that look identical to almost everyone else.

#ai lf30_b05_02.jpg | Editorial still-life of a glass prism on a white linen cloth casting a soft rainbow of light across the fabric, gentle morning window light, calm and quiet mood, no people, no text, no logos

@photo lf30_b05_02.jpg | The same light, different worlds
It's a humbling thought. The same beam of light can land on two pairs of eyes and become two slightly different experiences. One person sees two identical shades. Another, sitting right beside them, sees two.

@list A03 autumn_overlook | Colour, tested | Colour words can speed up seeing ;; Cone types differ between people ;; Some see fewer distinctions ;; A rare few may see more
So let's gather this up. Colour words are not just labels. In small ways they can help the brain draw lines in real time. And even before language gets involved, eyes differ. Some people see fewer distinctions, and a rare few may see more. The colours you see are yours, built by your eyes, your brain and even your words.

@char A13 cabin_fireplace | A bigger surprise | Missing what's right there
But all of this is about the details of what we see. What about the big things, the obvious things, the things right in the middle of the view? Surely, if something large and strange walked right in front of you, you would notice. That belief is next, and it turns out to be one of the most surprising of all.
