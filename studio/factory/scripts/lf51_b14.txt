@chapter N27 cabin_fireplace | Not all boredom is the same
So far we've talked about boredom as though it were one thing. But as you might have noticed in your own life, it comes in different moods. The boredom of a dull lecture is not quite the same as the boredom of a rainy Sunday, or the boredom of a job that has stopped being a challenge.

@char N10 living_room_day | A study | Five kinds of afternoon
In 2014, a research team led by Thomas Goetz, with Anne Frenzel, Nathan Hall, Ulrike Nett, Reinhard Pekrun and Anastasiya Lipnevich, published a paper in the journal Motivation and Emotion called Types of boredom: An experience sampling approach. They asked students to record how they felt, at random moments, in their daily lives.

@char N19 cafe_sunset | What they found | Four flavours
By analysing the students' answers, the team identified several distinct kinds of boredom. They called them indifferent boredom, calibrating boredom, searching boredom and reactant boredom. The differences lay in how pleasant the feeling was, and how restless the person felt.

@list N11 cafe_sunset | Four flavours of boredom | Indifferent: calm, withdrawn, a little relaxed ;; Calibrating: drifting, open to anything that comes ;; Searching: restless, actively looking for change ;; Reactant: agitated, wanting to escape right now
Here is how they described them. Indifferent boredom is calm and withdrawn, a bit like drifting in neutral. Calibrating boredom is a drifting state in which the mind is open to anything that turns up. Searching boredom is restless and actively looking for a way out. And reactant boredom is agitated, with a strong urge to escape right away.

@char N09 living_room_day | Why it matters | Different responses
Why bother with the differences? Because a different flavour calls for a different response. Someone in indifferent boredom may need very little, perhaps a quiet pause. Someone in searching boredom might need a new activity. And someone in reactant boredom may be better off simply leaving, or taking a break, before frustration builds.

@char N02 rooftop_dusk | A reminder | Tuning in
That's a nice reminder that boredom isn't just one itch to be scratched in the same way every time. When it shows up, it can help to ask which kind it is. Am I calm and a bit empty, or am I restless and fed up? The honest answer often points toward what would help.

@char N10 cabin_fireplace | Another review | The knowns and the unknowns
In 2018, Quentin Raffaelli, Rudy Mills and Kalina Christoff published a review in Experimental Brain Research, with the title, The knowns and unknowns of boredom. They pointed out that although boredom had been studied for decades, many basic questions remained, including how it is best defined and what exactly happens in the brain. That's a good reminder that the field is young.

@char N19 living_room_day | Who gets bored? | A difference between people
People differ quite a lot in how easily they get bored. We mentioned the boredom proneness scales earlier, and the shorter version published in 2017 by Struk and colleagues in the journal Assessment. Some people score low and rarely feel it, while others score high and feel it often, across many situations.

@char N09 cafe_sunset | An interesting finding | Trouble getting started
In 2018, a team led by Danilo Mugon, with Andriy Struk and James Danckert, published a paper called A failure to launch: Regulatory modes and boredom proneness in the journal Frontiers in Psychology. They reported that people who were more prone to boredom tended to score lower on a measure of what's called locomotion, which is the drive to get going and make progress on things.

@char N23 rooftop_dusk | What that suggests | The itch without the push
That's an intriguing idea. It suggests that for some people, the trouble isn't that nothing is interesting. It's that the step from the wanting to the doing is harder. They feel the itch, but not the push that turns it into action. If that's the case, a very small first step may matter more than a grand plan.

@char N10 living_room_day | A caution | Correlation again
As with most of this research, it's correlational, so it can't tell us what causes what. And it's about averages. Plenty of people who are bored often are full of drive, and plenty who rarely feel bored don't get much done. It's one piece of a big and complicated puzzle.

@char N19 cabin_fireplace | Another factor | The situation matters too
It's also easy to forget that boredom depends on the situation, not only the person. A very engaged person can be bored in a poorly designed meeting. A very restless person can be absorbed in a project they love. Context, task and meaning all shape the feeling, in addition to personality.

@char N02 rooftop_dusk | A matter of fairness | Some situations are bleak
Some situations are bleak in ways that no change of attitude would fix. People who are stuck in long, repetitive jobs, with little say in how they spend their time, or people who are isolated and have few options, may have good reason to feel bored. In those cases, the signal is telling the truth, and the right response is to change the situation where possible.

@char N09 living_room_day | Boredom and mood | A careful line
We also want to be careful on a sensitive point. Occasional boredom is normal. But a flat, empty feeling that lasts for weeks, that makes it hard to enjoy anything, or that comes with low mood, is not something to try to fix with a hobby. It's worth talking about with a doctor or a mental health professional.

@char N10 cafe_sunset | A distinction | Boredom wants, flatness doesn't
One way to tell the difference, as the 2011 Goldberg study suggested, is that boredom wants. It's restless, it's looking for something. Depression and apathy tend to be flatter, where the wanting itself has faded. If you can't tell which one you're feeling, that's reason enough to ask someone.

@char N19 rooftop_dusk | A thought | Kindness first
Whatever kind of boredom you feel, it helps to meet it kindly. Many of us criticise ourselves for being bored, as though it showed a lack of imagination or gratitude. But the research suggests it's an ordinary, useful signal, and that it deserves the same patience we'd give to hunger or tiredness.

@char N27 cabin_fireplace | Back to Mira | Which kind is hers?
In the waiting room, Mira's boredom began as the reactant kind, a tight, irritable wish to be anywhere else. As the minutes passed, it softened into a calibrating drift, and then into something almost pleasant, a loose, open wondering. The feeling hadn't ended, but it had changed.

@char N10 living_room_day | What that means | Boredom isn't fixed
That's a quiet but important point. Boredom is not a fixed state that we simply endure. It moves, and it responds to what we do, even a little. Sometimes the best thing we can do is give it time, and some kind attention, and watch it turn.

Now that we know what boredom is, where it comes from and what it might be for, it's time to ask the practical question. What can we actually do with it?
