Let's start with what we're actually trying to explain. Déjà vu is a feeling of familiarity that doesn't belong in the situation. Elena knows, logically, that she has never been in this hallway. @char N19 living_room_day | The name | Émile Boirac, 1876
The phrase was given its modern meaning by a French scholar named Émile Boirac. In 1876, he wrote a letter to a philosophical journal, the Revue philosophique, describing the feeling and giving it the name déjà vu. Before then, people had noticed the experience for centuries, but they used a wide range of other words for it, and many of them blamed it on illness or on the supernatural.

@char N02 cafe_sunset | The key ingredient | Aware that it's wrong
One detail matters a great deal. In ordinary déjà vu, the person knows that the feeling is mistaken. They don't truly believe they have lived this exact moment before. They just feel as though they have. Psychologists treat that awareness as a defining feature, because it separates the everyday kind of déjà vu from more serious conditions, in which a person genuinely believes that they are reliving an event.

@char N09 living_room_day | A researcher's definition | The usual way to put it
Researchers who study the experience usually define it in a few plain words. Déjà vu is a subjective sense of familiarity with the present situation, together with the knowledge that the situation has not actually been experienced before. Alan Brown, a psychologist who wrote one of the standard books on the subject, called it a puzzling mismatch between a feeling and a fact.

@char N10 cafe_sunset | The cousins | Déjà vécu and déjà visité
There are some close relatives of the feeling, and researchers use separate words for them. Déjà vécu means already lived, a stronger sense of having been through the whole situation before. Déjà visité means already visited, the sense that you know a place you've never seen. And déjà entendu means already heard, which is most often used for conversations and sounds.

@char N19 street_day | Why the French | A borrowed phrase
It's curious that English-speaking scientists still use a French phrase. The reason is partly historical, since Boirac's letter set the term in the literature, and partly practical, because no English phrase has quite the same shape. Over the decades, the term has spread to almost every language, and it's now one of the few psychological phrases that a stranger in any country is likely to understand.

@char N23 rooftop_dusk | A first puzzle | Why so specific?
Here's a first puzzle to take away. If déjà vu were just a vague sense of knowing, it would be easy to wave away. But people often describe it as sharply detailed, as if they could see the whole scene already laid out, with every object in its place. Elena, for example, felt that she knew the window and the mug before she saw them. Where could such a precise feeling come from, if she has never been here?

@char N10 cabin_fireplace | Three possible answers | Where the feeling might come from
Broadly, scientists have offered three kinds of answer. The first says the feeling is a memory, a very real one, that the person can't identify. The second says it's an error in the brain's machinery for judging familiarity, a kind of false alarm. And the third says it's a temporary mismatch between different parts of the brain, which disagree for a moment about whether the scene is old or new.

@char N09 living_room_day | A gentle thought | Nothing mystical needed
If it ever worries you that déjà vu feels uncanny, take comfort in this. Almost everything researchers have found points to the mundane, though wonderfully intricate, workings of an ordinary memory system. It means that Elena's hallway moment isn't a warning, and isn't a message. It's the brain doing something it does constantly, but at the wrong time, and noticing itself in the act.
