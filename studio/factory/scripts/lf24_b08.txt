@chapter A10 attic_rain | The cost of interruption
Let's leave mood for a while, and look at attention. A notification buzzes. You glance at it, perhaps you answer, and then you return to what you were doing. It feels like a small, free interruption. Psychologists who study attention have spent years asking what these interruptions actually cost, and their answers are more surprising than you might expect.

@char A12 living_room_day | The setting | Watching people work
One of the best known researchers in this area is Gloria Mark, a professor of informatics at the University of California, Irvine. Her approach is unusual. Instead of asking people how they work, she watches them, sometimes with a stopwatch, sometimes by logging their screens, and records exactly how often they switch tasks.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Mark, Gudith & Klocke | Proceedings of the SIGCHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems, 2008 | The cost of interrupted work: more speed and stress
In one experiment, people did writing tasks and were interrupted partway through, while others worked without interruptions. Those who were interrupted finished their tasks just as fast, or faster, but they reported more stress, frustration, time pressure and effort. In other words, people could compensate for the interruption, but it came with a psychological cost.

@char A04 attic_rain | A hidden bill | Speed paid for with stress
This is a nice example of an effect that doesn't show up in the output, only in the person. The work gets done, so the interruption seems harmless. But the person had to run faster to catch up, and pays for it in tension. Over a day, or a year, such costs may add up, even if no single moment shows them.

@stat A14 conspiracy_board | Time to return after interruption | About 23 min | a figure Gloria Mark has described from her field observations of office workers
Mark has also reported, from observing people in real workplaces, that after a significant interruption it can take on the order of twenty minutes to return to the original task. That figure comes from observations rather than a single controlled experiment, so it's best taken as a rough illustration, not a law. The point is that interruptions don't end when the interruption does.

@chapter A13 rooftop_dusk | Shrinking attention spans?
Mark has also tracked how long people stay on one screen before switching. In her studies, the average time on a single screen dropped over the years, from about two and a half minutes in 2004 to roughly forty-seven seconds in more recent measurements. Those figures come from her own samples of office workers, so they don't describe everyone, but the trend is striking.

@char A01 cafe_sunset | A cautious interpretation | Switching, not shrinking
It's important to be careful with the phrase attention span. Researchers don't have good evidence that human beings are physically losing the ability to concentrate. A more accurate description is that people are switching more often, between tabs, apps and tasks, and that frequent switching is a habit, shaped by the environment, which could be changed.

@list A12 living_room_day | What we can and can't say | People switch screens more often than they used to ;; Frequent switching is linked to more stress ;; Interruptions carry costs, even when work gets done ;; There's no clear proof that attention capacity is shrinking
That last line matters. A popular claim says that people now have an attention span shorter than a goldfish. That claim has no good scientific basis, and it has been traced to a poorly sourced marketing report. It makes for a good headline, but not a true one.

@chapter A12 living_room_day | The phone that doesn't ring
Now to a more surprising claim. Some researchers have suggested that the mere presence of a phone, even switched off and untouched, might reduce your thinking capacity. In 2017, a team led by Adrian Ward at the University of Texas published a study that was widely reported, and is a good case of a finding that needs a second look.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Ward, Duke, Gneezy & Bos | Journal of the Association for Consumer Research, 2017 | Brain drain: the mere presence of one's own smartphone reduces available cognitive capacity
They asked five hundred and twenty students to take attention and reasoning tests, with their phones either on the desk, in a pocket or bag, or in another room. Those with the phone on the desk performed worst on some tests of cognitive capacity, and those with the phone in another room did best. They called it the brain drain effect: the phone, they said, takes up part of your mental resources, even when you aren't using it.

@stat A14 conspiracy_board | Students in the brain drain study | 520 | tested with their phone on the desk, in a bag, or in another room
It sounded like a clear and useful finding, and it spread widely. But science moves on. Later teams tried to repeat the study, with larger groups and stricter methods, and several did not find the same pattern. A large replication in 2022 found no reliable effect of the phone's presence on cognitive performance, and the original result is now regarded as uncertain.

@char A04 attic_rain | A cautionary tale | Appealing and unproven
This is the same story we saw with power posing. An effect that seems intuitive and is easy to explain spreads quickly, and the replication comes quietly, years later. It doesn't mean the idea is definitely false. It means that we shouldn't treat it as established, and should be a little suspicious of results that fit our expectations perfectly.

@chapter A06 cafe_sunset | Notifications themselves
There is stronger evidence on the notifications themselves. In 2015, Cary Stothart, Ainsley Mitchum and Courtney Yehnert at Florida State University asked people to do a task that required sustained attention. Some received a phone notification, an alert or a call, during the task, which they were told to ignore.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Stothart, Mitchum & Yehnert | Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Perception and Performance, 2015 | The attentional cost of receiving a cell phone notification
The people who got a notification made more mistakes on the task than those who didn't, even though they hadn't looked at the phone. Simply being alerted was enough to distract them for a while, with performance sagging in a way that was comparable to people who actually used their phones. The size of the effect was modest, but it was consistent.

@list A03 living_room_day | What notifications seem to do | Pull attention even when ignored ;; Increase mistakes on demanding tasks ;; Fragment work into shorter chunks ;; Cost more when the task is difficult
The study gives a practical reason to turn off alerts that don't matter. Not because phones are evil, but because an alert acts like someone tapping your shoulder, and it can take time to find your place again.

@photo lf24_b08_01.jpg | A desk and an hourglass
A quiet desk, an hourglass, and a phone placed face down at the edge. The research on interruptions suggests that where you put the phone matters less than whether it can reach you, and that a protected stretch of time is one of the most valuable things you can give your work.

@list A03 living_room_day | Protecting a stretch of deep attention | Silence notifications for one hour ;; Put the phone in another room ;; Work in one window ;; Write down distracting thoughts and return to them later
To protect your attention, start with a single hour. Silence the notifications, close the extra tabs, and place the phone somewhere that takes effort to reach. If a stray thought arrives, such as an errand or a message to send, write it on a piece of paper and return to the task. That gives the thought a home, so that your mind doesn't have to hold it. Researchers on attention suggest that the first ten minutes are the hardest, and that things get easier after that. One good hour often outweighs several scattered ones.

@list A12 living_room_day | Words to keep from this chapter | Task switching ;; Attention residue ;; Sustained attention ;; Brain drain claim
Four terms about attention. Task switching is moving between tasks or screens. Attention residue is the part of your mind that stays on the last task after you switch. Sustained attention is the ability to stay focused over time. And the brain drain claim is the idea that a phone's mere presence uses up thinking capacity, which has not held up.

@char A15 cabin_fireplace | A gentle thought | Guard a small piece of time
You don't have to give up your phone to protect your attention. A single hour of quiet, with alerts silenced, is a good start. Many people find that one deep hour does more than four scattered ones. It's a gift to your future self, and it's one the research gives support for.

@char A08 cabin_fireplace | Looking back | What attention taught us
So what did we learn? That interruptions cost stress even when work is completed. That people switch screens more often, though we can't say that attention spans are shrinking. That the phone-in-the-room effect failed to replicate. And that even ignored notifications can raise errors.
