@chapter N27 rooftop_dusk | A zoo full of yawns
Oskar opens an old nature photo on his phone, one he took on a school trip: a hippopotamus with its mouth wide open, filling half the frame. His pupils called it the biggest yawn in the world. Whether it truly was a yawn or a warning is another matter, but it raises a nice question. Do different animals yawn for different lengths of time?

@char A03 autumn_overlook | Why length might matter | A prediction
Here's why that question is interesting. If a yawn does something for the brain, such as moving blood or shedding heat, then a larger brain might need a bigger or longer yawn to get the same effect. That gives researchers another prediction to test, one that doesn't depend on asking any person how they feel. All it needs is a camera and a stopwatch.

@study A10 attic_rain | Jorg Massen, Andrew Gallup and a large team | Communications Biology, published 2021 | Yawn duration and brain size
In 2021, Jorg Massen, Andrew Gallup and a team of colleagues published a very large comparison in the journal Communications Biology. They gathered video of animals yawning, from zoo visits and other sources, and measured how long each yawn lasted. Then they lined those durations up against what's known about the brains of each species.

@stat N09 bedroom_night | The size of the collection | 101 | species, 55 mammals and 46 birds
The scale is impressive. The analysis covered one thousand two hundred and ninety-one yawns from six hundred and ninety-seven individual animals, belonging to a hundred and one species. Fifty-five of those species were mammals and forty-six were birds. That's an enormous amount of patient watching, frame by frame, of creatures who had no idea they were contributing to science.

@char N29 living_room_day | Spotting a yawn | Not every open mouth
Counting animal yawns takes care. An open mouth can be a threat, a call, a pant or a bite of food. So researchers look for that same lopsided shape we met in the womb: a slow opening, a peak, and a quicker closing, without the signs of aggression or vocal calling that would suggest something else is going on.

@bars A12 cafe_sunset | Average yawn length | seconds | Mammals:3 ;; Birds:1
The first thing that stood out was a difference between the two great groups. Mammal yawns averaged about three point four seconds. Bird yawns averaged about one and a half seconds. The bars here are rounded to whole numbers, but you can see the gap: on average, a mammal's yawn lasted more than twice as long as a bird's.

@char N10 cabin_fireplace | The main finding | Bigger brains, longer yawns
And then the central result, drawn from those hundred and one species. Within both mammals and birds, species with larger brains tended to have longer yawns. The same was reported for the number of nerve cells in the brain: more neurons went along with longer yawning. The link remained visible when body size was taken into account, though it was then weaker and less certain, especially for some of the estimates.

@photo lf54_b07_01.jpg | A bird dozing on a branch at dusk
A small bird fluffed up on a branch as the light fades. Watch a resting bird for long enough and you may see it open its beak in a quick, silent gape, then settle again. Its yawn is over almost before you've noticed. A lion lying nearby would take much longer about the same business.

@char N20 rooftop_dusk | Body size is tricky | Disentangling the two
Why does body size need accounting for? Because big animals usually have big brains, and big jaws too, and big jaws simply take longer to open and close. If you didn't separate these things, you might mistake a slow, heavy jaw for a clever brain. The team used statistical models to pull them apart, and that's also where the picture becomes more cautious.

@char N01 autumn_overlook | What the finding supports | Consistent, not conclusive
The authors argued that the pattern fits the idea that yawning serves the brain, with larger brains calling for longer yawns. It does fit. But it's a correlation across species, and correlations can have more than one explanation. It tells us that yawn length and brain size travel together across a wide sample of animals. It doesn't show us the machinery that connects them.

@char A03 bedroom_night | Your pet is not a score | A gentle warning
It would be a mistake to time your cat's yawn and draw conclusions about its intelligence. This finding is about averages across species, and a single yawn from a single animal varies for all sorts of reasons. The dog who yawns slowly isn't necessarily a deep thinker. Oskar's dog Pepper, bless him, yawns magnificently and still barks at the kettle.

@list N10 attic_rain | Comparing animals can show | Which features travel together ;; How old a behaviour might be ;; Which ideas make successful predictions ;; But not, by itself, what causes what
Comparisons across species are a powerful tool. They can show which features tend to travel together, hint at how old a behaviour is, and let a hypothesis stick its neck out with a prediction. What they can't easily do alone is prove cause. For that, researchers need other kinds of evidence to meet this one halfway.

@char N27 living_room_day | A shared inheritance | Birds and mammals together
It's quietly remarkable that you can study yawning across birds and mammals with one measuring stick at all. These two groups have been on separate evolutionary paths for a very long time. Yet both open wide in a similar rhythm. That similarity is one reason many scientists think the yawn is ancient, or at least that it answers a need which many brains share.

@char N29 cabin_fireplace | A yawn at the window | The countryside outside
Outside the train window it's too dark to see animals now, but Oskar knows they're out there: cattle lying in fields, birds roosting in hedges, a fox perhaps, stretching at the start of its night. Many of them will yawn before morning, quietly, with nobody watching. Spontaneous yawning doesn't need an audience. It carries on in the dark.

@char A12 rooftop_dusk | But then there's the audience | Back to the carriage
And yet some yawns clearly do seem to care about an audience, or at least respond to one. A student has just got on the train, sat down opposite Oskar and, within a minute, given an enormous yawn. Oskar feels it arrive in his own jaw almost at once. We've spent a long while on the private yawn. It's time for the shared one.

@list A10 cafe_sunset | So far, on purpose and function | Oxygen: the direct test found no support ;; Arousal: fits the timing, effects look brief ;; Cooling: supporting studies, still debated ;; Signalling: still to come
Before we cross over, a quick tally of purposes. The oxygen idea found no support when tested. The arousal idea fits when people yawn, though the measured effects are short. The cooling idea has several supporting observations, including longer yawns in bigger-brained species, and remains debated. Signalling to others we'll meet properly very soon.

@char N20 autumn_overlook | Why contagion is its own puzzle | Two separate questions
Notice that none of those ideas, by itself, explains why you should yawn when someone else does. Your brain's temperature doesn't rise because a stranger opens her mouth. You didn't suddenly wake or tire. So contagion needs its own account. Something about perceiving the act has to reach into the system that produces it.

@char N01 bedroom_night | Next stop | Catching it
How reliably can a yawn be passed on? Does a picture work, or a sound, or even a sentence in a book? And which part of a yawning face does the job? Provine asked these questions with videotapes and printed pages, and some of his answers are likely to be happening to you while you listen.
