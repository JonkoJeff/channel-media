The man was Charles Darwin. In 1872, thirteen years after his famous book on evolution, he published another one, called The Expression of the Emotions in Man and Animals. It's a gentler book in many ways, full of descriptions of dogs and cats and monkeys and babies, and of the faces people make when they're happy, frightened, puzzled or sad.

@quote N09 cabin_fireplace | Blushing is the most peculiar and the most human of all expressions. | Charles Darwin, 1872
And near the end of that book, there's a whole chapter on blushing. It opens with a sentence that has been quoted ever since. Blushing, Darwin wrote, is the most peculiar and the most human of all expressions. He'd looked at a great many animals by then, and he couldn't find one that blushed the way we do.

@char A10 living_room_day | Why the most human? | It needs a self
Why would blushing be so human? Darwin's idea was that a blush needs a particular kind of mind. You have to be able to think about yourself as other people see you. A monkey can be frightened or angry, and its face can show it. But to blush, Darwin thought, you need to picture your own face in someone else's eyes, and to care about what they're thinking.

@quote N01 attic_rain | Self-attention directed to personal appearance, in relation to the opinion of others. | Darwin's cause of the blush, 1872
He put it in a phrase that sounds old-fashioned but is surprisingly precise. The original cause of blushing, he wrote, was self-attention directed to personal appearance, in relation to the opinion of others. In other words, it's not the mistake that makes you blush. It's the moment you become aware of being seen.

@char N20 cafe_sunset | Back to Elena | Nothing wrong, but seen
That fits Elena rather well. Nothing went wrong in her solo. But for eight bars, every eye in that hall was on her, and only her. She stepped forward out of the safe row of voices and became, for a little while, the only thing anyone was looking at. Darwin would have said that was quite enough.

@char A13 rooftop_dusk | Attention to a body part | Darwin's bold guess
Darwin went further, and this part hasn't held up as well. He suggested that when you pay close attention to a part of your body, more blood flows to it. So if you're thinking hard about your face, and how it looks to other people, the blood would rush there. It was an ingenious guess, but most researchers today don't think attention alone works that way.

@char N10 bedroom_night | Of no use at all? | Darwin was doubtful
There's another thing Darwin believed that later scientists have argued with. He couldn't see what a blush was for. He described it as happening without being the least service to either of them, meaning neither the person blushing nor the person watching. To him it was a side effect, a by-product of the mind turning its attention inward. We'll see later whether that view has survived.

Darwin worked the way he often did, by writing letters. He sent questions to missionaries, doctors and travellers in many countries, asking them what they'd seen. Did the people they lived among blush? Where on the body? At what kinds of moments? The answers came back slowly, by ship, over months and years.

@char A01 street_day | A question by post | Asked around the world
One of the questions he sent out, in 1867, was beautifully careful. He asked whether shame excites a blush, when the colour of the skin allows it to be visible. He already suspected that people of every background blush, but that it's simply easier to see on some skin than on others. That idea turns out to be important, and we'll come back to it.

@quote N07 living_room_day | Man is the only animal that blushes. Or needs to. | Mark Twain, Following the Equator, 1897
A generation later, the writer Mark Twain put the same idea in a sharper way. In a book of travel writing from 1897, he included one of his little sayings. Man is the only animal that blushes, he wrote. Or needs to. It's a joke, of course, but there's a serious thought inside it, that blushing has something to do with our being moral, social creatures.

@char N29 cabin_fireplace | Is it really only us? | For a long time, yes
Is it really true that only humans blush? For most of the time since Darwin, that's what nearly everyone believed. Many animals change colour, of course, and many creatures signal with their skin and feathers. But the social blush, the one switched on by being looked at, seemed to be ours alone. Later tonight, we'll meet some parrots who may have something to say about that.

@char A02 autumn_overlook | The first careful study | Not the last word
Darwin's chapter was the first careful attempt to study the blush, and it set the questions that people are still asking. What exactly happens in the skin? Who blushes, and when? Does it mean the same thing everywhere? And what, if anything, is it for? For about a century after him, surprisingly little was done to answer those questions with measurements.
