So if letting it out doesn't cool the fire, and holding on to it keeps it warm, what does help? This is the part of tonight's story that I find most hopeful. Because the research here isn't about becoming a person who never feels angry. It's about small, quiet moves that give the feeling a little room to change.

@char A13 living_room_day | A word for it | Simple, but not silly
The first one sounds almost too simple. It's putting the feeling into words. Not shouting it, not acting it out, just naming it. I'm angry. I'm frustrated. I feel hurt. It can seem a little silly, like something you'd say to a small child. But there's a surprising piece of brain research behind it.

@study A10 cabin_fireplace | Matthew Lieberman and colleagues | University of California, Los Angeles | published in 2007
In 2007, a team led by Matthew Lieberman at the University of California, Los Angeles, published a study in the journal Psychological Science. Thirty people lay in a brain scanner and looked at photographs of faces showing strong emotions. Sometimes they simply looked. Sometimes they chose a word to describe what the face was feeling, a word like angry, or scared.

@stat A12 cafe_sunset | Lieberman and colleagues, 2007 | 30 | people in a brain scanner, naming the feelings on faces
When people chose the word angry for an angry face, something interesting happened. The response in the amygdala, that small, alert region deep in the brain, became weaker. And at the same time, an area towards the front of the brain, on the right side, called the right ventrolateral prefrontal cortex, became more active. The researchers called this affect labeling.

@char A03 living_room_day | Naming it | Turning the volume down
In plain words, putting a feeling into words seemed to turn its volume down a little. Not off. Just down. It doesn't solve the problem, and it doesn't mean the feeling was wrong. It simply gives the thinking part of you a moment to join the conversation. And that small moment can change what happens next.

@char A19 cabin_fireplace | A step back | Seeing it from outside
The second idea is about where you stand when you look back at a moment that made you angry. Most of us replay it from the inside. We're right there again, hearing the words, feeling the heat. But there's another way to look. You can step back and watch it from a little distance, as if you were someone else in the room.

@char A05 autumn_overlook | Inside or outside | Two ways to remember
Think of the last time someone annoyed you. Perhaps a driver cut in front of you, or a colleague spoke over you in a meeting. When you remember it, are you back behind your own eyes, hearing every word again? Or can you see yourself from across the room, a small figure in a bigger scene? Most people, it turns out, do the first without even noticing.

@study A13 rooftop_dusk | Mischkowski, Kross and Bushman | Ohio State University and the University of Michigan | published in 2012
In 2012, three researchers, Dominik Mischkowski, Ethan Kross and Brad Bushman, published two studies in the Journal of Experimental Social Psychology. In the first, ninety-four students were provoked in an annoying way. Then some of them replayed the scene from the inside, some from a distance, like a fly on the wall, and some got no instructions at all.

@bars A12 cafe_sunset | Mischkowski, Kross and Bushman, 2012 | students | Study one:94 ;; Study two:95
The students who stepped back, like a fly on the wall, reported less anger and had fewer aggressive thoughts than the other two groups. In the second study, with ninety-five students, the ones who took that distant view also gave their provoker shorter, softer blasts of noise. A small shift in perspective, and the heat came down.

@char A10 living_room_day | The oldest advice | Still standing
The third idea is the oldest of all. We met it a moment ago. Nearly two thousand years ago, the Roman thinker Seneca wrote a long essay called On Anger. And in it, there's a line that is still quoted today, in a common translation.

@quote A18 cabin_fireplace | The greatest remedy for anger is delay. | Seneca, On Anger
The greatest remedy for anger is delay. Not denial. Not pretending. Just delay. A little time between the feeling and the action. Long enough for the body to settle, for the first story to soften, for another reading of the moment to appear. Seneca didn't have a brain scanner. But he had noticed something real.

@char A12 autumn_overlook | Not never angry | Angry well
And there's a fourth idea, older still, that ties all of this together. It's not that anger is bad and should be switched off. Remember, anger is the feeling that pushes us towards something that matters. The question is what we do with that push.

@quote A15 cafe_sunset | Anyone can become angry, that is easy. But to be angry with the right person, to the right degree, at the right time... that is not easy. | Aristotle, a famous paraphrase
In a famous paraphrase of Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics, anyone can become angry, that is easy. But to be angry with the right person, to the right degree, at the right time, for the right purpose and in the right way, that is not easy. Anger, on this view, isn't the enemy. It's a skill we're all still learning.

@list A13 living_room_day | What helps | Name the feeling ;; Step back and watch ;; Give it a little time ;; Then choose what to say
So here they are, side by side. Name the feeling. Step back and watch the moment, like a fly on the wall. Give it a little time. And then, once the heat has come down, choose what you actually want to say. None of these are dramatic. That's rather the point.

So Sam deletes the sharp reply. And he writes a new one. Not a fake cheerful one, and not a grovelling one. Just an honest one. You're right, I forgot, and I'm sorry. I was annoyed when I read your message, but I think I was mostly hungry and tired. Can I call you tomorrow? Then he presses send.

That's what tonight has been about, in the end. Anger isn't a mistake, and it isn't a monster. It's information.
