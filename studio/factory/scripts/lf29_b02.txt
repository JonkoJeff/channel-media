@char A20 living_room_day | A child at play | Thinking out loud
Picture a small child on the living room floor, building a tower of blocks. If you listen, you'll hear a running commentary. Now the red one. No, not there. Careful, careful. Oh no. There we go. Nobody asked for it, and the child isn't really talking to anyone. The words simply pour out while the hands get on with the job.

@char A02 cafe_sunset | Familiar sound | Every parent knows it
If you have ever spent time with three and four year olds, you'll know this sound well. They narrate their drawings, scold their teddy bears, and talk themselves through putting on their shoes. For a long time, people saw this as a cute phase, or perhaps a sign that small children simply hadn't learned to keep their thoughts to themselves yet.

@chapter A05 autumn_overlook | Two great psychologists
In the early twentieth century, two of the most important thinkers in child psychology looked at this same chatter, and came to very different conclusions about what it meant.

@study A13 cabin_fireplace | Jean Piaget | Switzerland, 1923 | The Language and Thought of the Child: young children often speak without really addressing anyone
The first was the Swiss psychologist Jean Piaget. In his book The Language and Thought of the Child, first published in 1923, he described how much of a young child's talk isn't really aimed at a listener. He called it egocentric speech. In his view, it was a sign that the child couldn't yet take another person's point of view. As children grew and became more social, he thought, this kind of talk would simply fade away, like baby teeth.

@char A18 attic_rain | A different idea | Not fading but moving
The second thinker was the Russian psychologist Lev Vygotsky. He watched the same children, heard the same chatter, and came to a very different idea. He didn't think the talk faded away. He thought it went somewhere. He thought it moved inside.

@study A03 living_room_day | Lev Vygotsky | Moscow, 1934 | Thought and Language: talk aimed at the self slowly goes underground and becomes inner speech
Vygotsky's book, usually known in English as Thought and Language, was published in 1934, the year he died, at only thirty seven. In it, he argued that children first learn language as a way of talking with other people, with parents who say, careful, or, try the blue one. Then children begin to say those same things to themselves, out loud. And over the years, this self-talk becomes quieter and quieter, until it disappears from the outside and lives on as inner speech.

@photo lf29_b02_01.jpg | A tower of blocks
So in Vygotsky's picture, the little voice saying, careful, careful, on the living room floor is not a mistake to grow out of. It's the beginning of something. It's a child learning to guide themselves with words, the same way an adult once guided them. The voice of the helper becomes the voice of the self.

@chapter A12 cabin_fireplace | Private speech
Researchers later gave this out-loud self-talk a gentler name. Instead of egocentric speech, they called it private speech, speech that is spoken aloud but meant for the speaker alone.

@list A15 cafe_sunset | What private speech does | Guides the hands ;; Plans the next step ;; Calms frustration ;; Praises a success
If you listen closely, private speech has jobs. It guides the hands, as in, this piece goes here. It plans, as in, first the blue, then the red. It soothes, as in, it's okay, try again. And it celebrates, as in, I did it. Those are the same jobs our silent inner voice does for us as adults.

@char A10 living_room_day | Tested, not just observed | Researchers checked
These ideas didn't stay as theories. In the decades that followed, researchers watched children while they worked on puzzles and tasks, and recorded what they said to themselves. One well known study, by Lawrence Kohlberg and his colleagues, was published in the journal Child Development in 1968. It looked at how private speech changes with age, and its findings fitted Vygotsky's picture better than Piaget's.

@list A13 autumn_overlook | What the studies tended to find | Private speech rises when tasks get hard ;; It becomes quieter and shorter with age ;; It turns into whispers and mutters ;; Then it seems to move inside
Across many studies, a pattern kept showing up. Children talk to themselves more when a task is hard enough to be a challenge, but not so hard that they give up. As they grow older, their private speech gets quieter. Full sentences turn into fragments, fragments turn into whispers and lip movements, and then, for most children, the speech seems to vanish from the outside altogether.

@char A19 cabin_fireplace | Not gone | Just quieter
But vanishing from the outside is not the same as vanishing. This is the heart of Vygotsky's idea. The voice that guided the child on the living room floor doesn't disappear. It becomes the voice in your head that says, right, where did I put my keys, or, don't forget the milk.

@chapter A16 rooftop_dusk | Laura Berk's playrooms
One of the researchers who did most to test these ideas in real classrooms was the American psychologist Laura Berk, who studied private speech in children for many years.

@char A14 street_day | Watching children work | Talk and attention
Berk and others watched children in ordinary settings, working on maths, building things, and doing school tasks. Their work suggested that private speech is not a sign of a child who is struggling or being silly. Often, it's the opposite. Children who used task-relevant private speech tended to be more focused on what they were doing, especially on challenging tasks.

@list A12 living_room_day | A lesson for adults too | Don't hush the helper ;; Self-talk is part of learning ;; It peaks when things get hard
There's a gentle lesson here, for parents and teachers and for all of us. Telling a child to stop talking to themselves may take away a tool they are using to think. And the same may be true for us. When a task gets hard, the urge to talk it through is not a sign of weakness. It may be your mind reaching for one of its oldest tools.

@chapter A03 cafe_sunset | How inner speech changes shape
Vygotsky noticed something else, too. When speech moves inside, it doesn't stay the same. It changes shape.

@char A18 attic_rain | Shorter and denser | A private shorthand
Out loud, we speak in full sentences, because the listener needs them. But when the only listener is you, there's no need to explain. You already know what you mean. So, Vygotsky argued, inner speech becomes abbreviated. It drops words, keeps the key ones, and packs a lot of meaning into very little. A single word, like, keys, can stand for a whole thought: I need to find my keys before I leave, or I'll be late.

@list A10 cabin_fireplace | Two kinds of inner speech | Expanded: full sentences, like real talk ;; Condensed: fragments and single words
Later researchers, such as the British psychologist Charles Fernyhough, picked up this idea and described inner speech as moving between two forms. Sometimes it is expanded, full sentences that sound almost like real talk, especially when we're stressed or rehearsing a conversation. And sometimes it is condensed, quick fragments and single words that rush past almost faster than we can notice.

@char A13 living_room_day | You know both | Notice the difference
You'll probably recognise both. When you rehearse what you're going to say to your boss, the inner voice speaks in proper sentences, with pauses and emphasis. When you're cooking and thinking, salt, timer, oven, it's more like a string of labels. Both are your inner voice. One is the formal version, and the other is the shorthand you use with yourself.

@chapter A05 autumn_overlook | A dialogue in one head
There's one last part of Vygotsky's idea that is worth holding on to tonight, because it explains something odd about the inner voice.

@char A02 cafe_sunset | Conversation inside | Two sides of one talk
If inner speech grows out of conversations with other people, then perhaps it keeps some of the shape of a conversation. That would explain why we so often argue with ourselves, ask ourselves questions and then answer them, or hear a thought in the voice of someone we know. Fernyhough has argued that a lot of inner speech is dialogic, a back and forth, rather than a simple monologue.

@char A19 bedroom_night | Back to the question | Who's listening?
And that brings us back to the riddle we started with. If you are talking, who is listening? Vygotsky's answer would be, you are both. The part of you that speaks learned to speak from others, and the part of you that listens learned to listen from them too. The inner voice is a conversation that moved in from the outside world and made itself at home.

@char A10 cabin_fireplace | Next | Catching the voice
So far, though, we've only looked at children, whose speech we can hear. The harder question is what the inner voice is like in adults, where it's completely silent. How would you even measure it? One psychologist came up with a beautifully simple answer: a beeper.
