@chapter A12 living_room_day | Extraordinary donors
There is a kind of generosity so unusual that scientists have only recently begun to study it. In several countries, a small number of people have given a kidney to a stranger, someone they have never met and may never meet. It is a major operation, with real risks, and no reward. What kind of person does this?

@char A04 attic_rain | The question | Is something different about them?
Abigail Marsh, a psychologist at Georgetown University, wanted to find out whether such donors differ in some measurable way from other people. She was interested in the emotional systems of the brain that respond to other people's distress, and in whether these might work differently in people who are unusually generous.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Marsh, Stoycos, Brethel-Haurwitz, Robinson, VanMeter & Cardinale | Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 2014 | Neural and cognitive characteristics of extraordinary altruists
Marsh's team compared nineteen people who had donated a kidney to a stranger with a group of twenty people who had not. They used brain scans to look at a region called the amygdala, which is involved in responding to emotional signals, while participants viewed faces showing different emotions. The donors' right amygdala was larger, on average, and responded more strongly to faces showing fear.

@stat A14 conspiracy_board | People in the kidney donor study | 19 donors, 20 comparison | brain scans while viewing emotional faces
It's worth being precise about what this does and doesn't show. The groups were small. The finding is a correlation, not proof that the difference caused the generosity. And the amygdala is involved in many things. But the results are interesting, because they point in a direction opposite to a popular idea.

@char A12 living_room_day | The surprise | Sensitive, not fearless
You might expect heroic helpers to be especially fearless. This study suggests something different: that people who are unusually generous may be especially tuned in to other people's distress, and especially responsive to signs of fear in others. Their kindness may begin with noticing.

@chapter A13 rooftop_dusk | A mirror image
Marsh has also studied the opposite end of the spectrum: people who score very high on psychopathic traits, a set of characteristics that include a lack of concern for others. In those studies, the pattern tends to run the other way.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Marsh, Finger, Fowler, Adalio, Jurkowitz, Schechter, Pine, Decety, Blair | Journal of Child Psychology and Psychiatry, 2013 | Empathic responsiveness in amygdala and anterior cingulate cortex in youths with psychopathic traits
In a study of adolescents with psychopathic traits, researchers found reduced amygdala responses to fearful faces compared with other young people. The study fits the idea of a spectrum, in which responsiveness to others' distress varies from person to person, with extraordinary altruists at one end and people with callous traits at the other.

@list A03 living_room_day | A spectrum of responsiveness | Some people respond very strongly to distress in others ;; Most people fall somewhere in the middle ;; Some respond weakly ;; The differences seem to be partly biological and partly shaped by experience
It's important not to read too much into this. The idea of a spectrum is a description, and not a destiny. These are patterns in groups of people, and they say little about any one individual. And nobody's responsiveness is fixed for life.

@chapter A06 cafe_sunset | Following up
In 2018, Marsh's group published another study of extraordinary altruists, this time using a different method.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Brethel-Haurwitz, Cardinale, Vekaria, Robertson, Walitt, VanMeter & Marsh | Psychological Science, 2018 | Extraordinary altruists exhibit enhanced self-other overlap in neural responses to distress
The researchers scanned the brains of kidney donors and others while they experienced a mild threat themselves, and while they watched a stranger experience the same threat. In the donors, patterns of neural response to their own and to the stranger's distress were more alike than in the comparison group. The finding was consistent with the idea that for them, another person's pain registers more like their own.

@char A01 cafe_sunset | An image to hold | Self and other, closer together
That's a lovely way to put it. For some people, there may be less distance between my pain and yours. The researchers were careful to describe this as a hypothesis supported by their data, and not as a final explanation. But it fits the accounts that donors give, many of whom say they simply could not imagine not helping.

@list A12 living_room_day | What donors often say | I had two kidneys and someone had none ;; It did not feel like a big decision ;; I would want someone to do it for me ;; I just couldn't say no
You'll notice echoes of the rescuers: the sense that the decision was obvious and not heroic. Whatever its origins, extraordinary kindness seems to feel, from the inside, ordinary.

@chapter A19 living_room_day | Can empathy be trained?
If some people are more responsive to others' distress, can responsiveness be grown? The evidence is encouraging, if preliminary. Studies of practices like compassion training suggest that people can learn to feel and act on concern more readily.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Weng, Fox, Shackman, Stodola, Caldwell, Olson, Rogers & Davidson | Psychological Science, 2013 | Compassion training alters altruism and neural responses to suffering
Helen Weng and her colleagues taught one group of adults two weeks of compassion meditation and another group a cognitive reappraisal technique. Afterwards, participants played a game in which they could redistribute some of their own game earnings to a victim of unfairness. Those who had trained in compassion redistributed more, and showed changes in brain activity linked to emotion regulation.

@char A12 living_room_day | The caveat | Small trials, early days
This was a small study with short training, and the tasks were laboratory games. Researchers are still working out how large and lasting the effects are. But the broad point is hopeful: the capacity to care looks like a skill that can be practised, as well as a trait that people have.

@list A03 living_room_day | Words to keep from this chapter | Extraordinary altruist ;; Amygdala ;; Self-other overlap ;; Compassion training
Four terms from this chapter. An extraordinary altruist is someone who gives at great personal cost to a stranger. The amygdala is a brain region involved in emotional signals. Self-other overlap is the closeness between how we respond to our own pain and another's. And compassion training is practice at generating caring feelings.

@list A12 living_room_day | A short compassion practice | Sit comfortably and breathe slowly ;; Picture someone you love and wish them wellbeing ;; Extend the same wish to someone neutral ;; Then to someone difficult ;; Finish by wishing it for yourself
Here is a short practice, modelled loosely on the compassion exercises used in research. Sit comfortably and breathe slowly. Picture someone you love and silently wish them wellbeing. Then extend the same wish to someone neutral, like a person you pass on the street. Then to someone difficult. Finish by wishing it for yourself. Picture this scene: a man waiting at a railway platform practises it for two minutes while the train is late. The person who normally irritates him, the man who talks loudly into his phone, becomes just another tired person. The train still comes late, but the wait is lighter. It isn't a cure for anything. It is a small, practised kindness.

@list A12 living_room_day | Pause and reflect | When do I feel other people's distress most? ;; What do I do with that feeling? ;; Who do I wish well in a day? ;; Who is hardest to include?
Take a pause to notice how you respond to other people's distress. Some of us feel it strongly and instantly, with a clench in the chest. Others take a moment, and feel concern only when something is pointed out. Neither is better. The research on extraordinary donors suggests that a strong response to distress can be a root of generosity, but the studies on empathy and compassion add that what you do with that feeling matters. Do you turn toward the person, or away? Practice can change that. Think about whom you wish well in a day. Family, friends, perhaps a colleague. Who is hardest to include in that wish? The practice of compassion training is simply to extend the circle a little, with patience. It doesn't mean pretending to like everyone. It means wishing that they are well, which turns out to be quite different.

@photo lf27_b10_01.jpg | A corridor at dawn
A quiet hospital corridor at dawn. Somewhere along a corridor like this, a stranger gives a kidney to another stranger, and the two may never learn each other's names. The scene is rare, but it stands for something common: the capacity of one person to carry another's need. The kidney donors remind us that generosity can be deep and quiet. They also remind us that responsiveness to others varies between people, and that caring is something that can grow with practice, as the compassion studies suggest.

@char A15 cabin_fireplace | A gentle thought | You may be more tuned in than you think
If you often feel other people's moods, and sometimes find it tiring, that sensitivity is also a gift. The research suggests that responsiveness to distress is one of the roots of kindness. The skill is learning to care without being overwhelmed, which is the subject of our last chapters.

@char A08 cabin_fireplace | Looking back | What the donors taught us
So what did we learn? That kidney donors to strangers showed larger and more responsive amygdalae in one study. That a later study found closer overlap between self and other distress. That the pattern runs the other way in people with psychopathic traits. And that compassion training may help people to care more readily.
