@chapter A10 cafe_sunset | How long does it take?
It is a few weeks later, and Ines has started to wonder about something. She has been walking most mornings now. Some days it feels like a pleasure, and some days it still feels like a small negotiation. And so she asks the question we all ask sooner or later. How long does this take? When will it simply stop being a decision, and start being a thing she does, like brushing her teeth?

@char A13 attic_rain | A calendar on the fridge | Counting the mornings
She has even started marking the mornings on a little calendar by the fridge, with a pencil. Each walk gets a small tick. It's a sweet thing to do, and it comes from a good place, a wish to be fair to herself and to see how far she has come. She wants to know where she stands. Somewhere in the back of her mind, she has heard that there is a magic number of days, and that after it, everything gets easy. She just doesn't remember what the number is.

@char A03 living_room_day | The number everyone knows | Twenty-one days
Perhaps you have heard the same thing. A popular idea says it takes twenty-one days to form a habit. It's neat, and it's easy to remember, and it has been repeated in a great many places. But it's a popular idea, not a finding from a careful study. So let's look at what researchers found when they actually followed people while they tried to build something new, day after day, in real life.

@chapter A18 rooftop_dusk | Following real people
The study we'll look at was carried out by Lally and colleagues, and it's one of the best known in this area. What makes it special is its ordinary setting. There was no laboratory, and nobody was told exactly what to do. Instead, people chose a small new behavior for themselves, and then simply got on with their lives, while the researchers followed along for twelve weeks.

@study A12 street_day | Lally, van Jaarsveld, Potts and Wardle | European Journal of Social Psychology, published 2010 | How long until a new behavior feels automatic?
The volunteers were university students. Each one picked something they did not already do, and linked it to a cue that came up once a day. They would do it every day, in response to that cue, and keep track of how it went. And at regular points they rated how automatic it felt, using a questionnaire about habit, adapted for this study by the researchers.

@list A19 cafe_sunset | What people chose | A piece of fruit with lunch ;; A bottle of water with lunch ;; A short run before dinner ;; Each one tied to a daily cue
To make it concrete, here are the sort of things people chose. Eating a piece of fruit with lunch. Drinking a bottle of water with lunch. Running for fifteen minutes before dinner. Each behavior had a daily cue attached to it, lunch or dinner, something that came around by itself. Notice how small and humble these are. These weren't grand life changes. They were the kind of thing a person might quietly try over a few weeks, on an ordinary Tuesday, with no fanfare.

@stat A05 cafe_sunset | Volunteers in the study | 96 | people who chose a new daily behavior
Ninety-six people began the study. Not everyone ended up with data that could be used. For some, the numbers simply didn't fit a clear curve. But among those who did settle into the behavior, a clear picture appeared. And it's a picture that tells us something rather reassuring about how a habit tends to grow, and about how patient we can afford to be with ourselves while it does.

@curve A18 living_room_day | How automatic it feels, as a sketch | days of practice | how automatic it feels | 0.9 | Fast at first, then it levels off
Here is the shape of it, drawn as a simple sketch. The authors noted that early repetitions bring larger gains in automaticity than later ones. In other words, the first few weeks seem to do a lot of the work. Then the curve flattens, and each extra day adds a little less. Eventually it reaches a plateau, a point where the behavior simply stops becoming more automatic. That's the moment we might call a habit.

@stat A20 cafe_sunset | The middle of the group | 66 | median days to reach that plateau
And how long did that take? For the people whose data fitted the curve, the median was sixty-six days. But the range was enormous. For one person it was eighteen days, and for another, two hundred and fifty-four. So the honest answer to Ines' question is that it depends. It depends on the person, on the behavior, and on the weeks they happen to be having. There's no single magic number.

@bars A19 cabin_fireplace | Days to reach the plateau, by behavior | days | Drinking:59 ;; Eating:65 ;; Exercise:91
Now here is something fresh. The researchers also looked at which kind of behavior people had picked. For those who chose drinking, the median was fifty-nine days. For eating, sixty-five. And for exercise, ninety-one. Exercise took about one and a half times longer than the others, which fits the idea that a more complex behavior takes more time to become automatic. A walk before work asks a little more of us than a glass of water.

@char A10 bedroom_night | A careful note | Small groups, soft edges
A careful note, though. Those groups were small, and the differences between them were not statistically significant. So it would be too much to say the study proved anything. The authors treat it as a suggestion. But it matches what most of us feel, that a morning walk takes more getting used to than a glass of water.

@char A03 rooftop_dusk | Not everyone got there | About half
There was another honest detail in the paper. Out of the eighty-two people with enough data, only thirty-nine showed a clear habit curve. The authors say that about half did not do the behavior consistently enough to achieve habit status. People who did it more consistently fitted the curve better, which is a nice thing to know. So it isn't only about time passing. It is about repeating, in the same setting, again and again.

@char A10 cabin_fireplace | Half a decision | The walk begins by itself
Something else is changing in Ines' mornings, and she only half notices it. In the first weeks, the walk began with a long little talk inside her head. Should I, shouldn't I, how cold is it. Now the talk is shorter. Some mornings her shoes are on before the talk has even started. That, quietly, is what automatic feels like from the inside. It isn't a feeling of force. It's more a feeling of not having to ask.

@list A12 cabin_fireplace | What the study suggests | Early days count the most ;; The timeline varies widely ;; Simple may be quicker ;; Repeating is what matters
Let's gather it up in a gentle way. The early days seem to count for the most, so it's worth putting a little care into the beginning. The timeline varies widely, so comparing ourselves with a number is not very useful. Simpler behaviors may take a little less time. And what seems to matter most is repeating in the same situation. None of this asks for perfection. It only asks for a return.

@char A15 street_day | Ines at the window | A slower kind of patience
Ines looks at her calendar again, and this time she isn't discouraged. If a median of sixty-six days is what it takes for some people, and a person can need much more or much less, then she has no reason to feel behind. She isn't racing anyone. She's only a person who started on a particular Tuesday, with her own mornings and her own pace. That thought makes the whole thing feel lighter, and surprisingly spacious.

@chapter A15 rooftop_dusk | A missed morning
One Thursday, Ines wakes to a heavy downpour, and decides, quite sensibly, to stay in for the morning. She makes a cup of tea, and she doesn't walk. For a moment, she feels the old sinking feeling, the one that says the whole thing is ruined. But then she remembers the calendar by the fridge, and the long run of ticks. She smiles and leaves a gap. Then she makes another cup of tea.

@char A13 rooftop_dusk | What the paper said | One day is only one day
And the research is on her side. In the study, the volunteers missed a day now and then, as people do. After a missed opportunity, automaticity dipped a little. But the authors found that the missed day did not seem to knock the habit off course. The next day, people simply carried on, and the curve carried on with them. A single gap in a long pattern is only a small gap, and a pattern can hold a great many of those without coming apart.

@quote A19 attic_rain | Missing one opportunity did not materially affect the habit formation process. | Lally and colleagues, 2010
That is how the researchers put it in their own words, and it is one of the kindest sentences in the whole literature. It's a good sentence to keep somewhere warm, perhaps on the fridge, right beside the calendar. It doesn't say that missing days never matters. A long stretch away from the cue is a different story. But one rainy Thursday, or one tired evening, or one lovely lazy morning, isn't a disaster. It's just a day, and the pattern is much sturdier than it feels.

@char A19 cafe_sunset | Tomorrow, the shoes | Quietly carrying on
On Friday morning, the rain has softened to a mist. Ines puts on her shoes by the door, and steps out onto the damp pavement, and the walk feels easy, almost ordinary. She stops counting the days. And as she walks, she starts to wonder whether there might be a way to help a new habit along more deliberately. Not by force, but by design. And that is a very good question to rest on, so she lets it float quietly, the way a leaf floats on slow water, and doesn't try to answer it tonight.
