@char A19 cafe_sunset | Loose attention | The door swings open
Remember what Berntsen's diaries showed. Memories from your own life tend to pop up when attention is loose and spread out, rather than focused on a task. In her later writing, including a review published in 2010 in Current Directions in Psychological Science, she called them the unbidden past. And there are few moments in a day when attention is looser than when you're lying in bed, waiting for sleep.

@quote A03 rooftop_dusk | The unbidden past | Dorthe Berntsen, 2010
The unbidden past. It's a lovely phrase for what Ivy is experiencing. Memories that weren't asked for, rising up because the mind finally has room for them. In daytime they're often crowded out by what's in front of us. At night, there's nothing in front of us except the ceiling. So the unbidden past has the whole stage to itself.

@chapter A18 living_room_day | The brain at rest
But there's more to it than an empty stage. When neuroscientists started looking at the brain at rest, they found something surprising.

@study A10 cafe_sunset | Marcus Raichle and colleagues | Washington University, St. Louis | Found brain regions that become more active at rest
For a long time, people assumed the brain mostly switched down when it wasn't doing a task. Then, in 2001, Marcus Raichle and his colleagues published a paper in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences. It described a set of brain regions that were reliably more active when people were resting quietly than when they were concentrating on a task. They called it a default mode of brain function.

@char A19 living_room_day | Never really off | A busy rest
It was a quiet surprise for the field. The brain, it turned out, is never really off. Even lying still with your eyes closed, it keeps using a large share of the body's energy, and much of that activity has nothing to do with the outside world. Rest isn't an empty space in the brain's day. It's a different kind of work.

@char A12 living_room_day | The default mode | The brain's own channel
This network of regions became known as the default mode network. It's active when we're not focused on the outside world. And over the following years, researchers linked it with exactly the kind of things Ivy is doing now. Remembering the past. Imagining the future. Thinking about ourselves, and about other people, and what they might think of us.

@list A03 attic_rain | What the resting brain tends to do | Remember the past ;; Imagine the future ;; Think about ourselves ;; Think about other people
Here's what the resting brain tends to drift toward, according to that research. It remembers the past. It imagines the future. It thinks about ourselves. And it thinks about other people, and how we fit in with them. Now look at Ivy's lecture hall memory. It's the past, it's about herself, and it's about how other people saw her. It's almost exactly what the resting brain is built to bring up.

@char A15 cabin_fireplace | Not a malfunction | The brain doing its job
That's quite comforting, in its way. When Ivy's mind brings up the wave in the lecture hall, it isn't malfunctioning. It's doing what it does whenever we stop asking it for something. It's sorting through our life, our place among people, our story so far. It's just picked a page she'd rather skip.

@quote A19 rooftop_dusk | The resting brain is still at work, sorting through our story. | A way to see it
The resting brain is still at work, sorting through our story. Most of us only notice it when the sorting lands on something uncomfortable. On a peaceful evening, the same network might bring up a childhood beach, or a plan for the weekend, and we'd hardly notice it at all, except as a pleasant drifting feeling just before sleep.

@char A13 bedroom_night | Drifting toward sleep | Thoughts loosen
And as we drift toward sleep, those wandering thoughts often loosen even further. They become less like sentences and more like pictures. A street, a face, a half-heard voice. Many people notice that just before they fall asleep, their thoughts turn strange and dreamlike. That's often a good sign. It means the mind is letting go of steering.

@chapter A13 bedroom_night | Why the social pages come up
There's one more reason the awkward memories in particular seem to come out at night.

@char A10 attic_rain | Other people, on our minds | Even when they're not here
A great deal of our resting thought is about other people. What they said, what they meant, what they might think of us. We're a deeply social species, and the brain seems to spend its spare time going over our relationships, the way someone might go over a map of a town they live in. Embarrassing moments are, almost by definition, social moments. So they're right at the top of the pile.

@char A12 cafe_sunset | A social map | Checking the roads
Think of it as a social map. During the day we travel the roads, talking, working, joking. At night the mind looks over the map. Which roads are smooth? Which ones had a bump? A slip with a waiter is a small bump. A wave meant for someone else is another. The mind marks them, gently but insistently, as if to say, remember this road.

@char A03 attic_rain | Bumps get marked | Smooth roads don't
And notice which roads get marked. The thousands of smooth conversations Ivy had this year, the polite hellos and easy chats, aren't on tonight's list. They went fine, so there's nothing to check. Only the bumps get circled. That makes the map look much rougher than the roads really are. A night spent looking at bumps can make a perfectly good year feel full of potholes.

@quote A18 living_room_day | The mind looks over the social map while we rest. | A picture to keep
The mind looks over the social map while we rest. It's not trying to make us feel small. It's checking on the roads that matter to it, and the roads that matter most to a social creature are the ones that lead to other people. Later tonight we'll see why that checking can get so loud, and why it's usually louder than it needs to be.
