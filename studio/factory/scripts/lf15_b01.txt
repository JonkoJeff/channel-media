Imagine a summer camp in the hills of Oklahoma. There are cabins, a creek, a swimming hole and a ball field. Twenty two boys arrive, ready for three weeks of hiking and games. What none of them knows is that almost every adult around them is quietly taking notes.

@list A13 living_room_day | Tonight, we'll explore | How strangers became a team ;; How two teams became rivals ;; What finally brought them together ;; The camp that was never in the textbooks
Tonight, we'll open one case file, step by step. We'll see how a group of strangers turned into a team, how two teams turned into rivals, and what finally brought them back together. And near the end, we'll look at a different camp, one year earlier, that almost nobody heard about for decades. Keep that one in the back of your mind.

@photo lf15_b01_01.jpg | Robbers Cave State Park, summer 1954
The place was Robbers Cave State Park, in the southeast of Oklahoma. The name comes from old stories that outlaws such as Belle Starr and Jesse James once used the area as a hideout. In the summer of 1954, it became the setting for one of the best known field experiments in social psychology.

@study A10 bedroom_night | Muzafer Sherif and colleagues | Robbers Cave State Park, Oklahoma | Intergroup conflict and cooperation, summer 1954
The study was led by the social psychologist Muzafer Sherif, working with Carolyn Wood Sherif and a team of researchers. The full account was published as a book in 1961, called Intergroup Conflict and Cooperation: The Robbers Cave Experiment. The question behind it sounds simple. Where does hostility between groups really come from?

@char A19 bedroom_night | The big question | Nature, or situation?
One common answer is that people are simply like that. We sort the world into us and them, and we can't help it. Sherif had a different idea. He suspected that ordinary, well adjusted people would turn against another group when the two groups wanted something only one of them could have.

@quote A03 living_room_day | Conflict comes about between groups of people, not because it's part of our human nature. | Gina Perry, describing Sherif's view
The writer Gina Perry, who later spent years digging into this study, put Sherif's idea like this. Conflict comes about between groups of people, not because it's part of our human nature. If that were true, then hostility could be created on purpose. And if it could be created, maybe it could also be undone.

@char A19 bedroom_night | Why a field experiment? | Real life, with a plan
Most psychology experiments happen in a lab. Someone sits at a desk, reads a few instructions and answers questions for an hour. That is useful, but it is also a little unreal. Sherif wanted something closer to everyday life, where people eat, sleep, play and argue together for weeks. So he took the experiment outside, into the real world, and gave it a careful plan.

@list A10 bedroom_night | Lab versus field | A lab: short, controlled, a little unreal ;; A field study: long, messy, closer to life ;; Robbers Cave: a field study with a script
You can think of it as a trade off. A lab study is short and controlled, but it can feel a little unreal. A field study is longer and messier, but closer to life. Robbers Cave tried to have both: a real camp, with real days and nights, and a script running quietly underneath.

@chapter N14 conspiracy_board | Choosing the campers
So how do you test an idea like that? You can't wait for two real groups to start a real feud. Sherif chose to build the groups himself, from scratch, and to watch what happened from the inside.

@stat A18 bedroom_night | The campers | 22 | boys, aged 11 to 12, who had never met
The researchers selected twenty two boys, aged eleven to twelve. None of them knew each other before the camp. They were chosen to be as similar as possible, so that any conflict could not be blamed on old friendships, old grudges or big differences in background.

@list A10 bedroom_night | How similar they were | White and middle class ;; Protestant, from two-parent homes ;; Judged well adjusted ;; No history of behaviour problems
According to the study's description, all of the boys were white, middle class and Protestant, from two-parent homes. They were judged to be well adjusted, with no history of behaviour problems. That was the point. If these boys turned against each other, it would be hard to say they had been troublemakers all along.

@char N09 living_room_day | Two balanced groups | Matched on purpose
The boys were then split into two groups. The researchers tried to balance them, so that each side had a similar mix of physical, mental and social talents. Neither group would start with an obvious advantage. Remember that detail, because later the contests were not quite as fair as they looked.

@char A13 living_room_day | Not a normal camp | The staff had a second job
Here is the part that makes this a case file. The boys believed they were at an ordinary summer camp. The researchers posed as counsellors, and one even worked as a caretaker. Every conversation, every game and every argument could end up in someone's notes.

@list N20 bedroom_night | What the parents were told | A camp funded by Yale ;; Leadership ;; Democratic values
And the parents? According to reporting on Gina Perry's research, the parents were told that the camp was funded by Yale and that it aimed to promote leadership and democratic values. The boys themselves didn't learn that they had been part of an experiment until decades later, some of them only when Perry contacted them.

@char A19 bedroom_night | A different time | Rules were looser then
Today, a study like this would face strict ethics reviews, informed consent and careful checks on any harm to the children. In the 1950s, those rules were much looser. Keep that in mind as we go. We can be curious about what the study found and, at the same time, honest about how it was done.

@list N10 bedroom_night | The plan, in three stages | Become a group ;; Meet the rivals ;; Find a way back
Sherif's plan had three stages. First, each group would become a real team on its own. Then the two teams would meet, under conditions designed to create friction. And finally, the researchers would test what could bring them back together. Tonight, we'll walk through all three.

@chapter A10 bedroom_night | Stage one: becoming a group
The two groups arrived on successive days, by bus, and were kept apart. For about the first week, each group thought it had the camp to itself. They had no idea another group of boys was living just a short walk away.

@photo lf15_b01_02.jpg | Hikes, swimming and shared work
So each group did what any group of new campers might do. They hiked, they swam, they cooked, and they worked together on small jobs around the camp. Slowly, the strangers began to rely on one another. Leaders emerged, and so did quieter followers.

@char A03 living_room_day | Habits and norms | A team takes shape
Within days, each group had its own habits. Certain ways of doing things became the right way. Certain jokes became their jokes. Sherif's first expectation was being confirmed: put strangers together with shared goals, and a structure appears, with roles, status and a sense of belonging.

@list A13 living_room_day | Signs of a real group | Leaders and followers ;; Shared rules ;; Inside jokes ;; A name of their own
Psychologists would describe this as an in-group forming. You can spot it from the outside. There are leaders and followers, shared rules, inside jokes and, very soon, a name. That name is where our story turns.

@char A03 living_room_day | You've seen this too | Groups form fast
You have probably seen something like this in your own life. Put a few strangers on a project, a sports team or even a long train journey, and within days they have their own little language. Someone becomes the organiser. Someone becomes the joker. A shared joke turns into a password that only the group understands.

@char N20 bedroom_night | A name on a flag | The Eagles and the Rattlers
One group called itself the Eagles. The other became the Rattlers. The names were put on their shirts and on their flags. It might sound like a small thing, but a name and a flag turn a loose collection of kids into something you can defend.

@chapter N13 conspiracy_board | The first hint of the others
Near the end of that first week, each group discovered that it was not alone. The researchers let them hear the other group, and catch glimpses of them, across the camp. And something happened almost immediately.

@char A04 conspiracy_board | Us and them | Before a single game
Before any contest had even been announced, the language shifted. There were the boys in our group, and there were those other boys. Both groups pressed the staff to let them challenge the newcomers. Nobody had told them to dislike the others. They simply wanted to beat them.

@char A19 bedroom_night | A question to carry | Was the rivalry really natural?
Here is a question to keep with you. Was this eagerness something that grew naturally in the boys? Or were the adults already nudging things along? We'll come back to that, because the answer turned out to be more complicated than the textbooks suggest.

@list A10 bedroom_night | What we know so far | Strangers became two teams ;; Each team built its own identity ;; Rivalry appeared before any prize
So far, our case file shows something striking. Twenty two strangers became two teams in less than a week. Each team built a name, a flag and its own rules. And rivalry appeared before there was any prize at all. Now, let's see what happened when a prize was put on the table.
