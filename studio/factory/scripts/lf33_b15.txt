@chapter A13 cabin_fireplace | Regret
There's one feeling Mara keeps bumping into tonight, whichever column she looks at. What if I go, and it's a mistake? What if I stay, and I always wonder? It's regret, imagined in advance. And it's one of the most powerful forces in any big decision.

@char A10 living_room_day | Fear of regret | Before anything happens
Psychologists sometimes call this anticipated regret. We imagine how bad we'd feel if a choice went wrong, and that imagined feeling pushes us one way or the other. It often pushes us towards staying put, because, as we heard earlier, harm from doing something tends to feel worse than harm from doing nothing. At least at first.

@study A01 cafe_sunset | Thomas Gilovich and Victoria Medvec | Cornell University, 1995 | In the short run people regret actions; over a lifetime they regret what they did not do
Thomas Gilovich and Victoria Medvec spent years asking people about their regrets. In a review published in 1995, they described a striking pattern. In the short term, people tend to regret things they did, the hasty word, the bad decision. But when people look back across their whole lives, their most lasting regrets tend to be about things they didn't do.

@char A15 autumn_overlook | The things not done | The longest regrets
The course not taken. The friendship not kept up. The trip not made. The feeling not spoken. When people describe their biggest regrets, these are the ones that keep coming up. Why would inactions linger longer than actions?

@char A12 rooftop_dusk | Why inaction lingers | No ending
Gilovich and Medvec suggested several reasons. When we act and it goes wrong, we tend to deal with it, learn from it, make our peace. Our quiet helper gets to work. But a path not taken stays open in imagination forever. We can picture endless wonderful ways it might have turned out, and reality never comes along to correct us.

@char A19 bedroom_night | Mara's two regrets | Which lasts longer
That's an interesting thought for Mara. If she goes and it's hard, she'll probably regret it sharply for a while, then adapt and make sense of it. If she stays, and spends years wondering about the sea and her sister's children growing up, that wondering might be the kind of regret that lingers. It doesn't decide anything. But it's worth knowing.

@chapter A05 autumn_overlook | Bronze and silver
Regret depends a great deal on what we compare ourselves to. And the clearest example of that comes from the Olympic Games. In 1995, Victoria Medvec, Scott Madey and Thomas Gilovich studied footage of medal winners at the 1992 Summer Olympics in Barcelona.

@study A03 living_room_day | Victoria Medvec, Scott Madey and Thomas Gilovich | Cornell University, 1995 | Bronze medallists looked happier than silver medallists
They showed people clips of athletes at the end of their events and on the medal stand, without saying which medal each had won, and asked them to rate how happy each athlete looked. On average, the bronze medallists looked happier than the silver medallists, even though silver is the better result.

@char A20 cafe_sunset | What they compared | Gold, or nothing
Why? Because of what each athlete was comparing themselves to. The silver medallist could think, I nearly won gold. The bronze medallist could think, I nearly got nothing at all. Psychologists call these counterfactual thoughts, thoughts about what might have been. The silver medallist looks up. The bronze medallist looks down, and feels lucky.

@char A13 cabin_fireplace | Up or down | We choose a little
The lesson isn't that silver is worse. It's that the same outcome can feel very different, depending on what we hold it up against. And sometimes, we have a little choice about that. After any decision, we can compare it to a perfect imaginary alternative, or to something worse that we avoided.

Picture three medals resting side by side on dark cloth. Gold, silver, bronze. Their order seems simple. But the feelings of the people wearing them don't always follow that order at all.

@chapter A16 autumn_overlook | Now and later
There's one more thing that shapes Mara's regrets, and all our choices. The difference between now and later. The person deciding tonight isn't quite the same as the person who'll live with the result. And we tend to choose differently for each of them.

@study A01 rooftop_dusk | Daniel Read and Barbara van Leeuwen | Netherlands, 1998 | Choosing a snack for next week, people picked fruit; choosing for right now, chocolate
In a study published in 1998, Daniel Read and Barbara van Leeuwen offered office workers in Amsterdam a choice of snacks, some healthy, like fruit, and some less so, like chocolate. Some chose a snack to be delivered a week later. Others chose a snack to eat right away.

@bars A18 cafe_sunset | Chose the healthy snack | percent | Choosing for next week:74 ;; Choosing for right now:30
When choosing for next week, about three quarters of people picked the healthy option. When choosing for right now, about seventy percent picked the less healthy one. The same people, the same snacks. Only the timing had changed. For our future selves, we choose what's good. For our present selves, we choose what's nice.

@char A15 living_room_day | The same with films | Highbrow later
In a related study, from 1999, Daniel Read, George Loewenstein and Shobana Kalyanaraman found the same pattern with films. When people chose films to watch on later days, they picked more serious, worthy ones. When they chose a film for tonight, they picked something light and easy. The future self, it seems, is always a little more virtuous than the present one.

@char A19 cabin_fireplace | Tonight's Mara | And next year's Mara
For Mara, this cuts in an interesting way. Tonight's Mara is tired, comfortable, and dreading the boxes. Next year's Mara, in either place, will have forgotten the boxes completely. The cost of moving all falls now. The benefits, if there are any, come slowly, over years.

@char A10 rooftop_dusk | Speaking for the future | A fair hearing
It's worth giving next year's Mara a voice tonight. What would she want? Not the Mara of moving day, with tape and cardboard everywhere, but the Mara of an ordinary Tuesday, twelve months from now. Imagining her clearly can help balance a choice that's naturally tilted towards the present.

@list A20 autumn_overlook | Regret, gently | Inactions linger longer ;; Comparisons shape feelings ;; Give the future self a voice
So, a few gentle lessons about regret. Over a lifetime, the things we didn't do tend to linger longer than the things we did. How we feel about any outcome depends a great deal on what we compare it to. And our future self deserves a voice in decisions made by our present self.

@char A02 cafe_sunset | Not a rule | Just a weight
None of this means Mara should go. Some people move and regret it. Some stay and are glad, for the rest of their lives, that they did. The research on regret is not a rule. It's simply one more weight to place on the scale, gently, alongside everything else.
