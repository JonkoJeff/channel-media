@chapter A05 autumn_overlook | The critic who stays quiet
So far we have seen how convincing a dream can be, with its pictures, its feelings and its sense of place. But that raises a second puzzle, and it is a gentler one. If a dream is so strange, why does it almost never strike you as strange while you are inside it?

@char A12 cabin_fireplace | A familiar kind of dream | Nobody questions it
Think of a dream you may have had. You are sitting an exam, but the room is your grandmother's kitchen, and the teacher is somebody you knew at primary school, and none of this troubles you in the slightest. You simply pick up the pen and carry on. Only afterwards, in daylight, do you think, well, that was odd.

@char A03 attic_rain | The question | Why no alarm bell?
It is worth pausing on this, because it is the heart of what makes dreams feel real. Awake, you would notice such a mix-up in a second. Asleep, the part of you that notices seems to be missing, or at least very quiet. So where did it go?

@study A13 living_room_day | Maquet and colleagues | Nature, 1996 | Functional neuroanatomy of human rapid-eye-movement sleep and dreaming
A team led by Pierre Maquet scanned the brains of seven volunteers with positron emission tomography while they slept. The volunteers kept up steady REM sleep during the scans, and they recalled dreams on waking. Compared with quiet wakefulness, some regions were more active in REM and others were less active, and the pattern was striking.

@list A03 cafe_sunset | In REM sleep, the scans showed | More active: both amygdalae, which are tied to emotion ;; More active: the anterior cingulate cortex ;; More active: parts of the brainstem and the thalamus ;; Less active: the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex on both sides ;; Less active: parts of the parietal cortex, the posterior cingulate and the precuneus
Notice the two directions. Regions linked with emotion were busy, which fits with how intense a dream can feel. And a region at the front of the brain, the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, was quieter than it is when you are awake. That quiet region is the clue we are following tonight.

@char A10 attic_rain | What that region helps with | The front of the thinking brain
The dorsolateral prefrontal cortex is often linked with the sort of thinking that checks and plans. It helps you hold a goal in mind, weigh up whether something makes sense, and step back from the moment. When it is less active, as in REM, you might expect exactly what dreamers report: events accepted without much questioning, and little sense of standing back to ask whether this is real.

@list A12 living_room_day | The kind of thinking that may go quiet | Stepping back from the moment ;; Asking, does this make sense? ;; Remembering what you were doing a minute ago ;; Noticing that you are the one having the experience
This is a careful claim, and it is worth saying so. A less active region does not mean a switched off mind, and the link between brain activity and dream experience is still being worked out. But it gives a sensible picture. The dreamer is fully immersed, and the inner critic is simply not taking part.

@char A01 autumn_overlook | A gentle reading | Immersed, not fooled
It may help to think of it this way. In a dream you are not being fooled by a clever trick. You are caught up in the story, the way you can be in a good film, except that in the film you remember there is a cinema around you. In the dream, the cinema has gone, and so has the person who would say, this is only a film.

@char A09 attic_rain | The other half of the scan | Feeling without a brake
Remember that the same scans showed busy amygdalae. So a dream can bring strong feeling, while the part of you that would calm it down with a thought, it's only a noise, it's only a shadow, is working less than usual. That may be one reason a frightening dream can leave your heart racing, even after you have woken and know perfectly well that nothing happened. The feeling arrived before the reasoning had a chance to catch up, and it takes a few moments to settle.

@chapter A06 rooftop_dusk | When the story jumps
There is a second half to this puzzle, and it concerns the story itself. Dreams do not just contain odd things. They change scene without warning, and somehow that feels normal too.

@list A13 cafe_sunset | Three kinds of dream strangeness | Discontinuity: the story jumps ;; Incongruity: something appears out of place ;; Vagueness: a person or object is not quite defined
Dream researchers often sort the oddness of dreams into three kinds. Discontinuity is when the scene or the plot jumps. Incongruity is when something turns up that does not belong, like a cow in a lift. And vagueness is when you know someone is there, but could not say who. The psychiatrist Allan Hobson wrote a great deal about these features, and linked them to the unusual state of the sleeping brain.

@char A15 autumn_overlook | The jump you accept | One minute here, the next minute there
Here is the part that matters for our question. A cut like that would jar you at the cinema, and you would sit up. In a dream you glide through it. You are in the garden, and then you are on a train, and you do not ask how you got there, because the question never forms.

@curve A19 attic_rain | An imagined dream, not measured data | Time inside the dream | How strange it feels to you | 0.5 | The scene changes suddenly
If we drew the feeling of strangeness over the course of a dream, we might expect a sharp spike at every jump. Many dreamers describe something closer to a flat line. The scene changes, the strangeness does not register, and the story simply carries on from the new place.

#ai lf28_b05_01.jpg | Editorial still-life illustration of a long quiet corridor with many doors, each one slightly open onto a different softly lit room, warm lamp light, muted palette, calm and dreamlike mood, no people, no text, no logos

@photo lf28_b05_01.jpg | Many rooms, one corridor
Picture a corridor with many doors, each opening onto a different room. Awake, you would remember which door you came through. In a dream, you are suddenly in the next room, and it feels as though you had always been there. That, many researchers think, is something like how a dream story is assembled, one room after another.

@char A02 cafe_sunset | Missing context | You arrive without a past
Hobson and his colleagues also link the quieter frontal regions with patchy memory in dreams. In waking life, each moment arrives with a little history attached: where you were, what you were doing, why you came. In a dream, a scene often arrives with no history at all. You are simply in it. A sudden change of place feels normal because there was never a firm memory of the last place to compare it with.

@study A13 living_room_day | Windt | Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, 2017 | Dreams and Dreaming
In her review of the philosophy and science of dreaming, Jennifer Windt describes one idea that fits well here. It suggests that dreams arise when the brain's predictions run without being checked against the world, so that inferences are accepted without question. In waking life the signals from your senses keep correcting you. In sleep, that corrective loop is weaker, so a strange guess can simply stand.

@list A03 cafe_sunset | Why a jump does not jar | The dream scene arrives without its history ;; The checking voice is quieter ;; The brain's model keeps running anyway ;; The story simply continues
There is nothing mysterious in that, only a handful of small effects adding up. Each on its own is modest. Together they make a dream that flows past the very places where a waking mind would stop and say, hold on.

@char A10 cabin_fireplace | A familiar idea, from another side | A model with no one to correct it
This connects to something we met a little earlier, that the brain builds a model of the world whether the eyes are open or closed. When the eyes are open, the world pushes back. When they are closed, the model runs on its own, and there is much less to push back against.

@chapter A11 autumn_overlook | A fair look at the doubts
A calm channel should also tell you where the picture is less settled. And on this topic, several researchers have pushed back, with some good arguments.

@study A04 attic_rain | G. William Domhoff | DreamResearch.net, 2005 | The Hobson versus Solms debate
Domhoff, a dream researcher who has spent decades studying dream reports, argues that the strangeness of dreams is often overstated. In laboratory and home studies, he writes, most dreams turn out to be fairly coherent, and clearly impossible events appear in about ten per cent of reports, or fewer. He also notes that other parts of the prefrontal cortex, such as the anterior cingulate and the medial and ventral areas, stay active in REM sleep.

@stat A14 conspiracy_board | Dream reports with clearly impossible events | About 10% or fewer | in laboratory and home studies, according to Domhoff
That is worth holding next to everything above. If most dreams are ordinary scenes, with familiar rooms, familiar people and plausible events, then the missing critic is only part of the story. A sleeping mind can accept the odd, but it does not produce the odd all the time.

@list A13 cafe_sunset | Where the evidence stands | The dorsolateral prefrontal cortex is less active in REM ;; That fits with weaker questioning inside dreams ;; Reasoning is not completely absent, though ;; Most dreams are less bizarre than we remember
Windt makes a related point from the philosophical side: reasoning in dreams is not uniformly absent. Sometimes it is present but corrupted, so that a dreamer can be misled by thinking that only appears sensible. That is a rather lovely detail. The dreaming mind is not simply asleep. It is working, with a few of its checks turned down.

@char A13 cabin_fireplace | Two things can be true | A quiet critic, and a mostly ordinary story
It would be easy to leave this chapter with a tidy slogan, and a calm channel should resist that. The scans point to a quieter prefrontal region. The dream reports point to stories that are often rather ordinary. Both findings can be right, because a mind with fewer checks does not have to produce nonsense. It only has to stop asking whether what it is producing is sensible.

@char A08 attic_rain | Why we remember the odd ones | The strange dreams are the ones we tell
There is also a simple human reason that dreams seem stranger than they are. The ones we remember, and the ones we tell at breakfast, are the ones with a talking dog or a flooded staircase. The quiet dreams, about walking to a shop or answering an email, slip away, and we conclude that dreams are all wild.

@char A02 cabin_fireplace | What you might notice | A small, kind experiment
If you would like to try something gentle, notice your own dreams over a week or two. Do not force it. Just see how many are truly impossible, and how many are simply a little off, an ordinary place with one detail wrong. Many people find the second kind is more common than they expected.

@list A12 living_room_day | Putting it together | The dream feels real because the brain builds a full scene ;; The critical voice is quieter ;; Scene changes are accepted without a question ;; The oddest dreams are the ones we remember
This is how the two halves of the puzzle fit. The first half, which we covered earlier, is about the vividness of the scene. The second half is about the missing doubt. Together they give you a dream that looks real, feels real, and is almost never questioned until you wake.

@char A05 autumn_overlook | A small waking parallel | When you are absorbed
There is a gentle echo of this in everyday life. When you are deeply absorbed in a book or a favourite programme, you accept what you are shown without checking it against the facts. You may only notice the oddity when someone speaks your name and you surface. A dream is a much deeper version of that absorption, and waking is the voice that calls your name.

@char A13 rooftop_dusk | A thought to take with you | Doubt needs a quiet moment too
It is rather comforting to know that questioning is a skill the waking brain does so smoothly that we hardly notice it. Only when it quietens, in sleep, do we see how much of our everyday calm rests on it. Waking up, in that sense, is partly the return of the one who says, wait, is this right?

@char A18 rooftop_dusk | Looking ahead | The body that stays still
There is one more thing worth wondering about. If the dreaming mind can be so convinced, and the story can be so vivid, why do we not get up and act it out? Next, let's look at the quiet mechanism that keeps your body still while the story plays on.
