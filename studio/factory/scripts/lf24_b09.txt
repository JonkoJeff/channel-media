@chapter A19 living_room_day | What spreads, and why
Every day, billions of items are shared online, and a tiny fraction of them go far. Why do some posts travel to millions while most go nowhere? And does the truth have any advantage over a falsehood in that race? These questions have been studied with large datasets, and the answers are among the more uncomfortable findings in this whole field.

@char A04 attic_rain | The setting | Millions of cascades
A research team at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, led by Soroush Vosoughi, Deb Roy and Sinan Aral, set out to study how true and false news moved across Twitter. They had access to the platform's full archive, which let them follow individual stories from the first post to the last reshare, something that few researchers had been able to do.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Vosoughi, Roy & Aral | Science, 2018 | The spread of true and false news online
They traced about one hundred and twenty-six thousand stories that had been shared by roughly three million people, more than four and a half million times, between 2006 and 2017. The stories had been checked by independent fact-checking organisations and classified as true, false or mixed. Then the team compared how far and how fast each kind spread.

@stat A14 conspiracy_board | Stories traced by the MIT team | About 126,000 | shared by roughly 3 million people on Twitter between 2006 and 2017
The finding was striking. False stories reached more people, and spread faster, than true ones. A false story was about seventy percent more likely to be reshared than a true one. And true stories took around six times as long as false ones to reach fifteen hundred people.

@list A03 living_room_day | The MIT findings | False news spread farther and faster ;; The difference was strongest for political news ;; False stories were seen as more novel ;; They evoked more surprise and disgust
The authors checked a likely explanation, which was that automated accounts were driving the spread. When they removed bots from the data, the pattern stayed the same. Humans, not machines, were doing most of the spreading, and the difference lay in what people found interesting.

@chapter A13 rooftop_dusk | Why novelty wins
Why would a false story have the edge? The authors suggested novelty. A false story can say anything at all, so it can be more surprising than the truth, which has to match reality. And people like to share things that are new, because sharing new information makes us look informed and interesting.

@char A12 living_room_day | A cautious note | One platform, one period
It's worth keeping some balance. The study looked at Twitter, in a period before several changes in how it worked, and at stories that had been fact-checked, which are not a random sample of everything people share. The results shouldn't be taken to mean that most things online are false. They show that when falsehoods do spread, they can spread effectively.

@chapter A12 living_room_day | The power of moral emotion
Another team asked a different question. Not true or false, but what kind of language makes a post spread? William Brady, Julian Wills, John Jost, Joshua Tucker and Jay Van Bavel analysed more than half a million tweets about three contested political topics: gun control, same-sex marriage and climate change.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Brady, Wills, Jost, Tucker & Van Bavel | Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 2017 | Emotion shapes the diffusion of moralized content in social networks
They counted words that were both moral and emotional, such as shame, evil, blame and fight. They found that each additional moral-emotional word in a tweet increased the likelihood of it being retweeted by about twenty percent. And the spread was mostly within political groups, not between them, so the language was rallying people who already agreed.

@char A04 attic_rain | What this shows | Outrage travels in a circle
The finding adds a second piece to the picture. Posts that spark moral emotion tend to be shared, and they tend to be shared among people who already share the view. A post that excites your side may barely register on the other. That can make a debate look intense, while the two sides never meet.

@list A12 living_room_day | What helps a post travel | Surprise and novelty ;; Strong emotion, especially moral emotion ;; Alignment with a group's identity ;; Being easy to share quickly
None of these four has anything to do with accuracy. A fact-check can help, but it travels slowly, because it's less novel and less emotional. That asymmetry is why a correction rarely catches up with the original.

@chapter A06 cafe_sunset | Do people want to share lies?
It might seem that people who share falsehoods want to deceive, or believe whatever suits them. But the research is more nuanced. Gordon Pennycook and David Rand at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology and Yale have argued that many people share false stories mainly because they aren't thinking about accuracy at all.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Pennycook, McPhetres, Zhang, Lu & Rand | Psychological Science, 2020 | Fighting COVID-19 misinformation on social media: experimental evidence for a scalable accuracy-nudge intervention
In experiments, people were willing to judge headlines as true or false reasonably well when asked. But when asked whether they would share them, they paid much less attention to accuracy, and more to whether a story would be interesting or would please their followers. When participants were simply reminded to think about accuracy, by rating one neutral headline first, the quality of what they said they'd share improved.

@char A01 cafe_sunset | The implication | A moment of thought helps
This suggests that a lot of sharing happens in a hurry. We're on a sofa, thumb moving, reacting to how a post makes us feel. A short pause before reposting, asking is this true, can help, and it needs no special skill. In the studies, a tiny nudge made a measurable difference, though the size of the effect was modest.

@list A03 living_room_day | A pause before sharing | Does this seem too perfect or too outrageous? ;; Who published it, and could I check? ;; Has anyone reliable reported it? ;; Would I be comfortable if it turned out to be wrong?
Four questions that take about ten seconds. The research doesn't promise that they'll stop every error. But a culture in which more people paused for a few seconds would be one in which false stories travelled a little less far.

@photo lf24_b09_01.jpg | Boats on a river
Paper boats on a river, a few carried far ahead of the rest. The MIT study found that a story's journey depends less on how true it is than on how much current it catches: surprise, emotion and the pull of a group.

@list A03 living_room_day | A short checklist before sharing | Read past the headline ;; Check where it came from ;; Notice how it makes you feel ;; Wait one minute before posting
Next time you feel the urge to share something that stirs a strong emotion, treat the emotion as a signal to slow down. The research we've covered suggests that surprise and moral outrage are exactly what help a false story travel. A one-minute pause, a quick look at the source, and a read beyond the headline, take very little effort. They won't catch everything. But if enough of us did it, the reach of misleading posts would shrink, and the people who trust us would be better served.

@list A12 living_room_day | Words to keep from this chapter | Misinformation ;; Novelty ;; Moral-emotional language ;; Accuracy nudge
Four terms about spreading. Misinformation is false or misleading content, shared with or without intent. Novelty is the quality of seeming new, which helps a story travel. Moral-emotional language uses words of blame and outrage, and boosts sharing within groups. And an accuracy nudge is a small reminder to think about whether something is true.

@char A15 cabin_fireplace | A gentle thought | A pause is a kindness
When you pause before you share, you protect yourself, and the people who trust you. It's not about suspicion. It's about taking one breath before you give something your name. The habit is small, and it travels, because the people around you notice it and follow.

@char A08 cabin_fireplace | Looking back | What spreading taught us
So what did we learn? That in a large study, false news spread farther and faster than true news. That moral and emotional words boosted sharing, mostly within groups. That many people share without thinking about accuracy. And that a small pause helps.
