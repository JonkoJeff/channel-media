@chapter A15 autumn_overlook | Toward, not away
For a long time, psychologists sorted emotions into two simple boxes. Pleasant ones and unpleasant ones. Joy and contentment on one side. Fear, sadness and anger on the other. And on the surface, that seems to make sense. Nobody enjoys being angry. So surely anger belongs in the same box as fear and sadness?

@char A10 cabin_fireplace | A different question | Not pleasant or unpleasant
But some researchers started asking a different question. Not, does this emotion feel good or bad? But, which way does it push you? Does it pull you toward something, or does it make you want to move away? And when you sort emotions that way, anger suddenly looks very different from its supposed neighbours.

@study A13 living_room_day | Charles Carver and Eddie Harmon-Jones | A review in Psychological Bulletin | published in 2009
In 2009, two psychologists, Charles Carver and Eddie Harmon-Jones, published a long review of the evidence in the journal Psychological Bulletin. Its title said it plainly. Anger is an approach-related affect. In other words, anger isn't an emotion of retreat. It's an emotion that moves us forward.

@list A12 cafe_sunset | Which way an emotion pushes | Fear: pull away ;; Sadness: slow down ;; Anger: move toward
Think about it this way. Fear tends to pull us away from things. It says, get out, get safe. Sadness tends to slow us down, to make us withdraw for a while. But anger does something else. Anger pushes us toward the problem. Toward the person, the obstacle, the thing that's in the way.

@char A03 rooftop_dusk | A surprising family | Anger and eagerness
And that puts anger, in this one respect, in a surprising family. Alongside things like eagerness and desire, the feelings that make us reach for something we want. Anger feels unpleasant, yes. But it has the same forward lean. It's the feeling of wanting something to change, and wanting to be the one who changes it.

@char A19 attic_rain | Sam leans in | Not away
Now look at Sam again. When the message arrived, he didn't want to hide from it. He didn't put the phone away and look out of the window. He leaned in. He started typing straight away. That's anger's direction. Toward the brother, toward the conversation, toward setting things straight.

@photo lf36_b04_01.jpg | A path that leads somewhere
Picture a narrow footbridge at dusk, leading across a stream toward a lit window. That's a gentle way to imagine anger's direction. It doesn't run away from the problem. It wants to cross the gap. The question is only how it crosses, carefully, or all at once.

@chapter A12 living_room_day | What anger is for
So if anger pushes us toward things, what is it actually for? Why would a feeling like this have stayed with us for so long? It's easy to think of anger as a kind of mistake, a glitch we'd be better off without. But that's probably not the whole story.

@char A13 cabin_fireplace | A signal | Something matters here
One helpful way to think about anger is as a signal. It tends to show up when something we care about is being blocked, ignored or treated unfairly. Our goals, our plans, our sense of what's right, our sense of being respected. Anger says, wait, this matters, and something here isn't okay.

@char A05 autumn_overlook | Energy to fix it | Not just a signal
And it isn't only a signal. It also comes with energy. Remember Cannon's racing heart and ready muscles. Anger gives us the push to speak up, to defend someone, to refuse something unfair, to keep going when an obstacle is in the way. Without some of that energy, a lot of good things in the world would never have happened.

@list A15 cafe_sunset | When anger can help | Noticing something unfair ;; Speaking up for someone ;; Setting a boundary ;; Pushing past an obstacle
Think of the times when a bit of anger might actually help. Noticing that something is unfair. Speaking up for someone who's being treated badly. Setting a boundary with someone who keeps crossing it. Pushing past an obstacle instead of giving up. In each of these, anger is doing something useful.

@char A19 cabin_fireplace | The fairness alarm | Quiet but strong
Many people notice that their strongest flashes of anger aren't really about themselves at all. They're about fairness. Someone pushing in front of an older person in a queue. A child being spoken to unkindly. A friend being left out. Anger there works like a quiet alarm, saying this isn't how things should be.

@char A03 living_room_day | A sense of self | Being seen properly
And sometimes, anger is about how we're seen. Being dismissed, being talked over, being treated as though we don't count. Anger pushes back against that. It insists, in its own clumsy way, that we matter too. That's not a flaw. That's part of how people protect their place in a relationship and in the world.

@char A10 bedroom_night | And when it costs us | The same energy
But of course, the same forward energy can also cost us. It can push us toward a sharp word we regret. Toward a message sent too fast. Toward an argument that didn't need to happen. Anger is very good at moving us. It's not always very good at choosing the direction.

@quote A13 living_room_day | Anger is good at moving us, and not always good at steering. | A way to hold both sides
Here's one way to hold both sides of anger at the same time. Anger is good at moving us, and not always good at steering. It provides the energy, but it doesn't always provide the wisdom. And so much of what helps with anger, as we'll see, is about adding a little steering.

@char A03 cafe_sunset | Sam's signal | What does it point at?
So what is Sam's anger pointing at? On the surface, it's about three words. You forgot again? But underneath, it might be pointing at something more. Maybe Sam feels he's always the one who gets reminded. Maybe he's tired of feeling like the unreliable brother. Maybe he's simply had a long day, and wants someone to be on his side.

@char A18 rooftop_dusk | Useful information | If he listens
None of those things are silly. They're real and they're human. And if Sam can hear what his anger is pointing at, it becomes useful information. It might lead to a conversation that actually helps. But if he just acts on the first rush, the information gets lost, and all that's left is a sharp message.

@char A12 autumn_overlook | Not good or bad | Just powerful
So anger is neither simply good nor simply bad. It's powerful. It points at things that matter to us, and it gives us the energy to act on them. What it doesn't always give us is the pause in which to decide how. And that pause, as we'll keep discovering tonight, is where most of the real work happens.

@list A13 cabin_fireplace | What anger is | An approach emotion ;; A signal that something matters ;; Energy to act ;; Not always good at steering
So let's put it together. Anger is an approach emotion. It pushes us toward things rather than away. It's a signal that something matters, often that something feels blocked or unfair. It brings energy to act. And it isn't always very good at steering that energy wisely.

@char A10 attic_rain | Listening first | Then deciding
So perhaps the best way to treat anger isn't to fight it, and isn't to obey it either. It's to listen to it first. What is it pointing at? What does it want to protect? And then, once we've heard it, to decide what to do. That sounds simple, and in the heat of the moment it really isn't. But it's a direction worth leaning toward.

@char A05 living_room_day | Blocked goals | The next clue
And that word, blocked, is going to be our next clue. Because one of the oldest ideas in the psychology of anger is all about what happens when something stands between us and what we want. It was first put forward in 1939, by a group of researchers at Yale. And it begins with a very familiar feeling. Frustration.
