@chapter A20 street_day | Myth two: we know what will make us happy
Let's go back to Nora, a few months before she moved. She's sitting in her old room, the one that faced a brick wall, scrolling through pictures of flats by the sea. And she's doing something we all do, many times a day, usually without noticing. She's imagining a future, and asking herself how it will feel.

@char A12 living_room_day | A golden picture | Every single morning
In her imagination, every morning by the sea is golden. She pictures herself waking up, opening the curtains, and feeling that same rush of joy, again and again. She doesn't picture the rainy Tuesdays, or the busy mornings with her head in her phone, or the moment, six weeks in, when the view becomes just the view.

@quote A03 cafe_sunset | I know what will make me happy. | Myth number two
And that's tonight's second myth. I know what will make me happy. It feels like the one thing we can be sure of. We may not know much about the weather or the future of the world, but surely, we know our own hearts. We know what we want, and roughly how it will feel when we get it.

@char A13 autumn_overlook | Forecasting feelings | A science of guessing
Over the past few decades, a group of psychologists has studied exactly how good we are at that kind of guessing. They call it affective forecasting. Affective just means to do with feelings. So it's forecasting feelings, the way a weather forecaster forecasts rain. And just like weather forecasts, our feeling forecasts turn out to have some very consistent errors.

@study A18 living_room_day | Wilson and Gilbert | Reviews of affective forecasting | published in 2003 and 2005
Two of the leading researchers in this area, Timothy Wilson and Daniel Gilbert, summarised years of this work in a long review published in 2003, and a shorter one in 2005 with the lovely title Affective Forecasting: Knowing What to Want. One of the most reliable patterns they described has a name that sounds technical but is easy to understand. The impact bias.

@list A15 cafe_sunset | The impact bias | We guess how we'll feel ;; We overestimate how strongly ;; We overestimate how long ;; Good and bad alike
The impact bias is our tendency to overestimate how strongly, and for how long, a future event will affect us. It works for good things and bad things alike. We think a success will thrill us for longer than it does. We think a disappointment will hurt for longer than it does. In both cases, we underestimate how quickly life goes back to feeling like life.

@study A10 cabin_fireplace | Gilbert, Pinel, Wilson, Blumberg and Wheatley | Journal of Personality and Social Psychology | published in 1998
One of the best-known studies on this was published in 1998, by Daniel Gilbert, Elizabeth Pinel, Timothy Wilson, Stephen Blumberg and Thalia Wheatley. They wanted to see how people predict their feelings about big, uncertain outcomes in their own lives. So they turned to a group of people facing a very big outcome indeed. Young university teachers, waiting to find out whether they would be given a permanent position.

@char A19 attic_rain | A long wait | Years of hoping
In American universities, this decision is called tenure, and for a young academic it can feel like the most important verdict of their working life. People wait years for it. So the researchers asked assistant professors how happy they expected to be in the years after the decision, if it went their way, and if it didn't. And they also asked people who had already been through it, some who had been given tenure, and some who hadn't.

@char A12 living_room_day | The result | Not as expected
The forecasters expected a big difference. They imagined that being turned down would leave them clearly less happy for years. But when the researchers looked at people who had actually been through the decision, those who had been turned down weren't nearly as unhappy as the forecasters had predicted. The years after the verdict looked far more alike than anyone expected.

@list A13 cafe_sunset | Immune neglect | We recover more than we expect ;; We find new meanings ;; We reframe the story ;; We forget we'll do this
The researchers called one cause of this immune neglect. Just as the body has an immune system that fights off illness, the mind seems to have something like a psychological immune system. After a disappointment, we find new meanings, new reasons, new ways to tell the story. And when we forecast the future, we forget that this system will be there to help us.

@photo lf35_b04_01.jpg | Imagining the future
Picture a desk on a quiet afternoon, a notebook open, someone trying to imagine how a future will feel. It's such an ordinary thing to do. And yet the picture we draw in our heads is almost always too bright, or too dark, and almost always too lasting. The future, when it arrives, tends to feel more ordinary than we imagined.

@study A20 autumn_overlook | Wilson, Wheatley, Meyers, Gilbert and Axsom | Journal of Personality and Social Psychology | published in 2000
Why do we make these errors? A study published in 2000, by Timothy Wilson, Thalia Wheatley, Jonathan Meyers, Daniel Gilbert and Danny Axsom, pointed to one reason. They called it focalism. When we imagine a future event, we focus on that one event, and forget about everything else that will be going on in our lives at the same time.

@char A15 cafe_sunset | Football fans | A big game
To test it, they asked university football fans to predict how happy they would be in the days after an important game, depending on whether their team won or lost. The fans expected the result to colour their mood for days. But some of the fans were first asked to do something simple. To write down how they would actually spend those days. Classes, meals, friends, chores.

@char A03 living_room_day | A diary of the future | A smaller forecast
And those fans, the ones who had first pictured the rest of their days, made more moderate predictions. Once they remembered that life would be full of other things, the game seemed to shrink in their imagined future. Not because it didn't matter, but because it was only one thing among many.

@char A19 cabin_fireplace | Nora's focus | Only the sea
That's exactly what Nora did, sitting in her old room. She pictured the sea, and only the sea. She didn't picture the meetings, the laundry, the late trains, the friends she'd text, the days she'd be tired or busy or lost in her own thoughts. In her forecast, the view filled the whole frame. In real life, it would be one small part of a crowded picture.

@study A12 living_room_day | Dunn, Wilson and Gilbert | Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin | published in 2003
There's one more study I'd love to tell you about, because it's so close to Nora's story. It was published in 2003, by Elizabeth Dunn, Timothy Wilson and Daniel Gilbert. It was about students choosing where to live. At Harvard, first-year students are randomly assigned to one of the university's residential houses, where they'll live for the rest of their time there.

@char A13 autumn_overlook | Good houses and bad | A big decision, or so it seemed
Students cared a great deal about where they ended up. Some houses were thought of as desirable, close to the centre, with lovely buildings. Others were thought of as much less so. So before the assignments, the researchers asked students to predict how happy they would be in each house. And later, once they had lived there for a while, they asked them how happy they actually were.

@list A18 cabin_fireplace | Dunn and colleagues, 2003 | Students predicted big differences ;; Actual differences were small ;; They focused on buildings and location ;; Friends and social life mattered more
The students had predicted big differences. They expected to be clearly happier in the desirable houses, and clearly less happy in the others. But when the researchers looked at how they actually felt, the differences were small. The students had focused on the buildings and the location. What really shaped their happiness was something they had underweighted. The people they lived with, and the friendships they made.

#ai lf35_b04_02.jpg | Editorial still-life of an old brick university courtyard in autumn, a wooden bench under a tree with fallen leaves, warm late afternoon light, calm quiet mood, no people, no text, no logos

@photo lf35_b04_02.jpg | The house matters less than we think
Think of an old courtyard, with its bench and its falling leaves. It's easy to believe that a lovely place will make a lovely life. Sometimes it helps, a little. But again and again, research finds that we overestimate what a place will do for us, and underestimate what people will do for us. We'll come back to that idea later tonight.

@char A20 cafe_sunset | Not foolish | Just human
None of this means the students were foolish, or that Nora was wrong to want the sea. Wanting a beautiful place is a perfectly good thing to want. It just means that our imagination is a slightly unreliable narrator. It zooms in on one bright detail, forgets the rest of life, and forgets how quickly we'll get used to things, good and bad.

@char A05 living_room_day | A kinder lesson | Room for surprise
And there's a kind lesson hidden in that. If our forecasts are too extreme, then our fears are often too extreme as well. The things we dread usually turn out more bearable than we imagine. And the things we long for are rarely the only route to a good life. There are usually other doors, ones we didn't even think to picture.

But maybe you're thinking something else by now. Maybe the reason we drift back, and the reason big changes do less than we expect, is that happiness was never really up to us at all. Maybe each of us is simply born with a certain amount of it, the way we're born with a certain height. That's our next myth. And it begins with twins.
