CHAPTER: The Morning After the Sabbath
Every Christian service on Easter morning begins with the same astonishing claim: that the man who was crucified on Friday was alive again on the third day. For Christians, it is the center of everything. What do our earliest sources actually say about that morning? || Empty tomb, Church of the Holy Sepulchre, Jerusalem || wikimedia
Picture a rock-cut tomb outside the walls of Jerusalem, in the grey light before sunrise. A stone has been rolled across the entrance. A few women are walking toward it with spices in their hands, and none of them expects anything but grief. || Garden Tomb, Jerusalem || wikimedia
In this video we follow the accounts of the resurrection in the four Gospels, the oldest written testimony in the letters of Paul, and the way historians, theologians and believers of other faiths have read them. We tell it with respect for Christian belief, and with attention to the evidence. || The Resurrection, Piero della Francesca, Sansepolcro || wikimedia
As in our earlier episodes, we keep the layers apart. What the texts report. What the earliest writers add. What scholars argue about those texts. And what the faith itself teaches. Each layer has its own kind of evidence, and its own limits. || Codex Vaticanus, Gospel manuscript || wikimedia
It may surprise many viewers to learn that the earliest written witness to the resurrection is not a Gospel at all. It is a letter from Paul to the Christians of Corinth, written in the early fifties, years before Mark, Matthew, Luke or John were composed. || Papyrus 46, Pauline letters, Chester Beatty || wikimedia
We will come to that letter in a moment. First, it helps to remember how the story of the crucifixion ended in our last episode, because everything that follows depends on it. || Joseph of Arimathea, Entombment, Caravaggio || wikimedia
CHAPTER: Friday Evening, Saturday Silence
All four Gospels agree that Jesus died on a Friday, before the Sabbath began at sunset. A man named Joseph of Arimathea, described as a respected member of the council, went to Pilate and asked for the body. Pilate allowed it. || Entombment of Christ, Titian, Louvre || wikimedia
Joseph wrapped the body in linen and laid it in a new tomb cut from the rock. A large stone was rolled across the entrance. Because the Sabbath was beginning, there was no time for the full burial customs, and the women who watched from a distance noted where he was laid. || Rock-cut tomb, first century Jerusalem || wikimedia
Then came the Sabbath, a day of rest in which no work could be done. For the followers of Jesus, it must have been a day of fear and bewilderment. Their teacher had been executed as a criminal, and some of them had run away. || Jerusalem, Mount of Olives panorama, 19th century || loc
John's Gospel says that they were meeting behind locked doors out of fear of the authorities. Luke says that some women prepared spices and ointments before the Sabbath. In every telling, the mood on Saturday is the mood of people who believe that the story is over. || Upper Room, Cenacle, Jerusalem || wikimedia
Matthew alone adds a detail about that day. He says that the chief priests and Pharisees went to Pilate and asked for a guard at the tomb, because Jesus had said he would rise after three days, and they feared that the disciples might steal the body and claim it had happened. || Pilate and the Jews, Matthew 27:62-66 || text-card
Pilate told them they had a guard and could make it as secure as they knew how. According to Matthew, they sealed the stone and set the watch. We will return to this passage later, because it is found only in one Gospel, and scholars have discussed it for centuries. || Roman soldiers at the tomb, Ottheinrich Bible || wikimedia
The Hebrew Scriptures were also on the minds of the earliest Christians as they looked back at these days. The prophet Jonah spent three days inside a great fish, and the Gospel of Matthew has Jesus compare that sign to his own time in the earth. || Jonah and the Whale, Pieter Lastman || wikimedia
Psalm sixteen, quoted in the book of Acts, speaks of God not abandoning his faithful one to the grave. The prophet Hosea says that after two days God will revive his people, and on the third day raise them up. Christians would later read these lines in the light of Easter. || Hosea 6:2, Psalm 16:10 || text-card
CHAPTER: What the Disciples Expected
To understand why the first Easter was such a shock, we need to ask what Jesus' followers were expecting. Many Jews in the first century hoped for a Messiah, an anointed king of David's line, who would free Israel, restore the Temple and bring God's justice. A Messiah who was executed by Rome did not fit that hope. || Anointed king, David, Michelangelo statue || wikimedia
There were other movements in those decades led by charismatic figures. Josephus tells of a man named Theudas who led a crowd to the Jordan, promising that the river would part, and was beheaded by the Romans. He also mentions a man called Judas the Galilean, who resisted the census and was killed. || Josephus, Jewish Antiquities 20.97-98 || text-card
What happened to those movements? Some dispersed. Others, as in the case of Judas the Galilean, found new leaders, often a relative of the dead man, who took up the same cause. Historians have noted that in those cases, the followers did not usually claim that their executed leader had been raised. || Roman Judea, first century, map || map
Nor did the Gospels present the disciples as ready. In Mark, Jesus predicts his suffering and resurrection three times, but the disciples do not understand, and Peter rebukes him. At the arrest, they all flee. For a movement leader, this is a failure of nerve, and the Gospels do not hide it. || Peter's Denial, Rembrandt || wikimedia
This is also why the language of the Emmaus travelers, we had hoped, rings so true. Whatever else we think of the stories, they describe people who expected nothing, and who found themselves unable to stay silent. It is the sudden turn from despair to proclamation that historians find so interesting. || Supper at Emmaus, Velázquez || wikimedia
There is also a danger of reading the story backwards. We know the ending, and the first witnesses did not. It helps to imagine them as people who have just lost their teacher and their hope, hiding in Jerusalem, afraid that they will be next, and unsure what to do with the Sabbath. || Jerusalem at dusk, 19th century lithograph || loc
CHAPTER: The Women at the Tomb
SHORT: The Gospel That Ends in Fear
The oldest of the four Gospels, Mark, was probably written around the year seventy. Its account of the resurrection is the shortest and the strangest. Three women, Mary Magdalene, Mary the mother of James, and Salome, come to the tomb early on the first day of the week. || Three Marys at the Tomb, Hubert and Jan van Eyck || wikimedia
They wonder who will roll the stone away from the entrance, since it is very large. But when they look up, they see that the stone has already been rolled back. Entering the tomb, they find a young man in a white robe seated on the right side, and they are alarmed. || Rolling stone tomb, Herodian family tomb Jerusalem || wikimedia
The young man tells them not to be afraid. He says that they are looking for Jesus of Nazareth, who was crucified, and that he has been raised and is not here. He points to the place where the body had been laid. || Mark 16:1-6 || text-card
He then gives them a message for the disciples and for Peter: Jesus is going ahead of you into Galilee, where you will see him, just as he told you. In the oldest manuscripts of Mark, the Gospel ends with the women fleeing from the tomb, trembling and afraid. || Codex Sinaiticus, Gospel of Mark || wikimedia
Mark's last words in the oldest manuscripts are that they said nothing to anyone, for they were afraid. That is an abrupt ending, and it troubled later readers so much that other endings were added to the Gospel in the following centuries. || Codex Vaticanus, end of Mark || wikimedia
Most modern translations note that the passage known as the longer ending, which describes appearances and the great commission, is missing from the earliest manuscripts. It seems to have been added by a later hand. It is part of the church's Bible, but it is not part of Mark's original text. || Mark 16:9-20, manuscripts || text-card
Why would Mark end so suddenly? Scholars are divided. Some think that the original ending was lost. Others think that Mark intended an open ending, one that leaves the reader to decide what to do with the news. We cannot be sure, and the debate is a good example of how careful this study has to be. || Mark, Evangelist, Book of Kells || wikimedia
What does seem clear is that Mark knows of an empty tomb, a message about Galilee, and the fear of the first witnesses. The women, in a society where their testimony counted for little in a court, are the ones who bring the news. That detail will matter when historians weigh the evidence. || Mary Magdalene, Donatello || wikimedia
Notice also what Mark does not describe. He does not show us the resurrection itself. No Gospel does. There is no scene of Jesus walking out of the tomb. The texts describe only what happened afterwards: an empty place, a messenger, and later, appearances. || Resurrection, Noli me tangere, Fra Angelico || wikimedia
CHAPTER: Matthew's Earthquake and the Guard
Matthew, probably written a decade or so after Mark, tells the story with more drama. There is an earthquake. An angel of the Lord descends from heaven, rolls back the stone, and sits on it, with an appearance like lightning and clothing white as snow. || The Angel at the Tomb, Matthew 28, Duccio || wikimedia
The guards, says Matthew, shook with fear and became like dead men. The angel then speaks to the women, telling them not to be afraid, that Jesus has risen as he said, and that they should tell the disciples to go to Galilee. || Women at the Tomb, Duccio di Buoninsegna || wikimedia
As the women hurry away with fear and great joy, Matthew writes that Jesus himself meets them. They take hold of his feet and worship him. He tells them not to be afraid and repeats the message: go and tell my brothers to go to Galilee, and there they will see me. || Christ appears to the Marys, Giotto, Scrovegni Chapel || wikimedia
Meanwhile, some of the guards go into the city and report to the chief priests what has happened. The priests meet with the elders, give the soldiers a large sum of money, and tell them to say that the disciples came by night and stole the body while they slept. || Matthew 28:11-15 || text-card
Matthew then adds a remark that is unusual in the Gospels. He says that the soldiers took the money and did as they were told, and that this story had been widely told among the Jews to this very day. He is writing, in other words, in the middle of an argument. || The Bribing of the Guards, Mt 28 manuscript illumination || wikimedia
That sentence is valuable to historians for a surprising reason. It suggests that when Matthew wrote, a rival explanation was already circulating: that the body had been taken. Nobody, in that dispute, seems to have said that the tomb was still occupied. The argument was over why it was empty. || Jerusalem Old City, Church of the Holy Sepulchre exterior || wikimedia
Many scholars add a caution. The guard appears only in Matthew, and the other Gospels do not mention soldiers at the tomb. Some think the story was told to answer the rumor about the stolen body. Others think it reflects memory of an actual guard. Both views are found in serious scholarship. || Guard at the tomb, Matthew 27:65 || text-card
In Matthew, the story ends on a mountain in Galilee. The eleven disciples go there as Jesus had told them. When they see him, they worship, though Matthew candidly adds that some doubted. That honest note, that some doubted, appears in a Gospel written by a believer for believers. || Sermon on the Mount, Carl Bloch || wikimedia
Then Jesus speaks the words that Christians call the Great Commission: go and make disciples of all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father, and the Son, and the Holy Spirit, and teaching them to obey what I have commanded. And I am with you always, to the end of the age. || Matthew 28:16-20 || text-card
