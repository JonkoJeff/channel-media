@chapter A01 cafe_sunset | A door that changed people
Imagine you're walking across a university campus in the late 1990s, and a man with a map stops you to ask for directions. As you start to explain, two workers carrying a large wooden door walk right between you and him. When the door has passed, the man in front of you is a different man, with different clothes and a different voice.

@char A12 living_room_day | The experiment | Daniel Simons and Daniel Levin
This is one of the best-known experiments on what psychologists call change blindness. It was run in 1998 by Daniel Simons and Daniel Levin at Cornell University. A researcher, playing the part of a lost pedestrian, approached strangers, and during the conversation, was swapped for another person who had been carrying the door.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Simons & Levin | Psychonomic Bulletin & Review, 1998 | Failure to detect changes to people during a real-world interaction
The two men were different in height, build, hair and clothing, and their voices were different too. After the swap, the researchers continued the conversation as if nothing had happened. Then they asked the person if they'd noticed anything unusual. And a surprising share of people had noticed nothing at all.

@stat A14 conspiracy_board | Noticed the switch | About 50% | of the pedestrians in the door study
Roughly half of the pedestrians failed to notice that the person they were speaking to had been replaced. It's a rounded figure, and the result depended on who was being asked. When the person asking was a construction worker, and the pedestrians were students, they noticed even less. People seemed to notice the person less when they categorised them as being from a different group.

@char A10 attic_rain | A striking detail | Who the stranger seemed to be
That detail is interesting. In a follow-up, when the stranger was dressed as a construction worker and the passers-by were students, most didn't notice the switch. When the stranger looked like another student, they noticed more often. The brain seemed to keep a thin summary of the person, which was something like a student, a stranger asking for directions, and not a full picture.

@char A04 attic_rain | The idea | Your mental picture is sparse
The researchers' conclusion is that we don't hold a rich, detailed, photographic copy of the scene in our minds. We hold a few key points, the gist, and we rely on being able to look again if we need more. The world itself serves as our external memory, a kind of storage that we only consult when we choose to.

@chapter A13 rooftop_dusk | Flicker
Change blindness has been studied in a laboratory too, using a clever trick called the flicker paradigm. In 1997, Ronald Rensink, Kevin O'Regan and James Clark showed people a photograph, then a blank grey screen, then a version of the photograph with one thing changed, and then a blank again, repeating the cycle over and over.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Rensink, O'Regan & Clark | Psychological Science, 1997 | To see or not to see: the need for attention to perceive changes in scenes
The changes were often large. An engine of a plane might vanish, or a railing could shift, or a building might move a few feet. And yet people often needed many cycles, sometimes dozens, to spot what was different. Once they saw it, it was impossible to unsee.

@char A12 living_room_day | Why the blank matters | It hides the signal
The grey screen is the key. Normally, when something changes in front of you, there's a flash of movement, and that flash grabs your attention. The blank interval wipes out that flash. With no signal to draw your eye, you have to find the change by searching, and searching takes time.

@list A03 living_room_day | When changes are easy to miss | During a blink ;; During an eye movement ;; During a cut in a film ;; When a flash or screen wipe covers the change
Each of these hides the movement signal. Your visual system is extremely sensitive to sudden changes, but only when it can see them happen. Take away the sudden part, and the change can sit right in front of you, unnoticed.

@char A01 cafe_sunset | In the movies | Continuity errors
Film editors rely on this. In many films, a character's drink changes level between shots, a scarf moves, or a car appears and disappears. Most viewers never notice, because the cut hides the change, and because attention is on the story. People who collect these errors usually have to watch the film several times.

@char A10 attic_rain | A famous case | The coat that changed colour
In 1980, the film researcher Julian Hochberg described cases in which an actor's clothing changed between shots, and audiences didn't mind. Years later, Daniel Levin and Daniel Simons showed viewers a short film in which, with each cut, details about a character changed, including the colour of a scarf, and again, most never saw it.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Levin & Simons | Psychonomic Bulletin & Review, 1997 | Failure to detect changes to attended objects in motion pictures
In their study, only a small fraction of viewers noticed that the main actor's scarf changed from one shot to the next. The change was to something that they were looking at directly, and that mattered to the scene. It's more evidence that we keep a gist of the story, and not a careful record of every detail.

@chapter A12 living_room_day | Change blindness blindness
Here's the twist, and it's one that connects back to the gorilla. Daniel Levin and colleagues asked people how likely they were to notice changes of this kind, and then tested them. In 2000, they found that people greatly overestimated how many changes they'd spot. They were blind to their own blindness.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Levin, Momen, Drivdahl & Simons | Visual Cognition, 2000 | Change blindness blindness: the metacognitive error of overestimating change-detection ability
The researchers called it change blindness blindness. People who watched a video of someone else failing to notice a change said that they'd have noticed it, easily. They were confident, they were wrong, and they were wrong for the same reason as before. You can't feel what you haven't noticed.

@char A19 cabin_fireplace | A familiar feeling | I would have seen that
You might recognise this when watching a heist film, or a sports replay, or a news clip. We feel certain that we'd have caught the detail, and the feeling is quite ordinary. It's the same reason why eyewitnesses are so confident about what they saw, and why they can be wrong.

@char A04 attic_rain | The link to memory | Witnesses and details
This is where the topic touches on the justice system. When a witness remembers a face, a car or a scene, they're relying on the same sparse, gist-based picture. They may recall the feeling of it perfectly, and get the details wrong. Research on eyewitnesses has shown again and again that confidence and accuracy do not always go together.

@list A12 living_room_day | What eyewitness research suggests | Confidence at the time of a first identification is more informative than later confidence ;; Memory can be shaped by questions ;; Stress and attention can narrow what is noticed ;; Procedures that avoid hints help
These are cautious findings from a long literature, and they've shaped how many police forces run line-ups. The lesson is not that witnesses are useless. It's that careful procedures protect both the witness and the person being identified.

@chapter A06 cafe_sunset | What the mind keeps
So if we don't keep a detailed picture, what do we keep? Researchers like Kevin O'Regan have argued that the world works as an outside memory. We don't need to store everything because it's there, and we can look again. Seeing, on this view, is something we do, an activity, and not a picture that we hold.

@char A08 cabin_fireplace | Seeing as an activity | Asking the world questions
Think of how you use a kitchen. You don't carry a perfect plan of every shelf in your head. You know roughly where things are, and you look when you need to. The scene is a source you consult, not an image you keep. And so the feeling of a rich, complete visual world may come from the fact that it's always available.

@stat A06 cafe_sunset | The grand illusion | A phrase from 2000 | Alva Noe and colleagues called the feeling of a full visual picture the grand illusion
This idea, sometimes called the grand illusion, has been much debated. Some researchers argue that it overstates the case, and that we do keep more than the studies suggest. A fair summary is that we hold a good deal of gist and some detail, and a smaller amount of precise information than we think.

@char A04 attic_rain | A caution | Don't overstate it
It's worth being careful with some of the more dramatic versions of this story. You are not blind to most of your surroundings. You can read this room, find your way across it, and pick out a friend in a crowd. The studies show that certain kinds of change, under certain conditions, can go unnoticed, which is a narrower finding.

@list A03 living_room_day | What the studies do show | Attention is selective ;; Detailed memory for a scene is limited ;; Changes hidden by a flash or a cut can be missed ;; People overrate their own noticing
Four points, each supported by a lot of work. That's a more useful summary than the claim that perception is entirely an illusion. It's an editor's cut, and a good one, with a few cuts that are invisible if you don't know where to look.

It's the old spot-the-difference game, and it's a good humble reminder. When you search carefully, you often find that the difference was there from the start. When you don't, it's invisible. And in daily life, we rarely search.

@char A15 cabin_fireplace | A gentle thought | Look again, kindly
If you're ever in a conversation where someone says they didn't notice something, you might remember the door. They may not have been careless. They may simply have been looking at something else. A little curiosity goes a long way, and so does a second look.

@char A08 cabin_fireplace | Looking back | What the door taught us
So what did the man with the door teach us? That we hold a sparse picture, not a photograph. That changes hidden by a flash or a cut can pass unseen. That we are certain we would notice, and that we are often wrong. And that the world does some of our remembering for us.
