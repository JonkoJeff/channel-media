@char A03 living_room_day | Fifth myth | Time slows in a crisis
And that brings us to a fifth popular belief, one that has been told in countless stories. In a moment of danger, time slows down. People who've had a near accident often say that everything seemed to happen in slow motion. The car skidding. The glass falling. The ball flying toward them. It's as if the brain had switched into a special mode, seeing the world frame by frame.

@char A10 attic_rain | A real experience | But what is happening?
Those experiences are real, in the sense that people truly remember them that way. But scientists have wondered what's actually happening. Does the brain really speed up its processing during danger, so that it takes in more of the world each second? Or is something else going on, perhaps something that only happens afterwards, when the moment is remembered?

@chapter A12 rooftop_dusk | Fear and the falling study
To find out, a team led by the neuroscientist David Eagleman designed a rather unusual experiment. In a study published in 2007, with Chess Stetson and Matthew Fiesta, they asked volunteers to fall. Not in any dangerous way. They used an amusement park ride where people drop backwards from a tall platform, falling freely for about three seconds before landing safely in a net.

#ai lf40_b07_01.jpg | A tall steel tower ride with a large safety net at its base in an empty amusement park at dawn, soft pastel sky, calm and quiet atmosphere, no people, no text, no logos

@photo lf40_b07_01.jpg | A tall platform and a net
Picture the scene at dawn, before anyone arrives. A tall steel tower. A wide safety net stretched out below. It's quiet now. But for the people in this study, it was a moment of real fear. The ride was designed to be safe, yet the body doesn't always believe that. Falling backwards into empty air is one of the most frightening sensations most people will ever feel.

@char A20 street_day | Three seconds of falling | About seventy miles per hour
During the fall, according to the press release about the study, people reached around seventy miles per hour. Three seconds of pure free fall. Exactly the kind of sudden, frightening moment that people describe as slow motion. So the researchers asked two clever questions. First, how long did the fall seem to last? And second, could the brain actually see more during it?

@stat A13 cafe_sunset | How long did your own fall seem? | 36% | longer than watching someone else fall
The first answer was striking. After landing, people estimated that their own fall had lasted about thirty six percent longer than the falls they'd watched other people take. Same tower, same net, same three seconds. But their own frightening fall seemed to stretch out in their memory, by roughly a third.

@char A03 living_room_day | A clever device | A watch with flickering numbers
Now for the second question. To test whether people could actually see more during the fall, the researchers strapped a small device to each person's wrist, a bit like a watch. It showed numbers that flickered too fast for a normal eye to read. If time really slowed down during the fall, people should be able to read those numbers, the way slow motion lets you see every frame.

@study A13 living_room_day | Chess Stetson, Matthew Fiesta and David Eagleman | Published in 2007 | Falling people could not read numbers flashed too fast for normal sight
But they couldn't. People could read the numbers when they were shown at normal speeds, but not when they flickered faster than normal. The brain didn't suddenly gain super vision. As the researchers put it, people are not like the hero of The Matrix, dodging bullets in slow motion. During the fall, they saw the world at its usual pace.

@char A12 street_day | So where does slow motion come from? | Memory again
So where does the feeling of slow motion come from? The researchers suggested that it comes from memory. In a frightening moment, the brain seems to lay down memories more richly and densely than usual. When we look back, there's more stored detail than normal for such a short stretch of time. And the mind reads that density as length, so the moment seems to have lasted longer.

@quote A19 cabin_fireplace | We do not live in slow motion. We remember in it. | The falling lesson
Here's the lesson in one sentence. We do not live in slow motion. We remember in it. That's a remarkable finding, and it fits beautifully with everything we've seen so far tonight. Once again, it seems, what makes time feel long in hindsight isn't the clock, but how much the memory holds. A dense memory makes a long moment.

@char A15 cafe_sunset | Verdict on the fifth myth | A trick of memory
So what's the verdict on our fifth popular belief? Time doesn't actually slow down in a crisis, at least not in the sense of seeing more. The world keeps its pace. But our memory of the moment can be so rich that afterwards it seems to have unfolded slowly. The slow motion is real as a memory, even if it wasn't real as an experience.

@char A20 cabin_fireplace | Back to Hanna | A frightening moment
Hanna knows this feeling. In one of her diaries, there's an entry about a day when she was a teenager, when her bicycle slipped on a wet road. She remembers the wheel sliding, the grey sky turning, the ground coming up to meet her, all in what felt like slow motion. It probably lasted a second. But decades later, she can still replay every part of it.

@list A13 cafe_sunset | Long in memory, short in memory | A frightening second: long ;; A holiday week: long ;; A childhood summer: long ;; A year of routine: short
So here, too, we see the same rule we've met at every scale tonight. A frightening second, rich in memory, feels long. A holiday week, full of new things, feels long. A childhood summer, packed with firsts, feels long. And a year of routine, with few new markers, feels short. Memory seems to be the common thread that ties them all together.

@char A03 rooftop_dusk | A different question now | Is there a clock inside us?
But memory isn't the only piece of the puzzle. When we're in the moment, timing a few seconds or minutes, the brain seems to use other tools too. For more than a century, scientists have wondered whether there's something like a clock inside us, ticking away. And one of the strangest early clues came from a fever, and a husband who was paying close attention.
