@chapter A12 living_room_day | When help isn't help
Not all help helps. Most of us can recall being offered assistance we didn't want, or help that made us feel small. Researchers have examined this, and found that the way help is offered can matter as much as whether it's offered at all. This chapter is about getting the manner right.

@char A04 attic_rain | The paradox | Help can sting
It may sound strange, but receiving help can lower a person's self-esteem. Accepting help can signal that we are less capable than the helper, and for people who value independence, that can hurt. Psychologists have studied this under the heading of threat to self-esteem.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Fisher, Nadler & Whitcher-Alagna | Psychological Bulletin, 1982 | Recipient reactions to aid
Jeffrey Fisher and his colleagues reviewed research on how people respond to being helped. They found that aid could be experienced as supportive, when it signals care, or as threatening, when it implies inferiority or creates a feeling of obligation. How help is offered, and who offers it, shaped which reaction followed.

@list A03 living_room_day | When help feels threatening | It implies the person cannot cope ;; It comes with a sense of debt ;; It is offered by someone who seems superior ;; It is unwanted or unrequested
These are common sense once they are said aloud, but they are easy to forget in the moment. We help in the way that feels natural to us, and don't always ask how it feels to the person receiving it.

@chapter A13 rooftop_dusk | Help at work
The effect is not limited to personal life. In the workplace, researchers asked employees about the help they received from colleagues and found that it had mixed results.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Deelstra, Peeters, Schaufeli, Stroebe, Zijlstra & van Doornen | Journal of Applied Psychology, 2003 | Receiving instrumental support at work: when help is not welcome
Researchers in the Netherlands studied employees who received practical help from colleagues. When the help was unsolicited, they found, it was associated with more distress. Help that the employee had asked for did not show the same pattern. The fit between the offer and the need was key.

@char A12 living_room_day | The lesson | Ask first
The practical message is a simple one. Before you step in, ask. Would you like a hand with that? What would be most useful? A question hands control back to the person, and turns an act done to someone into an act done with them.

@chapter A06 cafe_sunset | Invisible support
Another finding is that the best help is sometimes the kind that goes unnoticed. In a study of couples, researchers found that support was most effective when the recipient did not realise that it was being given.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Bolger, Zuckerman & Kessler | Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 2000 | Invisible support and adjustment to stress
Niall Bolger and his colleagues followed couples where one partner was preparing for a major professional exam, and kept daily diaries. On days when the partner gave support that the exam-taker reported noticing, the exam-taker felt more anxious and depressed. On days when support was given but not reported by the recipient, there were improvements in mood. Visible support carried a hidden cost.

@list A12 living_room_day | What invisible support looks like | Quietly taking over a chore ;; Making a meal without mentioning it ;; Giving someone space to study ;; Listening without making a show of it
The finding is not that we should hide our kindness. It is that support which doesn't draw attention to the other person's difficulties can feel lighter. A note on the counter, a task done early, and a quiet presence can help more than a dramatic offer.

@chapter A19 living_room_day | Dignity
Underneath all of this is the matter of dignity. People want to be helped in ways that respect their abilities and choices. Researchers who study helping between groups, for example, have found that help can reinforce inequalities if it signals that the receiver is dependent.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Nadler & Halabi | Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 2006 | Intergroup helping as status relations: effects of status stability, identification, and type of help on receptivity to high-status group's help
Arie Nadler and Samer Halabi found that when members of a lower-status group were offered help by a higher-status group, they tended to be more receptive when the help was empowering, such as tools or teaching, than when it was simply doing the task for them. How the aid was framed affected whether it was seen as supportive or as a reminder of unequal status.

@char A01 cafe_sunset | The principle | Help people do, not just do for them
This echoes an old saying about teaching someone to fish. The research suggests that help that builds a person's own capacity tends to be received more warmly than help that simply replaces it. Of course, in an emergency, we simply act. But in the long run, helping someone to help themselves is often the more respectful way.

@list A03 living_room_day | Words to keep from this chapter | Threat to self-esteem ;; Unsolicited help ;; Invisible support ;; Autonomy-oriented help
Four terms from this chapter. Threat to self-esteem is the sting of being seen as less capable. Unsolicited help is help that was not asked for. Invisible support is help that the recipient does not notice. And autonomy-oriented help is help that builds the receiver's own ability.

@list A12 living_room_day | Before you offer help | Ask what would be useful ;; Offer a choice ;; Keep your tone level, not superior ;; Accept a no gracefully
Here is a short checklist before you offer help. Ask what would be useful. Offer a choice. Keep your tone level, and not superior. And accept a no gracefully. Picture this scene: a neighbour sees an older man struggling with a heavy bag. Instead of grabbing it, she says, I'm going that way, would you like some company? He says yes, and she walks beside him, carrying nothing, until he hands over the bag himself at the corner. He kept his dignity and got his help. It cost her nothing but a question.

@list A12 living_room_day | Pause and reflect | When has help made me feel small? ;; When has help made me feel held? ;; What was the difference? ;; What will I ask next time I offer?
A pause to think about being on the receiving end. When has help made you feel small? Perhaps someone took over a task you could have done, or spoke to you in a tone that suggested you couldn't cope. And when has help made you feel held? Perhaps someone asked what you needed, sat beside you, or did something quietly that took the weight off. What was the difference between the two? Usually it isn't the size of the help but the respect in it. The research on unsolicited help, invisible support and empowering aid all points in the same direction. People want to be treated as capable, and as having a say. So think about what you will ask next time you offer. Would you like a hand? What would be most useful? A question is a small thing, and it turns help from something done to someone into something shared.

@photo lf27_b15_01.jpg | A hand held back
A hand held back, not reaching, waiting to be invited. It is a picture of the oldest advice in the research on helping: a question first, then the offer. Good help has manners. It asks, it listens, it respects, and sometimes it hides itself. These small courtesies are not decoration. The studies suggest they decide whether help is received as care or as a burden.

@char A15 cabin_fireplace | A gentle thought | Ask, then help
If you've ever been hurt by help that was meant kindly, you know why it matters. The best kindness listens first. It is a simple discipline, and it makes the difference between help that lands and help that lands wrong.

@char A08 cabin_fireplace | Looking back | What the research on aid taught us
So what did we learn? That help can threaten self-esteem when it implies inferiority. That unsolicited help at work was linked to more distress. That support that goes unnoticed can work best. And that asking, and building ability, respects dignity.
