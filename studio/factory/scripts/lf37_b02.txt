Myth number one. Personality is a type, a box you belong in. You're either this kind of person, or that kind. Thinking or feeling. Planner or improviser. Introvert or extravert. It's the idea behind Nora's four letters, and behind a great many quizzes. And it feels natural, because sorting things into kinds is one of the oldest habits the human mind has.

@char A13 attic_rain | An ancient idea | Four kinds of people
The idea is far older than any quiz. In ancient Greece, physicians linked to Hippocrates believed the body contained four fluids, or humours. Blood, phlegm, yellow bile and black bile. Health, they thought, depended on keeping these in balance. And over time, the idea grew that the balance of these fluids might shape not only the body, but a person's character too.

@study A19 living_room_day | Galen | On the Temperaments | written in the 2nd century
That idea was developed further by Galen, a Greek physician working in the Roman world. In his writing On the Temperaments, from the second century, he described how different mixtures could make a person's nature lean one way or another. It became the basis of what we now call the four temperaments, a way of describing people that lasted for well over a thousand years.

@list A12 cafe_sunset | The four temperaments | Sanguine, cheerful ;; Choleric, quick-tempered ;; Melancholic, thoughtful ;; Phlegmatic, calm
You might still recognise the names. Sanguine, linked to blood, cheerful and sociable. Choleric, linked to yellow bile, quick to anger and full of drive. Melancholic, linked to black bile, thoughtful and prone to sadness. And phlegmatic, linked to phlegm, calm and steady. Four kinds of people. Four boxes. And you were meant to fit, mostly, into one of them.

@char A05 autumn_overlook | Wrong about the body | Right about something
The biology, of course, turned out to be wrong. Our moods aren't set by yellow bile. But the old system was getting at something real. People really do differ in how cheerful they tend to be, how quickly they get upset, and how calm they stay under pressure. The ancient physicians noticed real differences. Where they went further was in turning those differences into a few fixed boxes.

@char A03 rooftop_dusk | A new kind of typing | Many centuries later
Jump forward many centuries, to Switzerland. The psychiatrist Carl Jung had spent years listening to patients, and thinking about why people seem to approach the world so differently. Some seemed to turn outward, toward people and events. Others seemed to turn inward, toward their own thoughts and feelings. He wanted a map of these differences, and like the ancient physicians, he drew it as types.

@study A15 cabin_fireplace | Carl Jung | Psychological Types, a book | published in 1921
In 1921, Jung published a book called Psychological Types. In it, he described two broad attitudes, introversion and extraversion. And he described different ways of taking in the world and making judgements about it, such as thinking and feeling, sensing and intuition. It was a rich, thoughtful book, full of clinical observation. But it was based on his own impressions, not on careful measurement.

@char A10 living_room_day | A mother and daughter | Two keen readers
Jung's book found two very keen readers in the United States. Katharine Briggs, and her daughter, Isabel Briggs Myers. Neither was a trained psychologist. But both were fascinated by why people are so different, and both believed that understanding those differences could help people get along, choose work that suited them, and be kinder to one another. It was a generous idea.

@char A18 attic_rain | Building a questionnaire | Year after year
So they set about turning Jung's ideas into something people could actually use. A questionnaire. Isabel spent many years writing and refining the questions, testing them on whoever she could. The result became the Myers-Briggs Type Indicator, often just called the MBTI. Pairs of opposites, combined into four-letter types. And it went on to become one of the best-known personality questionnaires in the world.

@char A13 bedroom_night | Nora's letters | Where they come from
Nora's quiz online wasn't the official questionnaire. Many free quizzes borrow the idea and the letters without being the real thing. But the logic is the same. You answer questions, and at the end you're placed on one side or the other of each pair. Thinking or feeling. Judging or perceiving. And you're handed a type, as if you'd been sorted into a room.

@quote A19 rooftop_dusk | Personality is a type, a box you belong in. | Myth
So here's the myth again, stated plainly. Personality is a type. There are a set number of boxes, and each person belongs in one. You might be on the border sometimes, but deep down, you're one kind or another. It's tidy. It's easy to remember. It gives you a word for yourself and for the people around you. Now, what the research actually says.

@char A12 living_room_day | What the research says | Measure, don't sort
What the research actually says. To test whether people really fall into types, you need to look at how their answers spread out. If people truly come in two kinds, you'd expect two clear clumps, with very few people in between. Like two hills with a valley. But if personality varies smoothly, you'd expect one big hill, with most people near the middle.

@study A15 cafe_sunset | Robert McCrae and Paul Costa | Journal of Personality | published in 1989
In 1989, two psychologists, Robert McCrae and Paul Costa, looked closely at the Myers-Briggs. They compared it with a questionnaire built on the Big Five, the model most personality researchers use today. We'll hear exactly where it came from later tonight. Their question was simple, and quite fair. How do letters like Nora's line up with the measures scientists rely on?

@char A05 autumn_overlook | Familiar dials | Under new names
What they found was interesting. The MBTI scales weren't measuring something mysterious or new. Four of them lined up quite well with four of the Big Five dimensions. The pull between introversion and extraversion matched extraversion. The other scales matched openness, agreeableness and conscientiousness. In other words, the questionnaire was picking up real differences. It was just describing them in a different way.

@char A20 cabin_fireplace | One hill | Not two
But there was a catch, and it's an important one. When you look at how people actually score, they don't fall into two separate camps. Most people land somewhere near the middle, with fewer and fewer people towards either end. One hill, not two. The MBTI draws a line through the top of that hill and calls one side thinking and the other side feeling.

@char A03 living_room_day | A line in the middle | Where most people stand
Think about what that means for someone standing right in the middle. A person just a little to one side of the line gets one letter. A person just a little to the other side gets the opposite letter. They're almost exactly the same, but the labels make them sound like opposites. And someone far out at one end gets the very same letter as someone standing right beside the line.

@char A12 rooftop_dusk | Dials, not boxes | A gentler picture
So most researchers have come to see personality differently. Not as boxes, but as dials. Each trait is a dial that can be set anywhere along its range. A little more this way, a little less that way. Most of us are set somewhere around the middle on most dials, with a few that turn noticeably higher or lower. And that's perfectly normal.

@photo lf37_b02_01.jpg | Dials, not boxes
Picture an old radio with a row of round dials. None of them is simply on or off. Each can be turned a little, or a lot. Together, they make one particular sound. Personality is a bit like that. It isn't one switch with two positions. It's several dials, each set somewhere along its range, and the particular mix is what makes you sound like you.

@char A13 bedroom_night | Back to Nora | A point, not a box
So what does this mean for Nora's four letters? They aren't wrong, exactly. They're pointing at real dials. But they're turning each dial into a switch. Maybe Nora's extraversion is just a touch below the middle. Maybe her thinking and feeling scores are nearly even. Her type isn't a box she lives in. It's a rough point on several dials, rounded off.

@char A10 cabin_fireplace | What we keep | And what we let go
So here's the first gentle lesson. Myth number one says personality is a type, a box you belong in. The research says the differences are real, but they're smooth. Most of us sit near the middle, and the boxes are lines drawn through a crowd. And that leaves an obvious next question. If Nora is near a line, what happens when she takes the quiz again?
