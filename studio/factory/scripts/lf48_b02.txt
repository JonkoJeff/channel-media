@chapter N02 autumn_overlook | The first experiment
In 1980, a pharmacologist at Stanford named Avram Goldstein tried to answer a question that sounds almost too simple. If music gives people a rush of pleasure, is that rush connected to the body's own opioid chemistry?

@char N10 cabin_fireplace | Who was Goldstein? | A pharmacologist by training
Avram Goldstein wasn't a music researcher. He was a pharmacologist, a scientist who studies how drugs act on the body, and he'd spent years on the brain's opioid system. That's the system that morphine and similar medicines act on, and that the body uses, in its own way, for pain relief and for pleasure.

@char N18 street_day | The idea | Is the shiver an opioid effect?
His idea was straightforward. The body makes its own opioid-like molecules, called endorphins. If the pleasure of a thrilling piece of music depended on those molecules, then blocking the receptors they act on should dull the experience. And there happened to be a medicine that does just that, called naloxone, which is used in emergencies to reverse opioid overdose.

@study N07 cafe_sunset | Avram Goldstein | Physiological Psychology, 1980 | Ten people listened to favorite pieces after naloxone or a dummy injection; for three of them, the thrills were reduced
Here's what he did. He recruited a small group of volunteers who reliably got thrills from music, and had them listen to their favorite pieces on two different occasions. On one, they were given naloxone. On the other, an inactive dummy injection. Of the ten people in the study, three reported that their thrills were reduced after naloxone.

@stat N19 living_room_day | Goldstein, 1980 | 3 of 10 | listeners whose thrills dimmed on naloxone
Three out of ten. It isn't a large number, and it comes from a small study. But it was striking, because naloxone shouldn't change how a piece of music feels if the feeling were purely a matter of hearing and thinking.

@char N20 attic_rain | An honest caveat | Small, and not perfect
Let's be careful with this one. Ten people is not many. The effect wasn't seen in most of them. And Goldstein himself was cautious. The result was a hint that the body's opioid chemistry might play a role in the pleasure of music, not a proof that it did. In science, a hint like that is the kind of thing that makes other people want to try again.

@char N23 autumn_overlook | Did anyone try again? | Decades later
Other researchers did, and they did it years later, using more careful methods. In 2017, a team led by Daniel Levitin at McGill University gave a small group of participants naltrexone, a related drug that blocks opioid receptors for longer, and had them listen to music on a separate occasion without it. The volunteers reported less pleasure from music after naltrexone. They could still hear. They could still recognize the piece. They just enjoyed it less.

@char N02 living_room_day | What that suggests | The pleasure was dimmed
Notice what that does and doesn't mean. It doesn't mean music is a drug. It suggests that part of the way the brain makes music feel good runs through the same chemical pathways as other pleasures, and that when you dim those pathways, the pleasure dims too, even when everything else about the experience is the same.

@photo lf48_b02_01.jpg | A quiet laboratory
It's a good example of how many experiments in this field work. You take a feeling that everyone agrees exists but nobody can see, and you find a way to change one small piece of the body's chemistry, and then you ask whether the feeling changes. Slowly, over many small studies, a picture builds up.

@char N29 rooftop_dusk | Another early idea | What triggers the thrill?
Around the same time, others were asking a different question. Rather than what happens inside the body, they asked what happens inside the music. Which parts of a piece of music tend to give people chills, and is there anything in common?

@study N07 street_day | John Sloboda | Psychology of Music, 1991 | Asked 83 music lovers about their strongest moments; shivers went with new harmonies and sudden changes
In 1991, a British psychologist, John Sloboda, asked eighty-three people about their strongest musical reactions, and then went back to the scores to find the exact bars they were talking about. He found that shivers and chills tended to come with new or unexpected harmonies, and with sudden changes in loudness or texture.

@char N19 cafe_sunset | Tears were different | Sloboda's second finding
He also found that tears followed a different pattern. People tended to cry at passages with melodic lines that repeated a step higher or lower, called sequences, and at moments where a note leans on a neighboring note before settling, called an appoggiatura. Chills seemed to like surprise. Tears seemed to like a kind of yearning that resolves.

@char N10 bedroom_night | Four seconds at a time | Why this is hard
If that all sounds a little technical, it's worth remembering how hard it is to do this work. The moments are short. The responses are private. People can't switch them on at will. Every claim has to be made by catching a fleeting experience, and then matching it to a few seconds of sound.

@char N18 living_room_day | More careful measurements | Skin and heart
In the 2000s, German researchers led by Reinhard Kopiez and Eckart Altenmüller at the Hannover University of Music did exactly that. They asked listeners to press a button whenever they felt chills, while sensors tracked skin conductance and heart rate. They then looked at the music, moment by moment, for what tended to come just before the button presses.

@char N20 cafe_sunset | What they found | Entries and swells
Their results echoed Sloboda's. Chills often showed up when a new voice or instrument entered, when the music got suddenly louder, or when the harmony shifted in a way the listener hadn't seen coming. Not every chill fit that pattern, but many did, and it gave researchers something to look for.

@char N23 autumn_overlook | A pattern emerges | Surprise, in a safe place
Put these early findings together, and a picture starts to take shape. Chills seem to come when the music does something that departs from what we expected, in a setting where nothing bad is going to happen. A surprise that arrives in safety. And in the next chapter, we'll see what happens in the brain when it does.
