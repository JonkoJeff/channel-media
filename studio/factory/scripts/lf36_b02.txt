@chapter A13 cafe_sunset | Why we feel wronged
When Averill asked people what had made them angry, one answer came up again and again. It wasn't that something had simply gone wrong. It was that someone had done something wrong. Anger, in his diaries, was almost always about another person, and about a sense that they had crossed a line.

@stat A12 living_room_day | Averill's diaries | 85% | said the other person had done them wrong
In about eighty-five percent of the episodes, people said the other person had done something wrong to them. Something unfair, or careless, or avoidable. That's a very particular kind of feeling. It isn't just pain. It's pain with a story attached. Somebody did this, and they shouldn't have.

@char A10 cabin_fireplace | Pain with a story | That's the key
That little story is going to matter a lot tonight. Because two people can have exactly the same experience, and only one of them gets angry. The difference is often the story. Did they mean it? Could they have done better? Were they being careless with me? Keep that thought somewhere close. We'll pick it up again later.

@char A03 autumn_overlook | And then? | What people did next
Now, here's the part of Averill's work that surprises most people. When we imagine anger, we often imagine something loud. Slammed doors, raised voices, a fist on the table. But when Averill looked at what people actually did when they were angry, the picture was much quieter, and much more varied.

@bars A12 cafe_sunset | What people did when angry | % of episodes | Calming activities:60 ;; Talked to someone else:59 ;; Verbally aggressive:49 ;; Talked to the offender:39 ;; Extra friendly:19 ;; Physically aggressive:10
Look at these numbers for a moment. In about sixty percent of the episodes, people did something to calm themselves down. In nearly as many, they talked to someone else about it. About half the time, there was some verbal aggression, a sharp word or a raised voice. And physical aggression was much rarer, around one episode in ten.

@char A15 living_room_day | Extra friendly | Wait, really?
And then there's that strange little bar near the bottom. In almost one episode in five, people said they had actually been extra friendly toward the person they were angry with. That sounds odd at first. But you've probably done it too. Being a little too polite. A little too cheerful. Smoothing things over, while the feeling is still there underneath.

@char A19 bedroom_night | Many ways out | Anger isn't one thing
So anger doesn't come with one fixed script. The same feeling can lead to a calm walk, a long phone call with a friend, a sharp message, or an unusually polite reply. Which means the feeling and the action are two different things. And the space between them is exactly where Sam is sitting right now, on that bus.

@chapter A18 rooftop_dusk | Why the people we love
Remember, in more than half of Averill's episodes, the target of the anger was someone close. A partner, a parent, a friend, a relative. So why is that? Why do the people we care about most seem to get the sharpest end of our anger?

@char A10 cabin_fireplace | Simple maths first | We see them the most
Part of the answer is very simple. We spend more time with them. We share kitchens and calendars and plans. More time together means more chances for small things to go wrong, a forgotten errand, a missed call, a misunderstood message. If you see someone every day, there are simply more moments in which they can annoy you.

@char A13 living_room_day | But there's more | Expectations
But it isn't only about time. It's also about expectation. We expect more from the people close to us. We expect them to remember, to understand, to be on our side. A stranger forgetting something barely registers. A brother forgetting something, or a brother reminding you that you forgot, can feel like it's saying something about the whole relationship.

@char A05 autumn_overlook | And safety | Anger needs somewhere to go
And there's one more, gentler reason. With the people closest to us, we often feel safe enough to let anger show. We hold it in at work, with strangers, in public. And then it comes out at home, with the people we trust not to leave. That isn't fair to them, of course. But it does tell us something about how much they mean.

@list A12 cafe_sunset | Why anger finds loved ones | We spend more time together ;; We expect more from them ;; Their words carry more weight ;; We feel safe enough to show it
So here's the picture. We get angry with the people we love partly because we spend more time together, partly because we expect more from them, partly because their words simply carry more weight, and partly because we feel safe enough to let it out. In a strange way, a lot of our anger is a sign of how much these people matter.

@char A03 rooftop_dusk | Back to Sam | Why his brother
So when Sam's brother writes you forgot again, it isn't a random message from a random person. It comes from someone who knows him, who has expectations of him, whose opinion matters. That's part of why it stings. And maybe, if we're honest, part of why Sam's reply came out so sharp so fast.

@quote A13 cabin_fireplace | Anger is often a sign that something matters. | A thought to hold on to
Here's a thought to hold on to as we go deeper tonight. Anger is often a sign that something matters. A relationship, a sense of fairness, a picture of who we want to be. That doesn't make every angry action wise. But it does mean the feeling itself is usually pointing at something real.

@char A15 living_room_day | A flicker, not a flaw | Ordinary and human
And that's the first gentle lesson of the evening. Anger is ordinary. It visits most of us often. It's usually about people we care about, and about a sense that something wasn't fair. And what we do with it varies enormously, from a sharp word to a quiet walk to a surprisingly friendly smile.

@char A19 cabin_fireplace | The picture in our heads | And the real one
It's worth pausing on how different this is from the way anger usually appears in films and stories. There, anger is often a hero's fury or a villain's rage. Big, loud, and dangerous. Real anger, in real diaries, is mostly smaller than that. A tight feeling after a phone call. A sharp sentence at dinner. A message typed too quickly on a bus.

@char A10 attic_rain | Small doesn't mean harmless | But it can be worked with
Small doesn't mean harmless, of course. A sharp sentence can sting for days. A message can sit between two people for weeks. But small does mean something hopeful. If most anger lives in these little everyday moments, then the little everyday choices we make about it can matter a great deal.

@list A13 living_room_day | What the diaries tell us | Anger is common ;; It's usually about someone close ;; It comes with a story of being wronged ;; What we do next can vary
So let's gather what the diaries have told us so far. Anger is common, a regular visitor in most lives. It's usually about someone close. It almost always comes with a story about being wronged. And what we do next can vary enormously. Four simple ideas. And each one will come back, in some form, before the evening is over.
