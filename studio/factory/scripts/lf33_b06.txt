@chapter A20 cafe_sunset | The shape of the question
Imagine you're asked to choose between two plans. The facts in front of you are fixed. The numbers are fixed. Only the words change, the way the plans are described. Should that make any difference to your choice? A perfectly logical mind would say no. Same facts, same decision. Human minds, it turns out, say something rather different.

@study A01 living_room_day | Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman | Science, 1981 | An outbreak expected to kill 600 people, two programmes, described as lives saved or lives lost
In 1981, Tversky and Kahneman published a paper in Science called The Framing of Decisions and the Psychology of Choice. In it, they asked students to imagine that their country was preparing for an outbreak of an unusual disease, expected to kill six hundred people. Two programmes had been proposed to fight it.

@char A13 cabin_fireplace | The first version | Lives saved
Some students read this version. If programme A is adopted, two hundred people will be saved. If programme B is adopted, there's a one third chance that all six hundred will be saved, and a two thirds chance that nobody will be saved. Which would you choose?

@stat A12 rooftop_dusk | Described as lives saved | 72% | chose the sure programme that saves 200
Most students, seventy two percent, chose programme A. The sure thing. Two hundred people definitely saved feels better than a gamble that might save nobody at all. That seems sensible. But then came the second version.

@char A10 attic_rain | The second version | Lives lost
Other students read this. If programme C is adopted, four hundred people will die. If programme D is adopted, there's a one third chance that nobody will die, and a two thirds chance that all six hundred will die. Now, take a moment. Programme C is exactly the same as programme A. Two hundred saved means four hundred lost.

@stat A03 cafe_sunset | Described as lives lost | 78% | chose the gamble instead
And yet, in this version, seventy eight percent chose the gamble, programme D. The same choice, the same outcomes, the same numbers. Only the words had changed. Saved, or die. And that was enough to flip most people's preference from the sure thing to the gamble.

@char A15 autumn_overlook | Gains and losses | Two different moods
Tversky and Kahneman explained it like this. When options are described as gains, people tend to play it safe. They'd rather lock in a sure good outcome. When the same options are described as losses, people tend to take risks. They'd rather gamble than accept a sure loss. The frame changes which mood we bring to the choice.

@chapter A05 cabin_fireplace | Losses loom larger
This connects to one of their most influential ideas. Losses tend to feel bigger than gains of the same size. Losing something you have hurts more than gaining the same thing feels good. Kahneman and Tversky described it in 1979, with the memorable phrase, losses loom larger than gains.

@char A19 bedroom_night | Everyday losses | A lost glove
You can feel this in small ways. Finding a lovely scarf on a park bench might make you smile for a few minutes. Losing your favourite scarf on the bus can bother you for days. The two events are, in a way, the same size. But they don't feel the same size at all.

@chapter A18 living_room_day | Doctors too
You might hope that experts would be immune to framing, especially experts dealing with life and death. In 1982, a team including the doctor Barbara McNeil and Amos Tversky tested exactly that, with patients, students, and physicians.

@study A12 cafe_sunset | Barbara McNeil, Stephen Pauker, Harold Sox and Amos Tversky | New England Journal of Medicine, 1982 | Surgery versus radiation, described as chances of surviving or chances of dying
They described two treatments for lung cancer, surgery and radiation therapy. For some people, the outcomes were described in terms of survival. Ninety percent survive the surgery itself. For others, the very same figures were described in terms of death. Ten percent die during the surgery. Same facts, different words.

@bars A20 rooftop_dusk | Chose radiation instead of surgery | percent | Survival framing:18 ;; Mortality framing:44
When the figures were framed as survival, about eighteen percent chose radiation over surgery. When framed as mortality, the number rose to about forty four percent. The effect was there for the physicians too. Ten percent die simply sounds worse than ninety percent survive, even to people who know they mean the same thing.

@chapter A16 autumn_overlook | Even on the plate
Framing doesn't only work on big, dramatic decisions. It reaches into the most ordinary things, like how food tastes. In 1988, Irwin Levin and Gary Gaeth ran a small, delightful study with minced beef.

@study A03 living_room_day | Irwin Levin and Gary Gaeth | University of Iowa, 1988 | The same beef labelled seventy five percent lean or twenty five percent fat
They served people the same beef, cooked the same way. Some were told it was seventy five percent lean. Others were told it was twenty five percent fat. People who heard lean rated the beef as better tasting and less greasy. The effect was strongest when people only read the label, and smaller once they actually tasted it, but it didn't disappear.

Picture two glasses on a windowsill, each filled to exactly the same level. One you might call half full. The other, half empty. The water doesn't care what you call it. But the words you choose quietly change how it feels to look at.

@chapter A13 cabin_fireplace | Did it hold up
So, has framing survived the test of time? In large replication projects, including the Many Labs project we met earlier, the gain and loss framing effect has generally been found again, though often a little smaller than in the original studies. It's a real effect, and a fairly sturdy one.

@char A10 cafe_sunset | Not a trick | A clue
And it's worth seeing framing not only as a flaw, but as a clue. When the words change our minds, it's often because they change what we pay attention to. Survive draws the eye to the people who live. Die draws the eye to the people who don't. Both are true. Each frame shows us one side of the same coin.

@char A15 rooftop_dusk | Mara's frames | Leaving or arriving
Now think of Mara's two columns. At the top of one, she wrote stay. At the top of the other, go. But she could have written them differently. Giving up her life here, or gaining a life near her family. Losing her friends nearby, or keeping a city she's outgrown.

@char A19 cabin_fireplace | Losses loom | What she would leave
Because losses loom larger, the things she would leave behind probably feel heavier tonight than the things she would gain. Her café, her street, her friends down the road. Those are real, and they matter. But part of their weight may come from the simple fact that they're hers already.

@list A01 autumn_overlook | Turning the frame | Say it as a gain ;; Say it as a loss ;; Notice what changes ;; Look at both
A simple habit can help with framing. Describe your choice once as a gain. Then describe it again as a loss. Notice whether your feeling changes. If it does, the frame was doing some of the work. Looking at both frames, side by side, gives you a fuller picture than either one alone.

@char A02 living_room_day | Mara tries it | Two sentences
So let's imagine Mara trying it. If I go, I gain my sister, the sea, and her children growing up nearby. If I stay, I lose all of that. And then the other way round. If I go, I lose my friends and my street. If I stay, I keep them. Same facts. But saying both out loud helps her see the whole shape of her choice.
