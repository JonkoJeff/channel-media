CHAPTER: A Roman Historian's Sentence
One of the earliest non-Christian writers to mention the death of Jesus was a Roman senator who disliked Christians. Around the year 116, Tacitus wrote that Christus had suffered the extreme penalty under Pontius Pilate. What do our earliest sources say about that execution? || Tacitus, Annals 15.44 || text-card
Picture a hill outside the walls of Jerusalem, on a spring morning around the year thirty or thirty-three. A crowd is gathered, Roman soldiers stand guard, and three men are being put to death. It is a scene that the Gospels describe with remarkable restraint. || Jerusalem walls from the north, 19th century engraving || loc
In this video we follow the final hours of Jesus as the Gospels tell them: the arrest in the garden, the hearings, the sentence under Pilate, the road to the place called Golgotha, and the burial. We will ask what historians can say, and where the sources part ways. || The Crucifixion, Mantegna, Louvre || wikimedia
We will keep four layers apart. What the Gospels report. What other ancient writers add. What archaeology can show. And what centuries of Christian, Jewish and Muslim tradition have made of it. Each layer has its own kind of evidence, and its own limits. || Gospel manuscript, Codex Sinaiticus || wikimedia
This is a story of great weight for millions of people. We tell it with respect, and without dwelling on suffering for its own sake. Our aim is to understand the texts, their world, and the long history of how they have been read. || Church of the Holy Sepulchre, Jerusalem || wikimedia
Tacitus is a useful place to begin because he is not a believer. In his Annals, describing the great fire of Rome in the year sixty-four, he explains who the Christians were, and says that their founder, Christus, had been executed by the procurator Pontius Pilatus under the emperor Tiberius. || Tacitus, Annals, Medicean manuscript || wikimedia
Notice what that sentence gives us. It names the emperor, the Roman official, and the penalty. It does not mention Jerusalem or the Jewish festival, and it comes from a writer with no sympathy for the movement. Its value is that it matches the Christian accounts at the core. || Tiberius, bust, Ny Carlsberg Glyptotek || wikimedia
A second Roman-era writer is the Jewish historian Josephus, who wrote in Rome in the nineties. In his Jewish Antiquities he refers to Jesus and says that Pilate condemned him to the cross on the accusation of the leading men among the Jews. || Josephus, Jewish Antiquities 18.63 || text-card
That passage, known as the Testimonium Flavianum, has a complicated history. Many scholars think that it was partly reworked by Christian copyists, because some of its wording sounds like a believer. Most conclude that a core statement about the execution probably goes back to Josephus himself. || Josephus manuscript, Ambrosian Library, Milan || wikimedia
Josephus also mentions, elsewhere in the same work, the death of James, whom he calls the brother of Jesus who was called Christ. That brief note is widely regarded as authentic, and it shows that Jesus was known to a Jewish historian in Rome as a real person. || Josephus, Jewish Antiquities 20.200 || text-card
Within the Christian writings, the oldest witness is Paul. In a letter written in the early fifties, he speaks of preaching Christ crucified, a message that was a stumbling block to Jews and foolishness to Gentiles. He does not describe the scene, but he treats the fact as foundational. || 1 Corinthians 1:22–24 || text-card
So, from several directions, the execution is among the best-attested facts about Jesus. Historians with very different outlooks, believing and sceptical, agree on it. What they discuss is how the Gospels tell the story, and what lies behind the differences. || Roman road near Jerusalem, Ein Kerem || wikimedia
We begin on the night we left in the last story: the doors of the upper room closed, the singing in the street, and a path down into a valley of olive trees. || Kidron Valley at night, Jerusalem || wikimedia
CHAPTER: Torches in the Garden
When Jesus and his disciples reach the olive grove, Mark says that Jesus asks them to stay while he prays. Three times he returns and finds them asleep. Then, while he is still speaking, Judas arrives, one of the Twelve, with a crowd carrying swords and clubs. || Mark 14:41–43 || text-card
The crowd has been sent by the chief priests, the scribes and the elders. They have come at night, outside the city's bustle, to take him quietly. Judas has told them to seize the man he kisses. The signal is a greeting, and the greeting is the betrayal. || Mark 14:44–45 || text-card
Judas comes forward, says Rabbi, and kisses him. In Matthew, Jesus replies, friend, do what you came for. In Luke, he asks, are you handing over the Son of Man with a kiss? The words differ slightly, but the pain of the moment is shared. || The Kiss of Judas, Giotto, Scrovegni Chapel || wikimedia
John tells the scene in a different way. In his account, Jesus himself steps forward and asks whom they seek. They answer, Jesus of Nazareth, and he says, I am he. At that, John writes, they drew back and fell to the ground. || John 18:3–6 || text-card
John also gives a larger force than the other Gospels. He mentions a cohort of soldiers and officers from the chief priests and Pharisees, carrying lanterns, torches and weapons. A cohort could mean hundreds, so many scholars read the word loosely, as a sizeable detachment. || John 18:3 || text-card
Then comes a moment of violence. One of those with Jesus draws a sword and strikes the high priest's slave, cutting off his ear. John names him as Simon Peter and the slave as Malchus. In Luke, Jesus says, no more of this, and heals the ear. || John 18:10–11; Luke 22:50–51 || text-card
Jesus then asks why they have come with swords and clubs as if against a bandit, when he sat teaching in the Temple every day and they did not arrest him. In Mark, the scene closes with a plain sentence: and they all deserted him and fled. || Mark 14:48–50 || text-card
Only Mark adds a strange detail. A young man, following Jesus wearing nothing but a linen cloth, is seized, and slips out of the cloth and runs away naked. The scene has no explanation in the text. Some readers wonder whether it preserves a personal memory. || Mark 14:51–52 || text-card
That is the kind of detail historians value, because it is so odd. It doesn't help the story along, and later Gospels leave it out. It might be a trace of someone who was there. It might be a symbol. We cannot say, and the text does not help. || Garden of Gethsemane, olive grove || wikimedia
The arrest happens at night and in secret. Jesus himself comments on that, pointing out that they could have taken him by day. The authorities were worried about the crowds in the festival city, and acted when he was away from them. || Temple Mount, Jerusalem, view from the Mount of Olives || wikimedia
Who made the arrest? The Gospels point to the Temple authorities, with Judas as a guide, though John adds Roman soldiers. Historians generally think that a quiet arrest by the high priest's servants, with Roman involvement coming later, is the likeliest outline. || Raymond E. Brown, The Death of the Messiah || text-card
Here is where the story moves from the garden to the court. Jesus is bound and led back across the valley into the city, toward the house of the high priest. Behind him, a disciple follows at a distance. || Kidron Valley bridge, Jerusalem || wikimedia
CHAPTER: Questions at the High Priest's House
In Mark and Matthew, Jesus is taken to the high priest, Caiaphas, where the chief priests, elders and scribes have assembled. In John, he is first taken to Annas, Caiaphas's father-in-law, a former high priest, who questions him about his disciples and his teaching. || John 18:12–14, 19–24 || text-card
Caiaphas was high priest from about the year eighteen to thirty-six, which is a long tenure for the Roman period. Archaeology has found an ossuary inscribed with the name Joseph son of Caiaphas, which many scholars take to belong to this family. || Caiaphas ossuary, Israel Museum || wikimedia
His long tenure suggests that he worked well with the Roman governor. The high priest in this era was a political figure as well as a religious one, responsible for keeping order in Jerusalem, especially at festivals, when crowds could easily become unrest. || Roman governor's residence, Caesarea Maritima || wikimedia
In Mark, witnesses are called, but their accounts do not agree. Then some accuse Jesus of saying that he would destroy the Temple made with hands and build another not made with hands. Even about this, Mark says, their testimony did not match. || Mark 14:55–59 || text-card
The high priest then asks directly: are you the Messiah, the Son of the Blessed? In Mark, Jesus answers, I am, and adds that they will see the Son of Man seated at the right hand of Power and coming with the clouds of heaven. || Mark 14:61–62 || text-card
The high priest tears his clothes and says they need no more witnesses. They have heard the blasphemy. All of them condemn him as deserving death. Some begin to spit on him and strike him. The night's hearing ends with mockery. || Mark 14:63–65 || text-card
Matthew follows Mark closely, with Jesus saying, you have said so, before the same prophecy about the Son of Man. Luke arranges the hearing differently, with a morning session. John has no formal trial before the Jewish council at all, only the interrogation before Annas. || Luke 22:66–71 || text-card
Historians have long debated what really happened. Later Jewish law, in the Mishnah, sets out procedures for capital cases, and the Gospel hearing does not match them. But the Mishnah was written down around the year two hundred, so we cannot assume that its rules applied in the first century. || Mishnah, Sanhedrin tractate || wikimedia
Most scholars therefore think that what we have is not a formal trial but a hearing, perhaps a preliminary inquiry to decide what charge to bring before Pilate. The Gospel accounts have been shaped by decades of retelling, and by the tensions between early Christians and other Jewish groups. || Raymond E. Brown, The Death of the Messiah, 1994 || text-card
It is important to say clearly what the evidence does not support. The Gospels name particular leaders. They do not charge the Jewish people as a whole. And the Roman sentence of crucifixion was carried out by Roman soldiers, on the order of a Roman governor. || Roman soldier relief, Louvre || wikimedia
The Catholic Church's declaration Nostra Aetate, issued in 1965, states that what happened in the Passion cannot be charged against all Jews then living without distinction, nor against Jews today. It was a landmark in the long history of addressing the harm done by that charge. || Nostra Aetate, Second Vatican Council, 1965 || text-card
Many Christian churches have since said the same. The charge of collective guilt has no basis in the texts, and it caused centuries of suffering. An honest telling of this story must name that history, and keep it from being repeated. || Interfaith dialogue, Jewish and Christian scholars, 20th century || wikimedia
While the questioning goes on inside, the Gospels turn briefly to a courtyard, where a fire is burning and a disciple is standing in the cold. || Courtyard fire, Church of St Peter in Gallicantu, Jerusalem || wikimedia
CHAPTER: The Fire in the Courtyard
Peter has followed Jesus at a distance, into the courtyard of the high priest's house, and sits with the guards, warming himself by the fire. In Mark's account, a servant girl looks closely at him and says, you also were with Jesus of Nazareth. || Mark 14:54, 66–67 || text-card
Peter denies it. He says he neither knows nor understands what she means, and goes out into the forecourt. Then a rooster crows. It is the first sound of a morning that Peter will remember for the rest of his life. || Mark 14:68 || text-card
The servant sees him again and tells the bystanders, this man is one of them. Again he denies it. A little later, the bystanders say, surely you are one of them, for you are a Galilean. His accent has betrayed him. || Mark 14:69–70 || text-card
The third time, Mark says that Peter began to curse and swear, I do not know this man you are talking about. At that moment, the rooster crows a second time, and Peter remembers what Jesus had said to him, and he breaks down and weeps. || Mark 14:71–72 || text-card
Luke adds one more detail. After the third denial, the Lord turns and looks at Peter. It is a small, silent moment, and many readers have found it among the most moving in all four Gospels. Peter goes out and weeps bitterly. || Luke 22:61–62 || text-card
All four Gospels include the denial. As we noticed earlier, this is a story that does not flatter the man who later became a leader of the church. Historians often take such embarrassing details as a sign that the tradition goes back to real memory. || The Denial of Saint Peter, Rembrandt, Rijksmuseum || rijksmuseum
Today, a church called Saint Peter in Gallicantu, a name meaning, at the crowing of the cock, stands on the slope of Mount Zion. Excavations beneath it have uncovered ancient steps and a rock-cut pit. Tradition links the site to the house of Caiaphas. || Church of St Peter in Gallicantu, Jerusalem || wikimedia
Scholars are cautious about the identification. The location is first attested in later centuries, and the exact site of the high priest's house remains uncertain. But a Roman-period stairway nearby may well date from the time of Jesus, and pilgrims have walked it for generations. || Ancient stairway, Mount Zion, Jerusalem || wikimedia
Notice how the story slows down here. Mark places the denial beside the hearing, as if the two were happening in parallel: inside, a man who will not deny who he is, and outside, a man who cannot say he knows him. The contrast is deliberate. || Rooster, Byzantine mosaic || wikimedia
It's a story about failure that is also a story about return. Later in the Gospel of John, after the resurrection, the risen Jesus asks Peter three times whether he loves him, once for each denial. But that belongs to another story. || John 21:15–17 || text-card
For now, the night is ending. The first light touches the hills east of the city. The chief priests and the whole council reach a decision, and Jesus, bound, is led through the streets to the residence of the Roman governor. || Jerusalem at dawn, 19th century photograph || loc
