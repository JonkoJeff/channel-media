Let's gather the big findings. In the large Finnish survey, more than nine in ten people had an earworm at least once a week. In diaries, a typical episode lasted around twenty-seven minutes. Only about a third were described as unpleasant. And most were gone by the following day. Earworms are common, short and usually harmless.

We've also seen what makes a song stick. In Jakubowski's study of about three thousand people, sticky tunes tended to be faster, with a common rising and falling shape and a few unusual intervals. Hearing a song more times makes it more likely to loop, as the 2023 chorus experiment showed. And earworms find room when our minds wander or when a task is too hard to hold our attention.

And we've glimpsed what's happening inside. The brain's hearing areas can stay active in silence, filling in familiar music from memory. Imagined songs play at close to their real tempo. The inner voice seems to be involved, which may be why chewing gum can help. And during sleep, the brain may even replay recently heard music.

Here's a gentle guide, built from tonight's research. First, notice the trigger, because simply understanding where a tune came from can make it feel less strange. Second, don't fight it head on, because suppression often makes it worse. Third, find something that absorbs you at the right level. Fourth, if you like, try chewing gum or a slow, calm song. And fifth, give catchy music a rest before bed.
