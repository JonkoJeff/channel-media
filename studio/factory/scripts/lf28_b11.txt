@chapter A19 autumn_overlook | Dreams and feelings
So far we have looked at how a dream is built, and how it can seem so solid while it lasts. Now let's turn to something quieter, which is what a night of dreaming might do for the way you feel the next day. Some of the most interesting research in sleep science asks whether dreams are only a show, or whether they are part of a kind of overnight housekeeping for your emotions.

@char A12 cabin_fireplace | A familiar morning | Last night's feeling, this morning's calm
You may know this from your own life. Something upsetting happens on a Tuesday evening, and it feels sharp and heavy. You sleep, perhaps you dream, perhaps you don't remember. And by Wednesday morning, the same memory is still there, but it has lost some of its sting. People have said for centuries that things look different in the morning. Scientists wondered whether that old saying describes something real.

@list A03 cabin_fireplace | The question for this chapter | Does sleep change how a memory feels? ;; Does dreaming play a part? ;; What does the evidence actually show?
The question is a careful one. It is not whether dreams can cure you of anything, because nobody is claiming that. It is whether the sleeping brain handles the feeling attached to a memory differently from the facts of the memory, and whether the dreaming stage of sleep has a role in that.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Matthew Walker & Els van der Helm | Psychological Bulletin, 2009 | Overnight therapy? The role of sleep in emotional brain processing
In 2009, Matthew Walker and Els van der Helm, at the University of California, Berkeley, published a long review of the research on sleep and emotion. They gathered findings from brain imaging, from experiments on memory, and from studies of people with mood problems, and they proposed a framework to tie them together. Their title ended with a question mark, and that is worth noticing.

@char A05 living_room_day | The question mark | Proposing, not proving
It is a good habit to notice a question mark in a title. Scientists use one when they are proposing, and not yet proving. Walker and van der Helm were not announcing that sleep is a form of therapy. They were asking whether it might act a little like one, and they laid out the evidence for and against, so that other researchers could test it. That spirit of careful curiosity is the one we will keep for the rest of this chapter.

@char A15 cabin_fireplace | Two jobs at once | Sleep to remember, sleep to forget
Their idea has a rather poetic name: sleep to forget and sleep to remember. The proposal is that overnight, and especially during REM sleep, your brain does two things at the same time. It keeps the facts of an emotional experience, what happened, where, and with whom. And it gradually reduces the emotional charge attached to that memory. The story stays. The sting fades.

@list A13 cabin_fireplace | What REM sleep offers, according to the review | Emotional brain regions such as the amygdala are active again ;; Slow rhythms called theta waves help regions to communicate ;; Stress-related chemicals are much lower than during waking
The authors point to three features of REM sleep. Emotional regions, including the amygdala, become active again. Theta rhythms may help different regions to share information. And levels of noradrenaline, a chemical linked with stress and alarm, fall very low. That combination, they suggest, could make REM sleep a safe setting in which to revisit an upsetting memory without the full alarm that came with it.

@char A12 living_room_day | A safe rehearsal | Remembering without the alarm
Think of it as looking at an old photograph of a hard day while sitting in a calm room, instead of living the day again. During the day, a painful memory arrives with a rush of stress. During REM sleep, the proposal goes, the memory can be replayed while the stress chemistry is turned down, and each replay might loosen the emotional grip a little. It is a lovely picture. The question is how well the evidence supports it.

@list A03 cabin_fireplace | Findings the review draws on | After 72 hours without sleep, brain responses to negative pictures were not reduced ;; People who slept showed a calmer response ;; In a nap study, emotional memory tracked time spent in REM sleep
The review leans on several experiments. In one of them, people who had been kept awake for 72 hours did not show the drop in amygdala response to negative pictures that people who had slept did show. In another, a nap study, the improvement in emotional memory followed how much REM sleep people had. These are suggestive results, and the authors present them as such.

@photo lf28_b11_01.jpg | A quiet window at night
Imagine a quiet window at night, a lamp left on, the day finally set down. That is where this research places its question. Whatever the sleeping brain does with your feelings, it does it while you are completely unaware, and mostly without a trace by morning. The only evidence we usually have is how the day looks when we wake, and that is exactly why careful experiments matter here.

@char A01 autumn_overlook | Why feelings may need a night | Fresh memories are raw
A memory that is only hours old is still fresh and raw. It has not yet been woven in with everything else you know. One way to picture the idea is that sleep helps with the weaving, linking new experiences to older ones, and putting them in context. Context is calming. A bad afternoon is easier to carry once it has become one event among many in a long life, and not the only thing in the room.

@chapter A10 rooftop_dusk | When life gets hard
There is another strand of this story, one that comes from people going through difficult times, and the researcher most closely linked to it is Rosalind Cartwright.

@char A18 cabin_fireplace | A sleep laboratory | Rosalind Cartwright's question
Rosalind Cartwright was an American sleep researcher, and she asked a very human question. What happens in the dreams of people who are living through a painful life event? To find out, she studied people who were going through a divorce, many of whom were also low in mood, and she recorded their sleep and dreams in the laboratory.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Cartwright, Lloyd, Knight & Trenholme | Psychiatry, 1984 | Broken dreams: a study of the effects of divorce and depression on dream content
One of her early papers, with the title Broken Dreams, looked at how divorce and depression show up in dream content. A later paper, in 1998, with Cartwright and colleagues, asked whether features of REM sleep and of dreams could help to predict who would recover from depression. The project was to ask whether the night, and not only the day, could tell us how a person was coping.

@char A05 living_room_day | A tentative pattern | Dreaming of what hurts
As summaries of her work describe it, there was an interesting pattern. People who dreamed about the person they were separating from, and who felt something in those dreams, tended to be more likely to have recovered from low mood when they were followed up later. People who left that person out of their dreams seemed less likely to have done so. It is important to read this carefully.

@list A12 cabin_fireplace | What this pattern does and does not say | It is a link, not proof of cause ;; Dreams may reflect progress rather than create it ;; Painful dreams are not a sign of harm ;; Summaries of the work leave out many details
A link between two things is not the same as one causing the other. It may be that people who were already coping well were the ones whose dreams could face the hard subject. Or it may be that the dreams helped. The research cannot easily separate these. What it does suggest is gentler than the old fear that a sad dream is a bad sign. A dream that touches a sore spot may sometimes be part of the work of mending.

@char A15 cabin_fireplace | Dreaming as mood care | A possible role, not a certainty
Cartwright herself came to describe dreaming as something like a mood regulation system, an idea that fits neatly with the Walker and van der Helm framework. Both point in the same direction. During sleep, the brain may be working on feelings that the waking day left unfinished. Yet both are careful to say that this is a theory, and that the details are still being tested.

@list A18 cabin_fireplace | Words to keep from this chapter | Amygdala: a brain region involved in emotion ;; Noradrenaline: a chemical linked with stress ;; REM sleep: the stage with the most vivid dreams ;; Theta waves: slow rhythms that link brain regions
Here are four words you may like to keep. The amygdala is a small region deep in the brain that is closely involved in emotion. Noradrenaline is a chemical linked with stress and alarm. REM sleep is the stage of the night when vivid dreams are most common. And theta waves are slow rhythms that may help distant regions of the brain to share information.

@chapter A13 autumn_overlook | What we do not know yet
It is worth being honest about the limits here, because this is a subject where a pleasing story can run well ahead of the evidence.

@char A03 attic_rain | The honest part | Where the evidence is thin
Walker and van der Helm call their model a tentative framework, and they say that many of its predictions have not yet been tested. Most of the experiments they describe used negative or arousing pictures, so we know much less about positive emotions. They also note that some findings do not fit, such as a study in which pictures were rated as more unpleasant after sleep, and they say this puzzle remains open.

@list A10 attic_rain | Open questions in this field | Is it REM sleep itself or the dreams that matter? ;; How much does the effect depend on the person? ;; Do the effects last for days, or only overnight? ;; Why do some nights seem to make things worse?
These are the questions researchers still argue about. One is whether the emotional effects come from the biology of REM sleep, or from the content of the dream, or from both. The review itself says it cannot rule out a role for dream content beyond the physiology. Another is why some people wake from a night of dreams feeling worse, not better. Nobody yet has a settled answer to that.

@char A19 cabin_fireplace | A sensible reading | Neither miracle nor myth
So how should we hold all this? Probably somewhere in the middle. It is unlikely that a night of sleep is a miracle cure for sorrow. It is equally unlikely that dreams are meaningless noise. The more modest reading, and the one the evidence best supports, is that sleep, and REM sleep in particular, seems to help the brain keep what matters about an experience while easing some of its emotional edge.

@char A20 living_room_day | A small experiment | Notice your own mornings
If you like, try a small experiment of noticing, and not of changing anything. On a day when something has bothered you, take note of how it feels in the evening. Then take note again the next morning. You don't need numbers, only a sense of it. Many people find that the morning version is a little softer.

@list A12 living_room_day | What this might mean for you | Hard days may feel different after a night's sleep ;; An upsetting dream does not mean something is wrong ;; Sleep and mood seem closely linked ;; Persistent distress deserves real support
We should keep this simple. If something has weighed on you, it is not strange for it to feel lighter after you have slept. If you have an upsetting dream, it does not mean that something is broken. And if a low mood or bad dreams stay with you for a long time, that is a good reason to talk to a doctor or someone you trust, because this chapter is a story about science and not a treatment.

@quote A13 rooftop_dusk | Sleep to remember and sleep to forget | Walker & van der Helm, 2009
Their phrase captures the whole idea in half a dozen words. Remember the facts, forget the sting. Whether the brain really manages that every night is still being studied, but it is a hopeful way of looking at what happens while we are away.

@char A03 cabin_fireplace | A quiet pause | Your brain keeps working
Before we move on, let's rest on one simple thought. Your brain does not switch off when you do. While you lie still, it is replaying, sorting and linking, and some of that work may be about how you feel.

@char A02 autumn_overlook | A softer view of bad nights | Even a restless night does something
If you have ever had a bad night of dreams and woken tired, it can feel as though sleep has let you down. This research invites a different view. A restless night can still be part of a longer process. Feelings are not settled in one evening, and neither is the brain's work on them. Some of it may happen over many nights, quietly, in the background.

@char A06 cafe_sunset | Looking ahead | From watching to steering
Everything we have discussed so far has treated the dreamer as a passenger, carried along by a story that the brain tells. But a small number of dreamers have a surprising experience. In the middle of a dream, they realise that they are dreaming, and sometimes they can decide what to do next.

@char A15 cabin_fireplace | A door ajar | The dreamer who wakes inside
What happens in the brain at that moment, and whether such a dreamer can send a message to the outside world, has been tested with some of the most remarkable experiments in the history of sleep research. So let's see what those researchers found.
