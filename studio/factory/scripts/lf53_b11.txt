@chapter N20 living_room_day | Inside the earworm brain
The folk tune is playing softly in Ruth's head as she scrolls idly through her phone. She doesn't mind this one at all. In fact, it feels rather nice, like a quiet friend humming in the next room. Earlier, the supermarket chorus felt a little intrusive. Now the folk tune feels like company. Why do some people, and some moments, make earworms feel welcome, and others make them feel like a nuisance?

@study N09 cafe_sunset | Nicolas Farrugia and colleagues | Consciousness and Cognition, 2015 | Tunes stuck in your brain
In 2015, Nicolas Farrugia, Kelly Jakubowski, Rhodri Cusack and Lauren Stewart published a study in Consciousness and Cognition called Tunes stuck in your brain. They asked whether the frequency of earworms, and how people feel about them, could be linked to differences in the physical structure of the brain. It was one of the first studies to look at earworms in this way.

@char N10 living_room_day | The volunteers | Forty-four people
Forty-four healthy volunteers, aged between twenty-five and seventy, took part. They had all been part of earlier brain imaging studies, so detailed MRI scans of their brains already existed. Each person also filled in questionnaires about their musical background and about their experience of earworms, so the researchers could compare the questionnaire answers with the scans.

@char N20 cafe_sunset | What was measured | Thickness and volume
The scans allowed the team to measure the thickness of the cortex, the brain's outer layer, in different regions, and the volume of grey matter, the tissue rich in nerve cell bodies. These measures vary from person to person, and they can be compared with differences in behaviour or experience. The question was whether earworm experiences lined up with any of these structural differences.

@list N27 rooftop_dusk | Regions linked with earworm frequency | Right frontal cortex ;; Right temporal cortex ;; Anterior cingulate ;; Left angular gyrus
And they did. People who reported earworms more often tended to have a thinner cortex in several regions: parts of the right frontal and right temporal cortex, the anterior cingulate, and the left angular gyrus. The temporal regions are involved in hearing, and the frontal and cingulate regions are involved in controlling attention and thought, among other things.

@char N09 living_room_day | Feelings about earworms | Two more links
The researchers also looked at how people felt about their earworms. Those who said they often wanted to suppress them, to push them away, tended to have differences in grey matter volume in a region called the right temporopolar cortex. And those who found their earworms helpful tended to have differences in a region called the right parahippocampal cortex, which is involved in memory and emotion.

@char N20 cafe_sunset | What it doesn't mean | Correlation, not cause
It's important to read this carefully. The study found correlations, links between brain structure and earworm experiences, in forty-four people. It doesn't show that brain structure causes earworms, or that earworms change the brain. Both could be related to something else. And forty-four is a small group for this kind of study. It is best read as a first step, not a final answer.

Even with those cautions, the study adds an interesting idea: that our experience of earworms may be partly shaped by the way our brains are built, in areas that handle both hearing and the control of thought. It fits the picture of an earworm as a meeting point between musical memory and the brain's systems for steering attention.

@study N10 living_room_day | Georgia Floridou and colleagues | Psychomusicology, 2015 | The Involuntary Musical Imagery Scale
To study differences between people, researchers also needed a good way to measure them. In 2015, Georgia Floridou, Victoria Williamson, Lauren Stewart and Daniel Müllensiefen published the Involuntary Musical Imagery Scale, or IMIS, in the journal Psychomusicology. It's a short questionnaire of fifteen items, developed using data from two thousand three hundred and fifteen people.

@list N27 rooftop_dusk | The four parts of the IMIS | Negative valence ;; Movement ;; Personal reflections ;; Help
The scale measures four separate aspects of the experience. Negative valence is about how unpleasant or troubling earworms feel. Movement is about the body's response, such as tapping, singing or dancing along. Personal reflections are about how people link earworms to themselves and their own lives. And help is about the beneficial, constructive side of earworms.

@char N09 cafe_sunset | Helpful earworms | Not just a nuisance
That last factor is worth pausing on. The very fact that researchers built a whole dimension called help into their scale tells us that many people find earworms useful at times. A tune in the head can lift the mood, keep a rhythm during a dull task, or simply keep us company. It's not only a nuisance to be got rid of.

@char N20 living_room_day | Ruth and the four parts | Where would she score?
If Ruth filled in the questionnaire tonight, she might score low on negative valence for the folk tune, but a bit higher for the supermarket chorus. She'd score high on movement, given her tapping foot and humming. She might score moderately on personal reflections, since the folk tune reminds her of her choir friends. And she'd probably score fairly high on help.

@char N10 cabin_fireplace | Different people, different profiles | Many ways to have an earworm
The scale shows that there isn't just one way of experiencing earworms. One person may find them frequent but harmless. Another may find them rare but very upsetting. Someone else may find them mostly a source of energy and rhythm. Measuring these differences carefully is how researchers can move beyond simply asking: do you get earworms?

@list N09 living_room_day | Why measurement matters | Comparing studies
Good questionnaires also make it possible to compare results across studies. When different research teams all use the same scale, their findings can be put side by side. The Farrugia brain study, for instance, used the IMIS to measure how its volunteers felt about their earworms, which is how it could link those feelings to particular brain regions.

@char N20 cafe_sunset | A gentle thought | Your earworms are your own
There's something kind in all of this. However you experience earworms, frequently or rarely, as pleasant or irritating, as a help or a hindrance, you're within the normal range of human experience. Your brain, your musical history and your mood all shape the way music plays in your mind. Ruth's evening is one version. Yours is another.

@char N10 living_room_day | Looking back | Inside the earworm brain
So, in this chapter, we've seen that Farrugia and his colleagues found, in forty-four people, that more frequent earworms were linked to a thinner cortex in frontal, temporal and cingulate regions and the left angular gyrus, with feelings about earworms linked to other regions. And we met the Involuntary Musical Imagery Scale, with its four parts, including one called help.
