@char A15 street_day | Tomas, in the daytime | A walk to the shop
The next morning, Tomas walks to the corner shop, in no hurry, with the night still lingering behind his eyes. He passes a man on a bench, an older neighbour who nods hello. And Tomas thinks, as many of us would, that loneliness is surely more his problem than Tomas's. After all, Tomas is in his early thirties, with a phone full of names.

@quote A10 rooftop_dusk | Only old people are lonely. | A very common assumption
That is today's myth, and it is a very understandable one. We picture loneliness as a grey-haired person in a quiet kitchen, and we rarely picture a young person with a busy calendar. The picture has some truth in it, which is exactly why it lasts so long. But a picture is not a count, and counting is what researchers can do.

@char A19 cabin_fireplace | Why it feels true | Pictures in our heads
Think about where the idea comes from. Films, charity adverts and news stories often show loneliness in later life. And later life does bring real changes. Friends move away. Partners are lost. Health can make it harder to go out. All of that is true, and it matters. Older people can be lonely, and they deserve warm attention. The question is only whether they are the only ones.

@char A03 autumn_overlook | A kind clarification | Not either, or
So let's be clear about what we are not saying. Nobody is claiming that older adults are fine. Some of the studies we met earlier followed adults over fifty, and their findings matter a great deal. The question tonight is simpler. If we asked people of every age how lonely they feel, who would say yes? And what might surprise us in the answer?

@chapter A18 living_room_day | Asking people of every age
There was a way to find out, and it came from an unusual place. A broadcaster, the BBC, worked with researchers to run a very large survey of loneliness, open to anyone who wanted to join in.

@study A12 cafe_sunset | Barreto and colleagues | Personality and Individual Differences, published 2021 | Younger people reported greater loneliness than older people
You might expect older people to be the loneliest. The research team, led by Manuela Barreto, analysed answers from the BBC Loneliness Experiment, and they looked at how loneliness varied with age, gender and culture. On age, the result was a surprise to many people. Younger people reported more loneliness than older people, and loneliness steadily decreased as people got older.

@stat A02 cafe_sunset | People who joined | 46,000 | aged 16 to 99, from many countries
Here is the scale. More than forty-six thousand people joined in, aged sixteen all the way to ninety-nine. They came from many countries and cultures. A group like that can show a trend that no small survey could. And the trend, across all of those lives, pointed in a direction that almost nobody expected.

@char A13 attic_rain | Reading it fairly | A volunteer crowd
It is fair to read this with a little care. This was a large survey that people chose to join, so it may not match every population exactly. And a survey gives a snapshot of how people feel, not a full story of their lives. But the pattern, the youngest reporting the most loneliness, was clear enough to make a lot of researchers think again.

@char A12 autumn_overlook | Why it surprised people | An old picture meets a new count
Why was this so surprising? Because the old picture had been repeated for so long that few people thought to test it. When a belief is comfortable, and seems to fit a few vivid examples, it tends to stay in place. A large count is a wonderful thing for that reason. It lets us look at the pattern itself, instead of the examples that come to mind first.

@list A10 cabin_fireplace | What this survey suggests | Loneliness is not only for later life ;; Younger adults reported the most loneliness ;; It tended to ease with age ;; Older adults can still be lonely
Let's gather what it says, in plain words. Loneliness is not only for later life. Younger adults reported the most. On the whole, it tended to ease as people got older. And none of that means older adults are never lonely. It simply means the picture in our heads was too narrow, and widening it is a kindness to everyone.

@char A05 street_day | Tomas, on the pavement | A small surprise
Tomas stops outside the shop with a loaf of bread in his hand. He realises that he has never once thought of himself as the sort of person this research is about. And yet, here it is. Someone his age, in a flat with a good phone and a busy diary, is exactly the person the survey was talking about. It is a strangely comforting thought.

@char A18 rooftop_dusk | Not a competition | Nobody has to win
There is one more thing to say, and it is important. None of this turns loneliness into a competition. Nobody is asking who is lonelier, the young or the old, as if sadness were a race with a prize. Each person's loneliness is felt from the inside, and it deserves the same care. The survey simply widens the circle of who we expect to notice.

Picture a flat on the first day of a new life. A few cardboard boxes. A plant on the sill. Floorboards that still smell faintly of polish, and sunlight with nothing in it but dust. It is a hopeful room, and for many people it is also a very quiet one. And that is where our next question begins.

@chapter A03 rooftop_dusk | Why the young feel it
If loneliness is common in younger adults, what might be going on in their lives? Here we step away from the survey itself, and into ordinary reasoning. What follows is not a finding, only a set of reasons that seem sensible and familiar.

@list A19 living_room_day | Everyday reasons, not findings | Moving to a new city ;; Starting a new job ;; Finishing studies and scattering friends ;; Working from home
Think about the early adult years. People move to new cities, and the old friends stay behind. They start jobs where nobody knows them yet. They finish studies, and the daily crowd of classmates dissolves overnight. Some, like Tomas, work from a flat, where days can pass without a single unplanned conversation. None of these is a fault. Each is a change.

@char A18 cabin_fireplace | Transitions | A gap, not a flaw
Notice the pattern here. Each of these changes removes something that used to arrive for free. A classroom brought people together without anyone arranging it. A hometown did the same. When those fade, connection suddenly has to be made on purpose, and few of us were ever taught how. So the gap is not a flaw in the person. It is a gap in the arrangement.

@char A12 cafe_sunset | Tomas's own version | A different flat, a different city
For Tomas, the story is one of those changes. A city where everyone seems to have somewhere to be. A job that fits into a laptop bag. Evenings that begin with an open screen and end with a closed one. It would be strange if he did not feel a little unmoored. It would be stranger still if he felt it was his fault.

@quote A09 bedroom_night | Everyone else seems to have it figured out. | A thought many young adults know
It is a thought that comes with the territory. Everyone else seems to have it figured out. Everyone else seems to have a group, a plan, a table at the weekend. But we have already seen that minds on the alert tend to underestimate how much others like us, and so the same may be true of how many others are also feeling lost.

@char A19 attic_rain | The quiet crowd | Many people, similar nights
That is a gentle thing to hold on to. If loneliness is most commonly reported by younger adults, then somewhere nearby, on the same street, there are probably other people awake with the same thought. Hardly anyone mentions it, because it feels like a private failing. But a private failing, shared by tens of thousands in a survey, starts to look like something else entirely.

@char A15 living_room_day | What it means for you | Whatever your age
So what might this mean for you? That your age is not an answer. If you are young and lonely, you are in very large company, and nothing has gone wrong with you. If you are older and lonely, you are not alone in that, either. Loneliness does not check a birth certificate. It simply visits, and it can be met with some patience.

@char A10 living_room_day | A word for older listeners | You are not forgotten
If you are older, and listening on a quiet night, a word for you as well. Nothing here says your loneliness is less real. The research is not about who deserves attention. It is about making sure that nobody is left out of the picture, whatever age they happen to be. You belong in this story just as much as Tomas does, and just as warmly.

@quote A19 rooftop_dusk | Nothing has gone wrong with you. | A line for any age
This is a small line, and not from any study. But the survey does give us a reason to say it. If so many people, of so many ages, report feeling lonely, then loneliness is not evidence of a broken person. It is evidence of a human one. A person made for company, noticing, quite correctly, that the company has gone a little quiet.

@list A10 cabin_fireplace | Tonight's thoughts on age | The myth is only half a picture ;; Younger adults reported the most loneliness ;; Life changes can open a gap ;; A gap is not a flaw
Let's keep four small ones. The myth gives only half a picture. Younger adults reported the most loneliness in a very large survey. Life changes can open a gap in connection. And a gap is not a flaw. Those four are enough to loosen an old assumption, and loosening an assumption is a very restful thing to do.
