@chapter A15 living_room_day | Forbidden snacks
Almost every home has one. A high cupboard, a special tin, a drawer that's only opened on Sundays. Inside, the treats that children are not supposed to have, or at least not yet. It's one of the most natural rules a loving parent can make. And it's one of the most interesting places to watch reactance at work.

@char A02 street_day | A familiar scene | Just one, please?
Picture a small child who knows exactly where the biscuits are kept. Every visit to the kitchen comes with a glance at that cupboard. Can I have one? Just one? Please? The treat is barely visible, and yet it seems to occupy half the child's thoughts.

@char A10 cafe_sunset | The question | Does keeping it away make it matter more?
So here's what researchers wanted to know. When we put a tasty food out of reach, does that help children eat less of it? Or does the rule itself make the food more desirable, so that they want it more, and eat more of it the moment they get the chance?

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Jennifer Fisher & Leann Birch | American Journal of Clinical Nutrition, 1999 | Restricting access to palatable foods affects children's behavioral response, food selection, and intake
In a study published in 1999, Jennifer Fisher and Leann Birch worked with preschool children, aged around three to five. Over several weeks, the children were offered two similar, tasty snacks. One was freely available. The other was placed in a clear jar, where the children could see it, but could only have it during short, limited moments.

@char A03 living_room_day | What happened | The restricted snack became special
The restricted snack quickly became the star of the show. When the jar was opened, the children paid more attention to it, asked for it more, reached for it more, and ate more of it than they had before the restriction began. The snack itself hadn't changed at all. Only its status had. And remember, the two snacks had been chosen to be very similar. The only real difference between them was the rule. One was simply there. The other was something you had to wait for.

@char A12 cafe_sunset | The cookie jar again | Scarcity at the snack table
If this sounds familiar, it should. It's the same pattern we met with scarce cookies and banned products. Something that is hard to get feels more precious. And for a small child, a clear jar that is only opened now and then is about as precious as it gets.

@char A19 attic_rain | Seen but not reached | Why the jar mattered
There's something clever about the clear jar in that study. The children could see the snack all the time, but they couldn't decide when to have it. That's a very particular kind of feeling. The food was always present, and always just out of their control. It's hard to imagine a better recipe for wanting something more.

@list A05 cabin_fireplace | What restriction did to the snack | More attention ;; More requests ;; More eating when it was allowed
Notice that none of these were what the rule intended. The aim of restriction is usually to reduce how much of a food a child eats. In the short run, at the moments when the food did appear, it seemed to do the opposite.

@chapter A13 rooftop_dusk | Do not eat the red ones
A few years later, a team in the Netherlands made the experiment even simpler. No jars, no weeks of restriction. Just one small rule, given once.

@study A04 attic_rain | Anita Jansen, Sandra Mulkens & Arnoud Jansen | Appetite, 2007 | Do not eat the red food! Prohibition of snacks leads to their relatively higher consumption in children
In 2007, Anita Jansen and her colleagues gave children a bowl of small sweets in several colours. Some of the children were told not to eat the red ones. Other children were given no rule at all. Later, all the children were allowed to eat whichever sweets they liked.

@char A10 living_room_day | The result | The red ones disappeared
Once the rule was lifted, the children who had been told not to eat the red sweets ate relatively more of the red ones than the children who had never heard any rule. The colour itself made no difference to the taste. What changed was that red had become the forbidden colour, and forbidden things seem to taste a little sweeter.

@char A13 bedroom_night | Like the white bear | A rule that keeps the food in mind
This is where reactance and the white bear meet. A rule like don't eat the red ones does two things at once. It threatens a small freedom, which invites pushback. And it keeps the forbidden food at the front of the mind, which makes it harder to forget. Both point in the same direction.

@char A05 cafe_sunset | Why this matters | A rule can outlast the moment
And the effect doesn't always stay at the table. A child who learns that a certain food is special and forbidden may carry that feeling for years. The cupboard becomes a little shrine. The treat becomes a reward, a comfort, a prize. Ordinary food can slowly turn into something much more loaded than it ever needed to be.

None of this means children should be given unlimited sweets. That's not what the researchers suggested. But it does mean that how a food is handled can change the way children feel about it, sometimes for longer than we'd expect.

@char A12 autumn_overlook | When healthy food becomes a chore | The other side of the rule
And it works the other way round, too. If forbidding a food can make it more tempting, what happens when we push children towards a food instead? Finish your vegetables, and then you can play.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Leann Birch, Diane Marlin & Julie Rotter | Child Development, 1984 | Eating as the means activity in a contingency: effects on young children's food preference
In 1984, Leann Birch and her colleagues gave preschool children a drink they hadn't tasted before. For some children, drinking it was the condition for getting to do something fun afterwards. Other children simply had the drink, with no deal attached. Then the researchers measured how much the children liked the drink, and compared the two groups.

@char A03 cabin_fireplace | The finding | The deal made it less liked
The children who had been asked to drink it in order to earn a reward ended up liking it less. The deal had sent a quiet message. This is something you have to get through to reach the good part. And children, like the rest of us, are very good at reading between the lines.

@char A18 bedroom_night | A gentle reminder | Small studies, real patterns
It's worth saying that these were small studies with young children, in particular settings. They don't mean that every rule about food will backfire, or that every reward ruins a vegetable forever. But together, they point to a pattern that many parents will recognise from their own kitchen.

@list A20 living_room_day | Gentler ways to handle treats | Keep them ordinary, not forbidden ;; Offer them now and then, calmly, with no big fuss ;; Serve healthy foods often, without deals or bribes ;; Eat well together and let children copy you
The idea isn't to give up on guidance. It's to take the drama out of it. A treat that is simply part of life, now and then, tends to lose some of its magic. And a vegetable that isn't a hurdle has a better chance of becoming a friend.

@char A13 rooftop_dusk | Not just children | The adult version
And if you're smiling at all this, thinking of your own childhood, it's worth noticing that adults do it too. A strict diet that bans a favourite food can make that food feel more tempting than ever. The jar may be different. The feeling is much the same. Often, the healthiest relationship with food is one where nothing is completely off-limits, and nothing is a battle.

@char A15 cafe_sunset | Who reacts most? | Some of us push back harder
But here's something you may have noticed along the way. Not every child grabs for the jar, and not every adult rebels against a rule. Some people seem to shrug off a ban, while others feel it like a pebble in their shoe. So who reacts most strongly, and why? That's what we'll explore next.
