Here's the holiday paradox. Imagine a week away somewhere new. While you're there, the days seem to rush by. Before you know it, it's time to pack. And yet, when you get home and look back, that week feels long and rich, as if you'd been away for much longer. Meanwhile, an ordinary week at home might drag while you live it, and then vanish almost completely from memory.

Let's set it out simply. A new and varied week away tends to feel fast while it's happening, and long when you remember it. An ordinary, repetitive week at home can feel slow while it's happening, and short when you remember it. The two kinds of time we met earlier, in the moment and looking back, are pulling in opposite directions.

@char A20 street_day | Why the reversal? | Memory again
Why would that happen? The explanation Hammond and others offer leans on memory. A week in a new place creates lots of new, distinct memories. New streets, new tastes, small surprises, little decisions. When you look back, there's a lot there, and the mind seems to read that richness as length. An ordinary week, by contrast, leaves few new memories behind, and so it seems short in hindsight.

So here's one way to remember it. Fun can make time fly in the moment. But newness can make it long in memory. These aren't opposites. A good holiday can do both at once. And a dull routine can do the reverse, dragging in the moment and then disappearing in hindsight. The question is not only whether we enjoy our days, but how different they are from each other.

So what's the verdict on our fourth popular belief, that time flies when you're having fun? It's true, but only half true. It describes how time feels in the moment. Which brings us to the heart of tonight's question. If looking back on a year is partly a matter of memory, then memory acts like a kind of measuring tape. When we try to judge how long a stretch of time was, the mind seems to ask, in a quiet way, how much do I remember from it? And the answer to that question shapes how long it feels.

After all, we don't have a ruler for the past. There's no little counter in the head ticking off each day of the year, waiting to be read on New Year's Eve. Instead, when Hanna thinks about her year, her mind has to make a kind of best guess, built from whatever it can reach. Some stretches come back clearly. Others hardly come back at all.

@char A03 cabin_fireplace | Routine and time | A careful study
In 2003, two researchers at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem, Dinah Avni-Babad and Ilana Ritov, published a paper with a simple title: Routine and the Perception of Time. They were interested in exactly this question. How does doing the same thing again and again affect our sense of how long things take, and how long they seemed afterwards?

@study A13 cafe_sunset | Dinah Avni-Babad and Ilana Ritov | Published in 2003 | Routine and the Perception of Time
Their work became part of a wider picture that many psychologists now share. Routine, it seems, makes time feel shorter when we look back on it. When days follow a familiar pattern, fewer of them stand out, and so they take up less room in memory. That doesn't make routine bad. Routines can be comforting and useful. But they leave a lighter footprint on our sense of time.

@char A12 street_day | The well-travelled road | A familiar route
You might know a small version of this from your own life. Think of a journey you make often, like the way to work, or to a friend's house. The first time, it probably felt long. You were watching for turns and landmarks. But after many trips, the same route seems to shrink. You arrive and hardly remember the drive at all. Some writers call this the well-travelled road effect.

@char A10 rooftop_dusk | Childhood's long road | Everything new
Now imagine being a child, when nearly every road is new. When school, friends, seasons and even simple things like snow or the sea are still being discovered. A child's year is packed with firsts, and so it may leave a dense trail of memories behind it. Later in life, many of those firsts have already happened, and more of each year can slip into familiar patterns.
