@chapter N10 living_room_day | Curiosity
Curiosity is one of the nicest feelings the mind can have. It's the pull to know, the itch that comes before a discovery. And when scientists looked at it closely, they found that it has a lot in common with boredom, as though the two were a pair, one pushing and one pulling.

@char N19 cabin_fireplace | A gap | Wanting to know
In 1994, the economist and psychologist George Loewenstein, at Carnegie Mellon University, published a review in Psychological Bulletin called The psychology of curiosity. His idea is known as the information gap theory. Curiosity, he argued, arises when we notice a gap between what we know and what we want to know.

@char N09 cafe_sunset | An everyday example | The unfinished story
You know the feeling. Someone starts a story and breaks off. Or you see a headline that asks a question, or a door that's slightly ajar. The gap opens, and you want to close it. And importantly, you need to know something already, to notice the gap at all. A complete beginner is rarely curious about the finer details of a subject, because the gap isn't visible.

@char N02 rooftop_dusk | A deeper idea | Curiosity feels like hunger
Loewenstein suggested that curiosity can feel a little like hunger, an uncomfortable wanting that is relieved by an answer. That makes it a very similar mental shape to boredom. Both are a wanting, and the difference is that curiosity has a target, while boredom is a wanting that hasn't found one yet.

@char N10 cabin_fireplace | Another view | Interest has two parts
The psychologist Paul Silvia, in his 2006 book Exploring the Psychology of Interest, offered a related picture. He suggested that interest involves two appraisals. First, is this new or complex or surprising? And second, can I make sense of it? When both answers are yes, we feel interested.

@char N19 living_room_day | A neat link | Back to the Goldilocks zone
If you've been following closely, you'll see how neatly that echoes the challenge and skill idea. Something too familiar isn't interesting. Something too baffling isn't interesting either. The sweet spot is a puzzle you can almost solve, and that's where curiosity burns brightest.

@study N09 cafe_sunset | Kang et al., 2009 | Caltech and Carnegie Mellon University, published in Psychological Science | Volunteers rated their curiosity about trivia questions while a scanner recorded brain activity, and were later tested on the answers
What happens in the brain? In 2009, Min Jeong Kang and colleagues, including Colin Camerer, published an experiment in Psychological Science. Volunteers read trivia questions and rated how curious they were about the answers, while their brains were scanned. The study was titled The wick in the candle of learning.

@char N23 autumn_overlook | The findings | Reward circuits at work
They reported that when people were more curious, activity increased in parts of the brain linked with reward, including a region called the caudate. And, when they were curious about a question, they were also more likely to remember the answer later. Curiosity seemed to turn on the brain's reward and learning machinery together.

@study N10 living_room_day | Gruber, Gelman and Ranganath, 2014 | University of California, Davis, published in Neuron | Curious volunteers also remembered unrelated faces shown while they waited for the answer
Five years later, Matthias Gruber, Bernard Gelman and Charan Ranganath published a study in Neuron, in 2014. They found that when people were in a state of high curiosity, they also remembered unrelated faces better if the faces appeared while they waited for the answer. The curious state seemed to prepare the memory system, in a way that involved dopamine-related regions.

@char N09 cabin_fireplace | Why this matters | Curiosity helps learning
That's a delightful finding. It suggests that being curious doesn't only help us learn the thing we're curious about. It may also make us better at taking in other things around the same time. Curiosity, in effect, opens the brain's doors a little wider.

@char N19 rooftop_dusk | A wider picture | Why animals explore
In 2015, Celeste Kidd and Benjamin Hayden published a review in Neuron, called The psychology and neuroscience of curiosity. They brought together work on humans and other animals and suggested that curiosity is a tool for exploring and learning, driven by the brain's drive to reduce uncertainty. It's a good, broad view of why we feel it at all.

@char N10 living_room_day | A pair | Boredom and curiosity
So how do boredom and curiosity connect? A neat way to see it is as a pair. Boredom is the signal that says, this isn't engaging me. Curiosity is the pull toward something that might. Boredom clears the ground, and curiosity picks a direction.

@char N02 cafe_sunset | A shift | From stuck to seeking
That's why the shift in Mira's mind, from the nagging wait to the wondering about the receptionist and the building, is such a good example. The boredom didn't vanish, it turned into a question. Once there's a question, attention has a handle, and the mind starts to move.

@char N23 rooftop_dusk | A skill | Asking small questions
And this suggests something practical. Curiosity can be nudged by asking small questions about whatever is in front of you. Why is this building shaped this way? What do the people on this bus do all day? How does a kettle know when to stop? Each question opens a tiny gap, and a tiny gap is often enough to wake attention up.

@char N09 living_room_day | A caution | Don't force it
It's not about forcing enthusiasm, though. If you try to be fascinated by something that has no interest for you, it can feel fake. The idea is closer to a gentle experiment. Ask a question and see whether anything stirs. If not, try another. You're simply looking for the handle that your attention can grab.

@char N19 cabin_fireplace | Different curiosities | A variety of styles
Researchers also point out that people differ in what kind of curiosity they have. In a 2018 paper in the Journal of Research in Personality, Todd Kashdan and colleagues described five dimensions, including a joy of exploring, a sensitivity to deprivation when we don't know something, and a comfort with the stress that comes with the unknown. People vary on each, which may be why one person finds a museum thrilling and another finds it dull.

@char N10 rooftop_dusk | Why this is hopeful | Interest can grow
Here's the encouraging part. Interest isn't fixed. Knowing a little about a subject can make you see the gaps in it, and that can make you want to know more. Many people find that the things they were bored by at school, a language, a type of music, a branch of science, became fascinating later, once they had enough knowledge to see what was missing.

@char N27 cabin_fireplace | A reminder | Boredom can become connection
Boredom, here, became a bridge. It pushed Mira out of her own head and toward another person, which is one of the oldest ways that humans find meaning. And while it's just a story, it echoes the research we've seen on how boredom can nudge us toward connection and purpose.
