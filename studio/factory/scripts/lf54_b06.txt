@chapter A03 living_room_day | A cooling breeze
The heating in Oskar's carriage has been working a little too well. He takes off his jacket and presses the back of his hand against his forehead. It's warm. If one group of researchers is on the right track, that detail might matter more than the stale air he was worrying about earlier. Their idea is that yawning is connected to the temperature of the brain.

@list N29 cafe_sunset | The brain cooling hypothesis | The brain works best in a narrow temperature range ;; A deep breath and wide jaw may move blood and air ;; This might carry some heat away ;; So conditions that cool the head should reduce yawning
Here's the reasoning. Your brain is busy, it produces heat, and it works best within a fairly narrow range. A yawn stretches the jaw, which may change blood flow around the head, and draws in a large breath, usually of air cooler than your body. Together these might help shed a little heat. If so, cooling the head in other ways ought to make yawns less necessary.

@study A12 cabin_fireplace | Andrew Gallup and Gordon Gallup Jr. | Evolutionary Psychology, published 2007 | Breathing styles and forehead packs
Andrew Gallup and Gordon Gallup, working at the University at Albany, tested that prediction in a study published in 2007. Volunteers watched videos of people yawning, which is a reliable way to bring yawns out. Some were asked to breathe in particular ways while they watched. Others held a pack against their forehead that was cold, warm or at room temperature.

@stat A10 rooftop_dusk | Watching yawns while breathing through the nose | No yawns | against about half of normal or mouth breathers
The breathing results were striking. About half of the people who breathed normally or through their mouths yawned while watching. Those who were told to breathe through their noses didn't. Nasal breathing is thought to help cool blood that's heading toward the brain, so this fitted the prediction, and rather dramatically.

@char N09 autumn_overlook | The forehead packs | Cold against warm
The packs pointed the same way. People holding a cold pack to the forehead did not yawn in response to the videos, while those with a warm pack or one at room temperature went on yawning as people usually do. Cooling the front of the head seemed to switch off the contagious yawns that the videos would otherwise have produced.

@char N10 bedroom_night | Worth a second look | Open questions
These are memorable results, and they're worth reading carefully. The volunteers knew whether they were breathing through their noses or holding something cold, so expectations or simple distraction could play a part. The study measured contagious yawns brought out by videos, and it didn't measure brain temperature in these volunteers. It supports the idea without proving the mechanism.

@photo lf54_b06_01.jpg | A cool cloth and water
A damp folded cloth beside a bowl of cool water. People have pressed cool things to their foreheads when feeling overheated and dull for as long as anyone can remember. The research doesn't turn this into a proven trick for stopping yawns in daily life, but it gives a curious new angle on a very familiar gesture.

@char N20 attic_rain | Seasons as a test | Nature's own thermostat
There's another way to test a temperature idea: let the weather change the temperature for you. If cooling is part of what yawning is for, then how often people yawn might shift with the air around them. When it's very cold outside, a yawn might not be needed. When the air is nearly as warm as the body, drawing it in might not help much.

@study N01 living_room_day | Jorg Massen, Kim Dusch, Omar Eldakar and Andrew Gallup | Physiology and Behavior, published 2014 | Pedestrians in a Vienna winter and summer
In a study published in 2014, Jorg Massen and colleagues asked one hundred and twenty pedestrians in Vienna to look at pictures of people yawning and then say whether they had yawned. Half were asked in winter, when the average temperature was just over one degree Celsius. The other half were asked in summer, when it was around nineteen degrees.

@bars N27 cafe_sunset | Reported yawning in Vienna | percent | Winter:18 ;; Summer:42
In winter, eleven of the sixty people, about eighteen percent, reported yawning. In summer, it was twenty-five of sixty, close to forty-two percent. Temperature was the factor that predicted the difference in their analysis. So in Vienna, the mild summer air went with more yawning than the freezing winter air did.

@char A03 cabin_fireplace | Now somewhere hot | Florida in late summer
What about real heat? In a related study published in 2015, Omar Eldakar and colleagues approached one hundred and forty-two pedestrians in Fort Lauderdale, Florida, during August and September. Temperatures ranged from about twenty-eight to thirty-six degrees Celsius. People viewed a set of yawn pictures and reported whether they had caught one.

@stat N29 rooftop_dusk | Fort Lauderdale, 28 to 36 degrees | Falling | the hotter the day, the fewer reported contagious yawns
Overall, about thirty-seven percent reported contagious yawning. And within that hot range, the pattern now ran the other way from Vienna: the higher the temperature, the less likely people were to report it. Even among those who didn't yawn, the reported urge to yawn was weaker on the hotter days.

@curve A12 autumn_overlook | The proposed thermal window | Air temperature | Yawning | 0.5 | most in mild warmth
Put the two together and you get the idea of a thermal window. Yawning would be low in bitter cold, higher in mild warmth, and lower again as the air approaches body temperature. This sketch shows the shape the researchers propose. It's a picture of a hypothesis built from separate studies in separate places, so don't read exact values into it.

@char A10 bedroom_night | Why self-report matters | An honest limit
These street studies have limits. People reported their own yawns, and we're not always accurate about that, or willing to admit it to a stranger. Different seasons also bring different daylight, sleep and daily routines. The researchers accounted for several factors, but a pavement can't be controlled like a laboratory. It's interesting evidence, and it deserves further testing.

@char N09 attic_rain | Not everyone agrees | A live debate
The brain cooling hypothesis has critics. Some researchers question whether a few seconds of yawning could shift brain temperature by an amount that matters, and others think arousal or social signalling explains more. This is a live scientific debate. I'd describe cooling as one of the leading ideas with a growing set of supporting observations, rather than as the settled answer.

@char N10 living_room_day | Oskar takes off a layer | A small experiment of his own
Oskar loosens his collar and takes a slow breath in through his nose, just to see. The next yawn seems to hold off. He's pleased with himself, though he knows perfectly well that one man on one train, who knows what he's hoping to find, is no kind of evidence at all. Still, the cool breath feels pleasant.

@list N20 cafe_sunset | Where we are | Nose breathing, cold packs: no yawns ;; Vienna: more in mild summer ;; Florida: less on hotter days ;; Mechanism still debated
So here's where the temperature trail has taken us. In one laboratory study, nose breathing and cold packs went with a lack of contagious yawns. In Vienna, mild summer weather went with more reported yawning than winter. In Florida, hotter days went with less. And the mechanism is still being argued over, which is how science ought to sound.

If yawning really has something to do with looking after the brain, you might wonder whether animals with bigger brains yawn differently. A large international team wondered exactly that, and the way they answered it involved a great many videos of birds and mammals, caught in the middle of opening their mouths. That's our next stop.
