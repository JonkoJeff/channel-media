@chapter A13 living_room_day | A picture is not yet a memory
And that distinction gives us somewhere useful to pause. You can picture an event, think it might have happened, and feel as though you remember being there. Those descriptions sound close together in conversation. For understanding this experiment, though, we need to leave a little space between them.

@char A19 bedroom_night | What does “remember” mean? | Let the question stay precise
Imagine someone asking whether you once wore a paper crown at a childhood gathering. This is just an example, not an invitation to search your past. You might answer, “Possibly.” You might picture a crown. Or you might describe a particular afternoon. Each answer would give the listener something different to work with.

@list A03 living_room_day | Different questions | Could it have happened? ;; Can I picture it? ;; Do I experience remembering it?
For our purposes, belief concerns whether you think an event happened. Imagery concerns what you can picture. Recollective experience concerns the feeling of remembering an occasion. You may find those experiences overlap, but you can still ask which one best describes what you mean.

@char A05 living_room_day | Leave room between the answers | A possible scene
Suppose that picture is very clear. Ask what it establishes on its own. It establishes that you have a picture to describe. To establish a childhood event, you would need to answer another question: what connects this particular picture to something that happened?

@study A10 living_room_day | Garry and colleagues | University of Washington; Indiana University | Published in 1996
Garry, Manning, Loftus, and Sherman measured confidence that childhood events occurred. Participants gave ratings before and after the imagination exercise. Before deciding what those answers mean, let's look at the comparison behind them.

@char A18 bedroom_night | A small but useful gap | Changing an answer
Think about the difference between saying, “I doubt it,” and saying, “Perhaps.” That is a meaningful change in an answer. Yet neither phrase tells us that the speaker now sees a whole scene unfolding. We should not quietly supply that scene ourselves while describing what the speaker reported.

@list A12 living_room_day | Read the answer at its own level | A confidence judgment ;; A description of imagery ;; A report of recollection
One way to keep the language honest is to ask what you would write down as an observer. If someone gives a confidence judgment, record that judgment. If they describe an image, record the image. If they report remembering, record that report. You can describe what they tell you without filling in the details they leave open.

@char A03 living_room_day | The comparison matters | What changed without imagination?
There is another question waiting beside the first. Even if a rating rises after an imagination exercise, how much might it have changed without that exercise? A before-and-after story can sound complete while leaving out the comparison that makes the change interpretable.

@study A13 living_room_day | The control comparison | Imagined events versus not-imagined events | Both were rated again
Each participant imagined selected events; other target events served as controls. The researchers compared later ratings for imagined and not-imagined events. That comparison matters because a changed answer, by itself, does not identify imagination as its explanation.

@char A20 living_room_day | Give the comparison a job | More than a second measurement
If you looked only at the imagined events, you would have just one side of the comparison. The other answers let you ask a sharper question: what is different about the changes associated with the extra imagination exercise? You can compare the changes instead of assuming that every shift needs the same explanation.

@bars A10 living_room_day | Ratings that stayed unchanged | percent | Imagined:57 ;; Not imagined:65
In the paper’s pooled descriptive results for initially unlikely events, 57% of imagined-event ratings and 65% of not-imagined-event ratings stayed unchanged. These are event ratings from the two conditions. We'll return to what those figures can tell us after considering the other possible explanations.

@char A19 bedroom_night | Keep the unit attached | A rating is the thing counted
As you look at those figures, picture answers being counted. One person could contribute answers about several events. You are comparing ratings here, so a percentage of ratings is not automatically a percentage of people.

@photo lf14_b07_01.jpg | Space for a careful answer
You can leave a little space before settling on an answer. “I can picture it, but I do not know whether it happened” is a complete answer for our example. It does not require you to reject the picture. It simply keeps the picture separate from a historical claim.

@chapter A05 living_room_day | What else could explain a change?
Now we can give the alternative explanations a fair hearing. A careful interpretation does not begin by choosing the most dramatic account. It asks what other routes could lead to a higher rating, and whether the experiment can distinguish those routes.

@list A13 living_room_day | Alternatives discussed in the paper | Familiarity ;; Reinterpreting an event ;; Remembering a genuine experience
The authors considered familiarity, broader interpretations of an event description, and genuine remembering. They also discussed regression toward the mean when interpreting changes without imagination. These were possible explanations to examine, not separately proven mechanisms behind every changed response.

@char A18 bedroom_night | The familiarity possibility | Knowing the idea
For a simple illustration, imagine meeting the same question again. You might recognize the question perfectly well while remaining uncertain about the childhood occasion. If you were explaining your answer aloud, it would help to say which thing you recognized: the recently encountered idea, or the proposed event itself.

@char A01 living_room_day | Recognition needs an object | Familiar in what way?
That is why “It feels familiar” invites a follow-up rather than finishing the discussion. Familiar as a phrase? Familiar as a scene you just constructed? Familiar as an event you experience remembering? You can hear how much remains open when the word familiar arrives without an explanation beside it.

@list A10 living_room_day | Keep a possibility conditional | It might contribute ;; It could explain some answers ;; This result does not identify every route
Notice the difference between “familiarity could contribute” and “familiarity caused this person’s answer.” The first offers an explanation to investigate. The second announces that the explanation has been established. We can consider the first without slipping into the second.

@char A12 living_room_day | The reinterpretation possibility | What counts as the event?
Return to the paper-crown example. Perhaps you initially understand the question as asking about a birthday celebration. Later, you wonder whether a classroom activity counts too. In that hypothetical exchange, a more permissive answer could reflect a different reading of the question, rather than a newly appearing scene.

@quote A03 living_room_day | What exactly am I saying happened? | A question to ask yourself
That question is useful here because an event description has edges. What occasion would qualify? What detail is essential? In our example, you could clarify those edges before deciding what your answer means. You would be making the statement more precise, without pretending that precision alone proves it true.

@char A19 bedroom_night | Another possibility | A genuine experience comes to mind
There is also the possibility that thinking about a question brings a genuine occasion to mind. It would be just as careless to rule that out automatically as to assume it happened. A careful reader must leave room for an answer to become more accurate, as well as less accurate.

@study A13 living_room_day | A stated limitation | Garry and colleagues, 1996 | Genuine remembering was not ruled out
The authors explicitly could not rule out improved remembering of genuinely experienced events. Their account therefore leaves uncertainty about what underlies particular rating changes. We should preserve that uncertainty when translating the experiment into an everyday story.

@char A05 living_room_day | Resist supplying the missing scene | A reader’s temptation
It is tempting to turn a changed response into a small movie: first an empty space, then an imagined picture, then a convincing childhood memory. That would make a tidy narrative. But the tidy narrative includes steps that a confidence rating does not, by itself, show us.

@list A20 living_room_day | What you can take from the result | Describe the measured change ;; Consider competing explanations ;; Leave unresolved details open
You can now return to those percentages with their limits in view. They do not count people who acquired false memories, and the authors cautioned against an overall statistical comparison of those pooled observations. You can still find the result interesting while leaving the reason for a particular person's changed answer unresolved.

@photo lf14_b07_02.jpg | A thought, then a conversation
Imagine moving to the other chair. Until now, our example has involved a question and a privately pictured scene. Soon another person will join the story. Before they do, it helps to keep hold of the distinction we have just made.

@char A10 living_room_day | No need to finish the picture | Uncertainty can remain
You are allowed to stop at “I am unsure.” For this example, there is no prize for producing a richer account. You do not have to choose between declaring the scene true and declaring every detail false. You can describe the limits of your answer in ordinary language.

@list A03 living_room_day | Words that preserve the distinction | I can imagine that ;; I think it may have happened ;; I experience remembering an occasion
Listen to those phrases as different claims, rather than as rungs you are expected to climb. The first does not oblige you to reach the last. If you were discussing this example with someone, choosing the phrase that fits would make your meaning easier to understand.

@char A18 bedroom_night | An image can stay an image | No inevitable conversion
And there is our answer to the question we left open. No, ordinary imagination does not inevitably become memory. Nothing about considering this experiment requires you to fear a passing invented scene. Our task is simply to avoid treating the ability to picture something as sufficient evidence that the event occurred.

@chapter A13 living_room_day | When the detail comes from someone else
Now let another person sit across from you. Suppose they mention a detail you have not supplied yourself. You can understand what they mean, consider whether you believe them, and ask whether you independently remember it. Those remain different questions, even when the conversation feels easy.

@char A05 living_room_day | From a private picture to a shared account | Where did this detail begin?
That brings us from information generated privately to information received from another person. We can now ask what happens when people discuss an event before reporting it individually. The next experiment gives that question a concrete setting: people who have seen different views of what appears to be the same event.
