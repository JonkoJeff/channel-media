@char A13 autumn_overlook | Babies cry first | Then smile, then laugh
Babies cry from the very first moment, and they begin to smile socially in the first couple of months. Laughter usually comes a little later. For many babies, the first real laughs arrive at around three or four months old. Parents often remember them vividly, because they're one of the first signs of a shared joke between the baby and the world.

@study A19 bedroom_night | Alan Sroufe and Jane Piccard Wunsch | Child Development, 1972 | The development of laughter in the first year of life
One of the classic studies of baby laughter was published in 1972 by the psychologists Alan Sroufe and Jane Piccard Wunsch. They asked mothers to try a series of simple activities with their babies at home, month by month through the first year of life, and recorded which ones made the babies laugh. The activities were the ordinary games parents play anyway.

@list A20 cabin_fireplace | Some of the things they tried | Kissing the baby's tummy ;; Bouncing and lifting ;; Funny noises ;; Peekaboo ;; Pulling a cloth from the face ;; Walking like a penguin
They included kissing the baby's tummy, bouncing and lifting, making funny noises, playing peekaboo, covering the mother's face with a cloth and letting the baby pull it off, and even the mother walking across the room like a penguin. All of them were the kind of gentle silliness that has probably been part of family life for as long as there have been families.

@char A10 rooftop_dusk | What changed with age | From touch to surprise
A pattern appeared as the months passed. The youngest babies laughed mostly at strong touch and sound, like tummy kisses and funny noises. As they grew, through the second half of the first year, they began laughing more at things they could see and understand, like the cloth game, peekaboo and the penguin walk, things that depended on noticing that something was surprising or out of place.

@char N01 attic_rain | The baby who gets the joke | Knowing what's normal
That shift makes sense. To find a penguin walk funny, you need to know how your mother normally walks. To enjoy peekaboo, you need to expect a face to still be there when it's hidden. As babies build up their picture of how the world normally works, they gain the ability to laugh when it suddenly doesn't. Understanding and laughter grow together.

@char N07 cafe_sunset | Peekaboo | The oldest joke
Peekaboo might be the oldest joke in the world. It's played by families in many cultures, in many languages, and it seems to follow the same simple shape. A face disappears. A little tension builds. And then, with a burst of delight, the face comes back. Researchers have pointed out that this rhythm, a build-up and then a safe surprise, appears in a lot of adult humour too.

Rooms like this are where human laughter begins, long before words, in games of hiding and finding, tickles and tummy kisses, and faces pulled just to see a baby grin.

@char N09 rooftop_dusk | Laughing with, not at | Babies laugh in company
Like adults, babies laugh mostly with other people, usually during play with someone they know well. A stranger's funny face may get a nervous stare rather than a laugh. A parent's funny face, in the same situation, often gets a giggle. From the very start, it seems, laughter depends on feeling safe with the person who's making it happen.

@char N29 cabin_fireplace | A baby's laugh is catching | Why parents love it
And a baby's laugh is one of the most catching sounds there is. Very few adults can hear a small child giggling without at least smiling. It's easy to see why that might matter. A baby who laughs invites more play, more attention and more closeness. And the adult who laughs back is building a bond that will matter for years.

@study N20 rooftop_dusk | Caspar Addyman and Ishbel Addyman | The Baby Laughter project, from 2013 | An online survey of what makes babies laugh
More recently, the psychologist Caspar Addyman, whose preschool cartoon study we met earlier, ran an online survey called the Baby Laughter project, in which parents from many countries described what made their babies laugh. Peekaboo, tickling, funny faces and silly noises were among the most common answers. Very few of those babies, as you'd expect, were laughing at puns.

@char A04 bedroom_night | A careful note | Every baby is different
A careful note, though. Every baby is different. Some laugh early and often, some later and more rarely, and the range of what's normal is wide. Surveys and small studies show broad patterns, not deadlines. If any parent ever feels worried about how their baby is developing, the right place to take that worry is to a doctor or health visitor, not to a video.

@list A03 cafe_sunset | What baby laughter tells us | Laughter arrives in the first months ;; It starts with touch and sound ;; Then grows with understanding ;; It needs safety and company
So what does baby laughter tell us? That laughter arrives early, within the first months of life. That it starts with touch and sound, and grows more complex as understanding grows. And that it depends, from the very beginning, on feeling safe and being with someone. A baby's laugh is a social sound from day one.

@char A05 living_room_day | Nadia's nephew | The sneeze
Nadia remembers her nephew's first laugh again, at her sister's sneeze. It was so unexpected. A big, loud achoo, and a tiny face that crumpled up, not into tears, but into a delighted squeal. Everyone in the room had laughed with him. Looking back, she realises that sneeze had everything: a surprise, a safe person and a room full of people ready to join in.

@char A13 bedroom_night | Growing up | The jokes change
As children grow, the things that make them laugh keep changing. Toddlers love slapstick and silly words. Older children discover riddles, puns and jokes with rules. Teenagers develop sharper, more cutting humour, often shared with friends. And adults, like Nadia's table tonight, can laugh until they cry at a teapot lid, if the company is right.

But that brings us to a puzzle that philosophers have argued about for more than two thousand years. When we do laugh at something funny, a joke, an accident, an absurd moment, what exactly is it about that thing that makes it funny? Why a teapot lid in a sugar bowl, and not a spoon on a plate?
