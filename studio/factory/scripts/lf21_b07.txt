@chapter A12 cafe_sunset | Who you think you are
Let's start this chapter with a small thought experiment. Two people are trying to stop smoking. Someone offers them a cigarette. The first says, no thanks, I'm trying to quit. The second says, no thanks, I don't smoke. It's the same refusal. But listen to the difference. One is describing a struggle. The other is describing a person.

@char A10 living_room_day | A small difference | Doing versus being
That difference, between something you're doing and something you are, turns out to be surprisingly powerful. And to see why, we need to go back to an idea from the early 1970s about how we come to know ourselves at all.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Daryl Bem | Advances in Experimental Social Psychology, 1972 | Self-perception theory
In 1972, the psychologist Daryl Bem proposed what he called self-perception theory. We usually assume that we act the way we do because of who we are. Bem suggested that it often works the other way round too. We watch our own behaviour, much as an outsider would, and work out who we are from what we do.

@char A13 rooftop_dusk | Evidence about yourself | Every action is a vote
If you notice that you keep going for walks, you start to think of yourself as someone who walks. If you keep helping a neighbour, you come to see yourself as a helpful person. Each action becomes a small piece of evidence in the ongoing case of who you are.

@char A03 cafe_sunset | It runs both ways | Identity guides action too
And once you see yourself a certain way, that view starts guiding what you do next. Actions shape identity, and identity shapes actions. It's a loop. Which raises a tempting question. What if you could nudge someone into a particular identity, just with words?

@chapter A15 autumn_overlook | Be a voter
That's exactly what a group of researchers at Stanford tried, during real elections in the United States.

@study A04 attic_rain | Bryan, Walton, Rogers & Dweck | PNAS, 2011 | Motivating voter turnout by invoking the self
In 2011, Christopher Bryan and his colleagues published a set of experiments run around elections in 2008 and 2009. Eligible voters filled out a short survey the day before the election. For some, the questions used a verb. How important is it to you to vote tomorrow? For others, the questions used a noun. How important is it to you to be a voter tomorrow?

@char A12 living_room_day | One word | The noun group voted more
That was the only difference. One word. Afterwards, the researchers checked official voting records. In their studies, people who got the noun version, be a voter, turned out to vote at higher rates than those who got the verb version. Being a voter, it seemed, felt like a statement about who you are, and people wanted to live up to it.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Bryan, Adams & Monin | Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, 2013 | When cheating would make you a cheater: implicating the self prevents unethical behavior
The same idea seemed to work in the other direction. In 2013, Christopher Bryan, Gabrielle Adams and Benoit Monin asked people to report the results of coin flips, with a reward for certain outcomes, so they had a private chance to cheat. Some instructions said, please don't cheat. Others said, please don't be a cheater. When cheating was framed as becoming a cheater, much less cheating showed up in the results.

@char A04 attic_rain | A note of caution | Not every replication agreed
Now, an honest note. When a larger team led by Alan Gerber tested the voter wording in big field experiments, published in 2016, they didn't find the same boost in turnout. So the noun effect may be smaller, or more fragile, than the first studies suggested. It's an intriguing idea, not a magic word.

@char A13 cabin_fireplace | Still a useful lens | The kind of person who
But the underlying idea remains useful. When you're trying to change, it can help to think less about the outcome and more about the person. Not, I want to read twenty books this year, but, I'm becoming someone who reads. Then every small action, even ten pages before bed, is a quiet vote for that person.

@chapter A10 attic_rain | Why change can feel threatening
There's a flip side to all this, and it helps explain why change can be so hard even when we want it. Our identities don't just guide us. We also defend them.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Swann, Pelham & Krull | Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 1989 | Agreeable fancy or disagreeable truth? Reconciling self-enhancement and self-verification
The psychologist William Swann developed what he called self-verification theory. His research suggested that people often seek out feedback that confirms how they already see themselves, even when that view is negative. In one line of studies, people with negative views of themselves sometimes preferred to interact with someone who saw them the same way, rather than someone who saw them more positively.

@char A03 cafe_sunset | Predictable feels safe | Even when it hurts
Why would anyone do that? Swann's answer was that a stable self-view makes the world feel predictable. If people treat you the way you expect, life makes sense. So even a painful self-image can feel safer than an unfamiliar one. That's worth knowing, because it means part of the resistance to change is a deep wish for the world to stay understandable.

@char A10 living_room_day | Be gentle | Let the new self grow slowly
This might be why sudden, dramatic reinventions so often fall apart. They ask us to swap a familiar identity for a strange one overnight. Small, steady changes give the new self-image time to become familiar, until one day it feels less like a costume and more like you.

@chapter A12 cafe_sunset | Possible selves
So far we've looked at who you are now. But psychologists have also studied how we think about who we might become.

@study A04 attic_rain | Markus & Nurius | American Psychologist, 1986 | Possible selves
In 1986, Hazel Markus and Paula Nurius introduced the idea of possible selves. These are the versions of ourselves we imagine in the future. The selves we hope to become, the selves we expect to become, and the selves we're afraid of becoming. They argued that these imagined selves are powerful motivators, because they give our goals a face.

@char A13 rooftop_dusk | A vivid future you | Giving goals a face
Daphna Oyserman and her colleagues later built school programmes around this idea, helping young teenagers picture realistic future selves, like the student who does their homework, and connect them to everyday steps. In a study published in 2006, they reported that the programme was linked with better grades and attendance. A future self, it seems, works best when it comes with a path.

@chapter A15 cabin_fireplace | The trouble with dreaming
And that brings us to an important twist. You might expect that the more positively you imagine your future self, the more motivated you'd be. One researcher has spent decades showing that it isn't that simple.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Oettingen & Mayer | Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 2002 | The motivating function of thinking about the future: expectations versus fantasies
In 2002, Gabriele Oettingen and Doris Mayer published studies comparing two ways of thinking about the future. Expectations, which are judgments about how likely something is based on past experience, and fantasies, which are free, positive daydreams. Across their studies, confident expectations predicted more effort and success. But very positive fantasies often predicted less.

@char A10 attic_rain | Why dreams can deflate | The mind tastes it early
One possible reason is that a vivid, effortless daydream lets the mind enjoy the goal before you've done anything to reach it. You get a little of the reward in advance, and some of the drive to act quietly drains away.

@list A12 living_room_day | Mental contrasting, step by step | Wish: name something you want ;; Outcome: imagine the best result ;; Obstacle: picture what inside you gets in the way ;; Plan: if that obstacle appears, then I will...
Oettingen's answer is a technique she calls mental contrasting, later packaged into a simple routine known as WOOP. Wish, outcome, obstacle, plan. You let yourself dream, and then you gently picture the obstacle, often something inside you, like tiredness or worry. Then you make an if-then plan for meeting it. In her research, this combination has helped people act on their goals more than dreaming alone.

@photo lf21_b07_01.jpg | Dream, then look at the road
It's a lovely balance. Dream, yes, because the dream tells you where you want to go. But then look honestly at the road, including the bumps you know are there, and decide in advance how you'll step over them.
