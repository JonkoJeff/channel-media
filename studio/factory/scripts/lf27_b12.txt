@chapter A12 living_room_day | Helping online
So far, our experiments have taken place on pavements, in corridors and in rooms. But a great deal of modern life, including a great deal of asking for help, happens on screens. A person writes a message in a chat room, a forum or a group, and many people read it. Does the bystander effect follow us online?

@char A04 attic_rain | The first test | A chat room
In the late 1990s, as chat rooms became popular, a psychologist named Patrick Markey decided to find out. He watched what happened when someone posted a request for help in public chat rooms, and measured how long it took for someone to respond.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Markey | Computers in Human Behavior, 2000 | Bystander intervention in computer-mediated communication
Markey analysed a large number of requests for help posted in internet chat groups. He found that people were slower to respond to requests in larger groups. Requests that were addressed to a specific person, by name, got answers faster than requests that were addressed to everyone. The same principle we saw on the pavement, diffusion of responsibility, appeared to operate online.

@list A03 living_room_day | The findings in brief | Larger chat groups produced slower responses ;; Naming a specific person speeded up the reply ;; The request was often answered eventually ;; The effect was small but consistent
The finding about names is particularly practical. If you want help online, it helps to ask a particular person. Writing, can anyone help, invites everyone to assume that someone else will. Writing, Sam, could you help with this, changes the calculation.

@chapter A13 rooftop_dusk | Experiments with virtual presence
Other researchers designed experiments to test the same thing in a controlled way. They asked students to take part in an online task, and arranged for a request for help to appear while they were working. Some believed they were alone online. Others believed that several other people were also present.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Blair, Thompson & Wuensch | Basic and Applied Social Psychology, 2005 | Electronic helping behavior: the virtual presence of others makes a difference
Cheryl Blair and her colleagues found that people who believed others were present online were less likely to help someone who sent a request for assistance, compared with people who believed they were the only recipient. The pattern echoed Darley and Latané's original study, with a change of setting but not of principle.

@char A12 living_room_day | The lesson | The crowd can be invisible
What these studies show is that physical presence isn't necessary. The mere belief that others could respond is enough to weaken our sense of personal duty. A message sent to a large group, a post read by thousands, a comment section under a distressing video: each is a crowd, and each carries the risk of diffusion.

@list A12 living_room_day | Features of online spaces that matter | Many potential helpers, mostly invisible ;; It is unclear who has already responded ;; Anonymity can reduce felt responsibility ;; Distance makes the need feel less real
These features can work against helping. But the online world also has features that can encourage it. It can bring together people with rare experiences, give help at any hour, and allow support that a person in a small community might never find.

@chapter A06 cafe_sunset | The upside
Many people find genuine help online. Support groups for rare illnesses, forums for people grieving, communities for people with similar identities can offer exactly the kind of peer support we discussed in other videos. Research on online support groups has often found benefits, although the quality of studies varies.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Eysenbach, Powell, Englesakis, Rizo & Stern | BMJ, 2004 | Health related virtual communities and electronic support groups: systematic review of the effects of online peer to peer interactions
Gunther Eysenbach and colleagues reviewed studies of online peer support groups. They found that the evidence on health outcomes was limited and mixed, and that the studies were of variable quality. They concluded that more rigorous research was needed. It's a good reminder that online help is promising, but not yet well measured.

@char A01 cafe_sunset | A balanced view | Real help, real limits
Online spaces can be warm, and they can also be cold. They can hold a person who has nowhere else to turn, and they can leave a person unanswered. The research suggests that how well they work depends on design, on moderation and on the habits of the people in them.

@list A03 living_room_day | Words to keep from this chapter | Computer-mediated communication ;; Virtual presence ;; Online peer support ;; Moderation
Four terms from this chapter. Computer-mediated communication is communication through screens. Virtual presence is the awareness that others are present online. Online peer support is help from people who share an experience. And moderation is the work of keeping a space safe and respectful.

@list A12 living_room_day | Helping well online | Reply to a request even if others might ;; Use the person's name ;; Say what you can offer, however small ;; Point to real help if the situation is serious
Here is how to help well online. Reply to a request even if others might. Use the person's name. Say what you can offer, however small. And point to real help if the situation is serious, such as a crisis line or emergency service. Picture this scene: someone posts in a group that they are having a terrible night and feel alone. A dozen people read it. One person writes, I'm here, Alex. Do you want to talk, or would you rather I just stay? Two others follow within minutes. The first reply broke the silence. Without it, the post might have sat there, read by many and answered by none.

@list A12 living_room_day | Pause and reflect | Do I answer requests for help online? ;; What makes me hesitate? ;; Whom could I name when I ask? ;; What would a kind first reply look like?
Here is a pause to think about your online life. When someone asks for help in a group you belong to, do you answer? Or do you read, feel sympathy, and scroll on, assuming that someone else will reply? Most of us do this more than we would like, and the research suggests it is not unusual. Notice what makes you hesitate. Is it worry about saying the wrong thing? Is it the sense that the person has plenty of others to answer? Then think about how you ask for help yourself. Could you name a particular person, or say what you need? And imagine a kind first reply, short and warm, something like, I'm here, tell me more. A first reply costs almost nothing, but it changes what the thread feels like to everyone who reads it afterwards. Someone has to go first, and it can be you.

A screen glowing in a dark room. Behind it, somewhere, a message is waiting to be answered. The research on online helping suggests that the most useful thing is often the first reply. The internet did not invent the bystander effect, and it does not remove our ability to help. It simply changes where the crowd stands. One clear, named, warm reply can still change what happens next.

@char A15 cabin_fireplace | A gentle thought | Be the first to answer
If you ever see a request for help sitting unanswered, remember that others may be waiting for someone else to act. Being the first to reply is a small gift, and it often makes it easier for others to join in.

@char A08 cabin_fireplace | Looking back | What online helping taught us
So what did we learn? That larger online groups produce slower responses. That naming a person speeds things up. That the belief that others are present can reduce helping. And that online support offers real promise, though the evidence is still limited.
