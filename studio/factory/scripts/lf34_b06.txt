@chapter A13 attic_rain | Memories in bold ink
Think back for a moment to a day that shook you. Perhaps a fright, a piece of bad news, a near miss on the road. Chances are you can still remember small details. Where you were standing. What the light was like. Ordinary days fade into a blur, but frightening ones seem to be written in bold ink.

@char A10 cabin_fireplace | Not an accident | The alarm and memory
That isn't an accident. The same alarm system that speeds up your heart also reaches into the parts of the brain that store memories. And in the 1990s, a group of researchers in California found an elegant way to show it.

@study A18 living_room_day | Larry Cahill, Bruce Prins, Michael Weber and James McGaugh | University of California, Irvine, 1994 | Beta-adrenergic activation and memory for emotional events
In 1994, Larry Cahill, Bruce Prins, Michael Weber and James McGaugh published a study in the journal Nature. Volunteers watched a short slideshow with a story. For some, the story was an ordinary one. For others, the middle of the story was emotional and upsetting, about a boy badly hurt in an accident and rushed to hospital.

@char A19 cafe_sunset | The twist | A pill before the story
Before the slideshow, the volunteers took a pill. Some got a harmless placebo. Others got a drug called propranolol, which blocks some of the effects of adrenaline and similar chemicals in the body and brain. Nobody knew which pill they'd been given.

@char A13 living_room_day | A week later | The memory test
About a week later, everyone came back for a surprise memory test. In the placebo group, people who had heard the emotional story remembered its upsetting middle part especially well, better than the people who'd heard the plain version. That's the bold ink effect.

@list A18 living_room_day | Four groups | Placebo, ordinary story ;; Placebo, emotional story ;; Propranolol, ordinary story ;; Propranolol, emotional story
So in the end there were four kinds of volunteer. Some had a placebo and heard the ordinary story. Some had a placebo and heard the emotional one. Some had propranolol and the ordinary story, and some had propranolol and the emotional story. The question was which of those groups would show the bold ink effect, and which would not.

@char A03 cabin_fireplace | The blocked boost | Same story, plain memory
But in the propranolol group, that extra boost was gone. They remembered the emotional story about as well as the ordinary one. Block the chemical signals of the alarm, and the bold ink fades to normal. It was strong evidence that the stress system itself helps stamp emotional moments into memory.

@char A12 rooftop_dusk | Why it helps | Remember the danger
From the body's point of view, this makes sense. If something frightened you, it's worth remembering where it happened and how, so that you can avoid it next time. A creature that forgot every close call wouldn't last very long.

@chapter A05 autumn_overlook | Flashbulb memories
Some memories feel so vivid that people describe them as photographs. As if, at the moment of hearing the news, a camera flash went off in the mind and captured the whole scene. Psychologists gave this feeling a name.

@study A03 living_room_day | Roger Brown and James Kulik | Harvard University, 1977 | Flashbulb memories
In 1977, Roger Brown and James Kulik, at Harvard, wrote about what they called flashbulb memories. They asked people about shocking public events, such as the assassination of President Kennedy in 1963. Many could describe, in rich detail, exactly where they were and what they were doing when they heard. The memories felt sharp, permanent, and certain.

@char A10 cafe_sunset | But are they true | Vivid is not the same as accurate
But feeling certain about a memory is not the same as the memory being correct. And a later study, run in the most unusual circumstances, put that difference under a microscope.

@study A19 living_room_day | Jennifer Talarico and David Rubin | Duke University, 2003 | Confidence, not consistency, characterizes flashbulb memories
On the 12th of September 2001, the day after the attacks in the United States, Jennifer Talarico and David Rubin at Duke University asked 54 students to write down how they had heard the news. They also asked each student to describe an ordinary event from the days before. Then they tested them again, weeks and months later.

@stat A18 cafe_sunset | Students tested | 54 | wrote down their memories the day after
Here's what they found. Over time, the flashbulb memories changed just about as much as the everyday memories did. Details drifted, swapped and disappeared. But there was one big difference. The students stayed far more confident about the flashbulb memories, and rated them as more vivid, even as they quietly changed.

@quote A03 living_room_day | Confidence, not consistency | Talarico and Rubin, 2003
The title of their paper says it beautifully. Confidence, not consistency. Stress and emotion make a memory feel vivid and certain. They don't seem to make it any more reliable. The bold ink can still be written over.

So Jonah will probably remember this morning for a long time. But the version he remembers in ten years may not be exactly what happened. The train might become later in his memory, the platform more crowded, the talk more dramatic. That's not lying. It's simply how memory works.

@chapter A15 autumn_overlook | Stress and remembering
So stress can help us store emotional moments. But what about getting memories back out again? That's the question that matters most to Jonah right now, standing at the front of a room and needing to find his words.

@study A19 cafe_sunset | Sabrina Kuhlmann, Marcel Piel and Oliver Wolf | Germany, 2005 | Impaired memory retrieval after psychosocial stress in healthy young men
In 2005, Sabrina Kuhlmann, Marcel Piel and Oliver Wolf, in Germany, asked young men to learn a list of words. The next day, before testing their memory, half of them went through the Trier Social Stress Test, the speech and the counting backwards we met earlier. The other half did a calm, similar task without the pressure.

@char A10 living_room_day | The result | Harder to reach
The stressed group recalled fewer of the words they had learned the day before, and the effect was clearest for emotional words. The memories were still there. They had been stored perfectly well. They were simply harder to reach in a stressful moment.

@char A12 cafe_sunset | The tip of the tongue | It comes back later
You've probably felt this yourself. In an exam, or an interview, a fact you know perfectly well refuses to come. And then, an hour later, on the walk home, it pops into your head without effort. The memory was never lost. The stress was standing in the doorway.

@char A19 living_room_day | Not a bad memory | A busy one
This matters, because people under stress often conclude that their memory is simply bad. That they're getting forgetful, or that they never really learned the thing in the first place. But very often, the learning was fine. The library is full. It's just that the librarian is busy dealing with an alarm.

@chapter A03 cabin_fireplace | Running on autopilot
There's one more thing stress does to memory, and it's a little sneaky. It changes which kind of memory takes charge of what we do. To see it, a pair of researchers used chocolate milk and orange juice.

@study A18 living_room_day | Lars Schwabe and Oliver Wolf | Germany, 2009 | Stress prompts habit behavior in humans
In 2009, Lars Schwabe and Oliver Wolf trained volunteers to press different keys to win small sips of two drinks, chocolate milk and orange juice. Then people ate as much of one of those foods as they wanted, until they really didn't want any more of it. Normally, people then stop working for the food they're full of. That's goal directed behaviour.

@char A13 cafe_sunset | Under stress | The old routine
But volunteers who had been stressed beforehand kept pressing the key for the food they no longer wanted. They carried on with the trained routine, even though it no longer matched what they wanted. Stress had tipped them from thinking about the goal to simply running the habit.

@list A19 cabin_fireplace | Stress and memory | Emotional moments are stored more strongly ;; Vivid memories feel certain, but still change ;; Remembering in the moment gets harder ;; Habits take over from goals
So here's what stress does to memory, in short. It helps stamp emotional moments in more strongly. It makes some memories feel vivid and certain, even when they've changed. It can make it harder to recall things in the moment. And it nudges us towards habit, and away from careful, goal directed choices.

@char A05 living_room_day | A kind word | For your autopilot
That last one might explain a few things. Why, on a stressful day, you end up taking your usual route home, even though you meant to stop at the shop. Why you snack without deciding to. It isn't weakness. It's a tired brain handing the wheel to autopilot.

@chapter A20 cafe_sunset | The feeling of control
But you might have noticed that one word keeps coming back in these studies. Uncontrollable. Stress hits hardest when we feel we can't do anything about it. So what happens if you give people even a tiny sense of control? The answer comes from an experiment with a button that nobody pressed.
