@chapter A13 rooftop_dusk | Myth twelve: they probably didn't like me
You've just had a conversation with someone new. Maybe at a party, maybe at work. On the way home, your mind starts replaying it. Why did I say that? Did I talk too much? Did I seem boring? Did they actually like me, or were they just being polite? If you've ever done this, you're very far from alone.

@char A10 attic_rain | The replay | We judge ourselves harshly
Most of us are much harder on ourselves after a conversation than we are on the other person. We remember our awkward moments in vivid detail, and we assume the other person noticed them too. A group of researchers decided to find out how accurate these worries actually are.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Boothby, Cooney, Sandstrom & Clark | Psychological Science, 2018 | The liking gap in conversations: do people like us more than we think?
In 2018, Erica Boothby, Gus Cooney, Gillian Sandstrom and Margaret Clark paired up strangers and asked them to have a short conversation. Afterwards, each person rated how much they liked their partner, and how much they thought their partner liked them.

@char A12 living_room_day | The gap | They liked us more than we thought
The result was consistent. People liked their conversation partners more than they thought their partners liked them. Each person tended to underestimate how much they were liked. The researchers called it the liking gap.

@char A03 cafe_sunset | Everyone does it | Both sides at once
Think about what that means. In a conversation where both people are quietly worrying, both are also quietly enjoying the other person more than either realises. Two people walk away, each thinking the other was a bit lukewarm about them, when in fact they both liked each other.

@char A13 rooftop_dusk | Why it happens | Too busy watching ourselves
The researchers suggested that we're so focused on our own performance, what we said, how we came across, that we miss the signals of warmth the other person is sending. They also found the gap held across different situations, from short lab chats to longer conversations.

@study A04 attic_rain | The roommate study | Psychological Science, 2018 | First-year university roommates followed over several months
In one part of the research, they followed first-year university students living with roommates they hadn't known before. The liking gap was there at the start. And it lasted for months. Only slowly, over the course of the year, did it begin to shrink. Even people who saw each other every day kept underestimating how much they were liked.

@char A10 cabin_fireplace | Something to remember | You were probably liked
So here's something worth remembering the next time you're replaying a conversation on the way home. The other person probably liked you more than you think. Not because of any trick, but because people are generally kinder in their judgements of us than we are of ourselves.

@chapter A15 cafe_sunset | Talking to strangers
That gap may also explain why many of us avoid talking to strangers at all. We expect it to be awkward, unwelcome or unpleasant. But are we right?

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Epley & Schroeder | Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, 2014 | Mistakenly seeking solitude
In 2014, Nicholas Epley and Juliana Schroeder approached commuters on trains and buses in Chicago. Some were asked to strike up a conversation with a stranger near them. Others were asked to keep to themselves, as most commuters do. A third group simply commuted as usual.

@char A12 living_room_day | The finding | Talking made commutes better
At the end of the journey, the people who had talked to a stranger reported a more positive commute than those who had sat in silence. Yet when other commuters were asked to predict what would happen, most expected the opposite. They thought talking to a stranger would make their journey worse.

@char A03 cafe_sunset | More willing than we think | Strangers were happy to chat
The commuters also tended to assume that other people wouldn't want to talk to them. But in the studies, the strangers were generally happy to chat. The fear of rejection was much greater than the reality. Many of us, it seems, are sitting next to someone who would quite like to talk, while we both stare at our phones.

@chapter A12 cafe_sunset | Small moments with strangers
And it doesn't even need to be a full conversation. Even very small moments of connection seem to matter.

@study A04 attic_rain | Sandstrom & Dunn | Social Psychological and Personality Science, 2014 | Is efficiency overrated? Minimal social interactions lead to belonging and positive affect
In 2014, Gillian Sandstrom and Elizabeth Dunn asked people buying coffee at a busy café to either be as efficient as possible, or to have a genuine interaction with the barista, smiling, making eye contact and having a brief chat. The people who had a genuine moment of connection left in a better mood and felt a greater sense of belonging.

@char A13 rooftop_dusk | Weak ties | Acquaintances matter too
In another study, the same researchers found that on days when people had more interactions with acquaintances, people they knew only a little, like classmates or neighbours, they tended to feel happier and more connected. Our close relationships matter enormously. But so do the many small threads that tie us to the people around us.

@photo lf22_b12_01.jpg | A brief hello
The nod to a neighbour. A chat with the person at the bus stop. A smile with the barista. These moments seem tiny. But they may be part of what makes us feel we belong somewhere, that we're part of a community rather than alone in a crowd.

@char A10 attic_rain | The same pattern | We underestimate others
Notice the pattern running through this whole chapter. We underestimate how much people like us. We underestimate how much they'd enjoy talking. We underestimate how much a small moment of connection might mean. Again and again, other people turn out to be warmer and more open than we expect.

@list A12 living_room_day | What the research suggests | People like us more than we think ;; The liking gap can last for months ;; Strangers are usually happy to talk ;; Small moments of connection lift our mood ;; Acquaintances matter too
People like us more than we think. That gap can last for months. Strangers are usually happy to talk. Small moments lift our mood. And even acquaintances add to our sense of belonging. It's a remarkably hopeful set of findings.

@char A13 rooftop_dusk | Why it's hopeful | The world is warmer than it feels
It's easy to feel that the world is cold and that people are closed off, especially in a busy city where everyone seems absorbed in their own lives. But these studies suggest that much of that coldness is a shared illusion. Behind the headphones and the busy faces, many people are quietly open to a kind word. They're just waiting, like us, for someone else to go first.

@char A03 cafe_sunset | A small experiment | Try it once
Perhaps it's worth a small experiment of your own. Some time this week, say a few more words to someone you'd normally just nod to. Or after a conversation, when your mind starts replaying it, remind yourself of the liking gap. You were probably liked more than you know.
