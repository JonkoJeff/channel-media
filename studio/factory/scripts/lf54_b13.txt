A ticket inspector comes through the carriage and stands beside Oskar's seat while he searches his pockets. He feels a yawn rising and, without quite deciding to, he swallows it. The moment she moves on, it comes back and takes over his whole face. He has behaved like this all his life, and it turns out he's very typical.

@char N29 street_day | The polite swallow | Manners meet reflex
We learn early that yawning at someone can look rude. So the presence of another person, especially one we don't know well or want to impress, puts a social brake on an automatic movement. The interesting question for researchers is how sensitive that brake is. Does it need a real human being, or will a picture of one do?

@char N14 attic_rain | A problem for the lab | The scientist in the room
This matters for the science itself. If being watched reduces yawning, then every laboratory study with a researcher in the room, or a camera pointed at the volunteer, may be measuring a smaller response than would happen in private. It's an awkward thought: the act of observing a behaviour can change the behaviour you came to observe.

@study A12 street_day | Andrew Gallup, Daniil Vasilyev, Nicola Anderson and Alan Kingstone | Scientific Reports, published 2019 | Yawning in virtual reality
In 2019, Andrew Gallup, Alan Kingstone and colleagues, with work done at the University of British Columbia, published a study using virtual reality. Volunteers wore a headset and found themselves in a simulated environment, where they watched videos of people yawning. The researchers could then add different kinds of watchers, virtual or real, and see what happened to the yawns.

@stat N22 attic_rain | Contagious yawning inside the headset | 38% | in line with typical real-life figures
Inside the headset, about thirty-eight percent of people caught yawns, which the team described as in line with the usual real-world range. So the basic effect survived the move into a simulated world. A yawn on a virtual screen, seen from a virtual room, could still reach the real jaw of the person wearing the headset.

@char N14 bedroom_night | A virtual watcher | Barely noticed
In a virtual reality study of contagious yawning, researchers added a witness inside the virtual world: a human figure, or a camera that appeared to be watching. You might expect that to make people more self-conscious. It had little effect on their yawning. The volunteers knew, at some level, that nothing there could really judge them, and the old movement carried on much as before.

@char N27 living_room_day | A real person | The brake comes on
What did make a difference was an actual researcher present in the physical room, outside the headset, where the volunteer couldn't even see them. With a real person there, yawning went down. Simply knowing that someone was nearby seemed to be enough to hold people back, even though their eyes were filled entirely with the virtual scene.

A virtual reality headset resting on a table beside an empty chair. The lesson of the study sits in that chair. Whether somebody is really sitting there changed behaviour more than any figure drawn inside the headset. Our bodies seem to keep a quiet account of real company, even when our eyes are busy with something else.

@char N29 cafe_sunset | What it suggests | Two systems at once
This suggests two things going on together. One part of you picks up a yawn and prepares to copy it, and it isn't fussy about whether the source is real. Another part monitors who might be watching, and it's much fussier. The result you see on someone's face is the outcome of both, the pull to join in and the pressure to look attentive.

@list A12 cabin_fireplace | Things that may change whether you catch a yawn | Your own general tendency ;; Your age ;; Whether you noticed it ;; Who yawned ;; Who might be watching you
Look how long the list has become. Whether a particular yawn reaches you may depend on your own steady tendency, your age, whether you noticed it, how close you are to the person, and whether anyone real is watching you. And all of that is before we count tiredness, temperature and plain chance.

@char N09 bedroom_night | Why headlines shrink it | One neat answer
You can see why headlines prefer a single answer. Yawns spread because of empathy is short and sweet. Yawns spread through a mix of perception, a variable movement system, attention, social closeness and self-consciousness, in proportions still under study, won't fit on a banner. It is, however, a much better description of what the research shows.

@char N10 attic_rain | Public and private | The yawn you save
It also explains an everyday habit. Many of us save our biggest yawns for when we're alone, in the car, in the bathroom, at the kitchen sink. In public we offer smaller, folded versions. So if you've ever thought that you hardly yawn in company and enormously at home, you may not be imagining it.

@char N27 rooftop_dusk | Yawning as a message | Still an open idea
And this brings back the signalling idea. A behaviour that we adjust according to who's watching is, whatever its origin, part of social life. It can be read, sometimes misread, and politely managed. Whether yawning evolved in order to send messages, or merely came to carry them, is a question researchers are still debating.

@char N29 autumn_overlook | To find older roots | Look to other animals
One way to approach that question is to look at animals who have no manners to worry about, or at least none like ours. If contagious yawning turns up in other social species, and if it follows friendships or family lines there too, that tells us something about how deep the roots might go. The idea can be tested in places with no ticket inspectors.

@list N20 bedroom_night | Where we are | Contagious yawning survived in virtual reality ;; A virtual watcher made little difference ;; A real person in the room reduced yawning ;; Social setting shapes what we show
In the virtual reality study, then, people still caught yawns from videos in a headset. A simulated watcher barely changed that. A real person in the room held yawning down. The setting we're in shapes how much of the urge ever reaches the surface, which is worth remembering when we compare one study with another.
