@chapter A10 cafe_sunset | The tin on the shelf
It's four o'clock on a Thursday, and Ines is at her desk in the new office. Somewhere behind her, on a shelf by the window, there's a tin of biscuits that someone left for the whole team. Ines hasn't decided anything. She hasn't even looked up. But her chair has turned, her feet have carried her across the room, and her hand is already resting on the lid of the tin.

@char A10 attic_rain | A quiet surprise | Not hungry at all
She lifts the lid, and then she pauses, because something feels a little odd. She had a big lunch today, with soup and bread, and she isn't hungry in the slightest. If anyone had asked her a minute ago, would you like a biscuit, she would honestly have said no thank you. And yet here she is, holding one, with a small, puzzled smile. It's the third day in a row this has happened. She tells herself it's only a biscuit, and it is. But she can't quite shake the feeling that someone else made that decision for her, somewhere just below the surface of her day.

@chapter A19 rooftop_dusk | Do we want it?
Now, let's go a little deeper, because Ines has stumbled onto a question that scientists have been asking for a very long time. When we do something again and again, are we doing it because we want what it gives us? Or has the action quietly come loose from the wanting, and now it simply happens, whether we want the result or not? There's a clever way to find out. Scientists have used it for decades, and it's gentler than it sounds.

@list A13 cafe_sunset | A test with three steps | Teach an action ;; Make the reward unwelcome ;; Watch what happens
Researchers call it outcome devaluation, which is a rather grand name for a simple idea. First, you teach an animal or a person to do something to earn a reward. Second, you make that reward unwelcome, perhaps by letting them have far too much of it. And third, you watch. If the action is driven by wanting the reward, it should fade. If it has become a habit, it may carry right on.

@study A12 cafe_sunset | Adams and Dickinson | Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology, published 1981 | Hungry rats pressed less for a food that had been made unpleasant
In the early nineteen eighties, psychologists Adams and Dickinson trained hungry rats to press a lever for a food reward. Then, away from the lever, they taught some of the rats to dislike that particular food. When the lever came back, those rats pressed it less than rats who had learned to dislike a different food. In other words, these rats were acting on what they knew about the reward. They weren't pressing because they'd always pressed. They were pressing because of what the food now meant to them.

@char A18 attic_rain | Acting with a goal | The rats were thinking ahead
That's what scientists call goal-directed behavior. The rats weren't just pressing out of reflex. Somewhere in their little minds, they were connecting the lever to the food, and the food to how they now felt about it. If the food was no longer welcome, there was no reason to press. It's a very sensible way to live, and it's how most of us begin when we learn something new. When Ines first learned the route to a new office, she looked at every corner, and thought about every turn.

@study A10 living_room_day | Adams | Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology, published 1982 | With extended training, rats were often less sensitive to the change
But then came a follow-up. In 1982, Adams ran more experiments in which the amount of training varied. With limited training, the rats again reduced their pressing once the food had been made unwelcome. But with extended training, rats often did not. The lever pressing carried on, even though the reward had lost its charm. The author suggested part of the reason might be repeated exposure to the reward itself.

@char A19 living_room_day | Not a flaw | A shift in who is steering
It's worth saying clearly that this isn't a story about foolish rats. Something sensible is going on. After a great deal of practice, an action can be handed over to a faster, simpler system, one that no longer stops to ask whether the reward is still wanted. It saves effort, and most of the time the old reward really is still good. The trouble only shows up when the world changes and the habit doesn't.

Picture that tin for a moment. Soft afternoon light on the window ledge. The lid sitting just a little crooked. Nothing about it is dramatic, and nothing about it is wrong. It's simply there, at the same place, at the same time of day, every afternoon. And that, as we'll see in a while, is exactly the kind of quiet arrangement that habits love to grow in. The same place, the same hour, the same little ritual, and a day that has no need to ask for anyone's opinion.

@chapter A15 cafe_sunset | And what about people?
Of course, rats are not people, and a fair question is whether humans do the same. In 2009, three researchers, Tricomi, Balleine and O'Doherty, decided to find out, using ordinary snack foods and a brain scanner. They wanted to see whether, with enough practice, a person could keep responding for a food they had just eaten until they had no wish for any more.

@study A03 cafe_sunset | Tricomi, Balleine and O'Doherty | European Journal of Neuroscience, published 2009 | After extensive training, people kept responding for a snack they had just eaten to fullness
Thirty-two adults learned to respond to cues on a screen to earn small food rewards, either chocolate candies or a salty corn snack. Half had a short amount of training, and half had six times as much, spread over three days. Afterward, everyone ate one of the two foods until it was no longer pleasant, and then, with no rewards on offer, they carried on responding while being scanned.

@stat A13 living_room_day | The volunteers | 32 | healthy adults took part
Here is the size of the study. Thirty-two healthy adults took part, split into two groups of sixteen. One person from the longer-training group was set aside as an outlier, leaving fifteen in that group. It's a modest number, as many careful laboratory experiments are, but the design was neat. Everyone did the same task, and the only big difference between the groups was how much practice they'd had.

@char A10 cafe_sunset | The result | Practice changed the answer
The people with short training behaved like the rats with short training. They responded less to the cue for the food they'd just eaten. But the people with extensive training kept responding to that cue at about the same rate as to the other one. They had eaten that food until it was no longer pleasant, and still their responding carried on. A little practice kept them goal-directed. A lot of practice made the action less dependent on the reward. Same people, same snack, same instructions. The difference was the amount of practice behind the action.

@curve A19 attic_rain | Practice and the reward | Amount of practice | How much the action ignores the reward | 0.8 | A habit takes over
Here is a gentle way to picture it. At the start, on the left, the action depends very much on wanting the reward, so a change in how much we want it changes what we do. As practice builds, that link loosens, little by little. Far along the curve, the action runs on its own, and the reward matters much less. It isn't a switch that flips overnight. It's a slow hand-over, like dusk turning into night.

@char A10 bedroom_night | A careful note | One lab, one task
Let's be careful about what this does and doesn't tell us. It was a small experiment with a simple key-pressing task, not a study of biscuit tins or kitchens. Nobody can say from it exactly what Ines is feeling at four o'clock. And it's one study among many, so we hold its conclusions lightly, the way you'd hold a warm cup. But it does show, in people, something that matches what researchers had seen in rats for years. With enough repetition, we can keep going for something we no longer want.

@list A20 cafe_sunset | What this gently suggests | Repetition can loosen the link to wanting ;; The action can outlast the appetite ;; This is normal brain work, not weakness
So if we put the pieces together, a few quiet ideas emerge. Repetition can loosen the link between an action and the wanting behind it. The action can then outlast the appetite. And all of this seems to be ordinary brain work, the same hand-over that lets us walk, type and drive without effort. It isn't a weakness of character. It's what happens to anything we've done many times in the same place. Ines can let that be true without needing to fix it tonight.

@char A03 living_room_day | Back to Ines | A small experiment of her own
Back at the shelf, Ines is still holding her biscuit. For once, she doesn't scold herself. She just notices, with a kind of mild curiosity, that her hand got there before her wishes did. She puts the biscuit back, closes the lid, and goes to make a cup of tea instead. Not as a rule, and not as a punishment. Just to see what happens when the walk to the shelf meets a different ending.

@quote A19 cabin_fireplace | The hand got there first. | A thought worth being kind about
There's nothing to be ashamed of in that sentence. The hand got there first. It does this for all of us, with the light switch in the old flat, with the phone in bed, with the biscuit tin in the afternoon. And once we can see it that way, as a path worn smooth by walking, rather than as a flaw, we're a little freer to ask a more useful question.
