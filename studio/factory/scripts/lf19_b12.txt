@chapter A12 living_room_day | Two minds, or one?
Our last claim is the biggest of all, and it needs to be handled with care. It's the belief that men and women have very different minds, that they think, talk, feel and reason in distinct ways, and that these differences are large. It's the premise of a thousand books and jokes. And it's one of the most studied questions in psychology.

@char A04 attic_rain | A careful start | What the research can and can't say
Before we begin, a note on method. Researchers compare groups using something called an effect size, which tells you how big a difference is, compared with the range of differences among individuals. A small effect means that the two groups overlap almost completely. A large effect means that they barely overlap. The question isn't whether any difference exists, but how big it is.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Hyde | American Psychologist, 2005 | The gender similarities hypothesis
In 2005, the psychologist Janet Hyde at the University of Wisconsin reviewed forty-six meta-analyses, which together summarised thousands of studies of psychological differences between males and females. These covered abilities like maths and verbal skills, communication, and aspects of personality and well-being. She asked how big the differences were, across the board.

@stat A14 conspiracy_board | Differences that were small or close to zero | About 78% | across 124 effect sizes in 46 meta-analyses
Her conclusion was that, for most of the psychological traits measured, males and females were more alike than different. About seventy-eight percent of the effect sizes that she examined were small or close to zero. She called this the gender similarities hypothesis, in contrast to the popular picture of two very different kinds of minds.

@list A03 living_room_day | What Hyde found | Most differences were small or negligible ;; A few were moderate or large ;; Some of the larger ones involved physical skills, such as throwing distance ;; Variation within each group was much bigger than between them
That final point is the one that's most often forgotten. There's more variation among men, and more among women, than there is between the two groups. A difference in averages tells you little about any one person.

@chapter A13 rooftop_dusk | Talking
Let's test one of the most popular stereotypes. Women, it's said, talk much more than men. A figure often quoted in popular books was that women speak twenty thousand words per day, and men only seven thousand. It sounded scientific, but it had no real source.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Mehl, Vazire, Ramirez-Esparza, Slatcher & Pennebaker | Science, 2007 | Are women really more talkative than men?
In 2007, Matthias Mehl at the University of Arizona and his team fitted a group of three hundred and ninety-six students with small recording devices that sampled their conversations throughout the day, for several days. They counted the words spoken. And they found that the two groups were almost identical in how much they talked.

@stat A06 cafe_sunset | Words per day | About 16,000 | women averaged 16,215 and men 15,669, a difference too small to be reliable
Women averaged around sixteen thousand words a day, and men around fifteen and a half thousand. The difference was small and could have been due to chance. The most extraordinary thing was the range. Some people spoke fewer than one thousand words per day, and some more than forty thousand, regardless of sex.

@char A10 attic_rain | The lesson | Individuals vary enormously
That finding is a good illustration of the whole topic. The variation between individuals was enormous, and the difference between men and women was tiny. If you wanted to predict how much a person would talk, knowing their sex would help you very little, and knowing the person would help you a lot.

@chapter A12 living_room_day | Where differences are found
It would be wrong to say that there are no differences at all. Researchers do find some. Men, on average, are taller and stronger, and score higher on tests of throwing and some kinds of spatial rotation. Women, on average, score higher on some measures of verbal fluency, and in personality studies, on traits like agreeableness. The question is how large they are, and how much they matter.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Del Giudice, Booth & Irwing | PLoS ONE, 2012 | The distance between Mars and Venus: measuring global sex differences in personality
Not everyone agrees with Hyde's view. In 2012, Marco Del Giudice and colleagues argued that when many personality traits are considered together, the differences add up to a larger overall gap than any single trait suggests. They found that the combined difference was substantial, and could be used to classify people with a fair degree of accuracy.

@char A04 attic_rain | An honest disagreement | Summing the traits
It's a good example of a real scientific argument. Hyde's approach looks at one trait at a time, and finds small gaps. Del Giudice's approach looks at the pattern across many traits at once, and finds a bigger separation. Both agree that the distributions overlap a lot, and that individuals vary widely. They disagree on how much emphasis the differences deserve.

@list A12 living_room_day | Where researchers agree | Most differences in single traits are small ;; The groups overlap a great deal ;; Individuals vary far more than the groups differ ;; Culture and circumstances change the size of differences
That final point is also well supported. Studies across countries have found that the size of differences in some measures varies from place to place, which suggests that experience and society matter, not only biology.

@char A01 cafe_sunset | The maths question | A changing gap
One example is mathematics. In the 1970s, boys scored higher on maths tests in many countries. Since then, the gap has shrunk in many places, and in some, it has disappeared. A 2010 analysis by Janet Hyde and Janet Mertz of international test data found that in countries with greater gender equality, the gap was smaller.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Hyde & Mertz | Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 2009 | Gender, culture, and mathematics performance
That suggests that the differences, where they exist, are not fixed. They respond to opportunities, expectations and encouragement. It's also a reminder that a difference in averages, seen at one time and in one place, may not be a permanent feature of human nature.

@char A19 cabin_fireplace | A kinder frame | See the person
There's a humane lesson here, too. If the groups overlap so much, then the sensible way to treat a person is as an individual. Their interests, strengths and quirks will tell you far more than their sex, and assumptions about what they can or can't do are likely to be wrong, and sometimes unfair.

@chapter A10 attic_rain | What twelve claims leave us
We've now looked at twelve popular claims about the mind. Habits, sugar, the moon, multitasking, sleep learning, brain cells, lie detection, brainstorming, power poses, willpower, birth order and the sexes. Some were false, some half true, and some turned out to be more interesting than the myth.

@list A12 living_room_day | Twelve claims, twelve verdicts | Habits: about two months on average, not twenty-one days ;; Sugar: no effect on children's behaviour in controlled studies ;; The moon: no reliable link with behaviour ;; Multitasking: mostly rapid switching, with a cost ;; Sleep learning: you can strengthen, not acquire ;; Brain cells: some adult neurogenesis is debated ;; Lie detection: barely better than chance ;; Brainstorming: solo thinking first works better ;; Power poses: feelings, not hormones ;; Willpower: not a simple tank ;; Birth order: tiny effects ;; Sex differences: mostly small, with overlap
That's the whole evening in twelve lines. It's a useful list, but the real value is in how each verdict was reached: with named studies, large samples and honest disagreement.

@char A01 cafe_sunset | The common thread | Plausible is not proven
If there's a thread running through all twelve, it's that a claim can be plausible, popular and repeated by experts, and still not be true. Each of these myths had a reasonable story, and some a published study behind them. What they were missing was a good test, or a replication, or both.

@char A04 attic_rain | A few questions to carry | A short checklist
So here are a few questions that you can carry into the next claim you hear. Who did the study? How many people took part? Was it repeated, and by whom? How big was the effect? And what would count as evidence against it? You don't need to ask them aloud. They're a quiet habit of mind.

@list A03 living_room_day | A pocket checklist | Who tested it, and how many people? ;; Has anyone repeated it? ;; How big is the effect? ;; What else could explain it? ;; Does it come from a story, or from a measurement?
Five questions, and none requires a degree. If you keep them in mind, you'll be less likely to believe a confident claim that sounds too good, or too tidy, to be true.

@char A15 cabin_fireplace | A gentle point | Being wrong is normal
It's worth saying, before we finish, that believing a myth is no sign of stupidity. Each of these ideas has been held by clever, careful people, including scientists. What matters is how we respond when the evidence changes. Being willing to say, I used to think that, and now I know better, is one of the most admirable habits there is.

@char A12 living_room_day | A note on science | Findings can change
And it applies to this video, too. Science moves, and some of what we've said may be refined by future studies. We've tried to flag where the evidence is mixed. If you ever find a newer result that changes the picture, trust it, and enjoy the fact that the story hasn't finished.

@char A12 living_room_day | A note on language | Averages and people
When we talk about differences between groups, it's easy to slip from averages into stereotypes. A phrase like men are better at this, or women are better at that, sounds like a statement about every person. In fact, it describes a small shift in an average, with enormous overlap. The scientific habit is to say, on average, by a small amount, and with wide variation among individuals.

@char A01 cafe_sunset | A closing thought on myths | Why we pass them on
Finally, it's worth asking why we pass myths on at all. Often, it's because they feel helpful. They give us a quick way of understanding other people, and a sense of order. But a better understanding is available, and it's one that is both more accurate and more generous. People are varied, and the evidence usually says so.

@list A03 living_room_day | How to read a headline about the mind | Check who was studied ;; Look for the size of the effect ;; See whether it has been repeated ;; Beware of words like always and never
As we finish, here are four ways to read a headline about the mind. Check who was studied. Look for the size of the effect, not only its existence. See whether it has been repeated. And beware of the words always and never, which almost never describe people. They take seconds to apply, and they protect you from a great many confident claims.

@char A19 cabin_fireplace | A reflective moment | Look for the overlap
The next time you're tempted to say that men are like this and women are like that, you might look for the overlap instead. Think of the friends who don't fit the stereotype, and of the ways in which you don't fit it yourself. That's the reality that the research describes, and it's a far richer and more forgiving picture than the one in the jokes.
