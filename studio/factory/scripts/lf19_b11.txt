@chapter A01 cafe_sunset | Birth order
Ask someone about their family, and you'll often hear a ready-made description. I'm the eldest, so I'm responsible and bossy. I'm the middle child, so I'm the peacemaker. I'm the baby of the family, so I'm the rebel, the joker, the one who gets away with things. It's a story we tell with a smile, and it has a surprisingly long history.

@char A12 living_room_day | The old idea | Alfred Adler
The idea that the order of birth shapes personality goes back to the Austrian psychologist Alfred Adler, writing in the early twentieth century. He suggested that the eldest child, who starts as an only child and then loses the spotlight, develops a certain character. The youngest, who is always the baby, develops another. Each position, he argued, brings its own challenges.

@char A04 attic_rain | The big claim | Born to Rebel
In 1996, the historian of science Frank Sulloway published a very influential book called Born to Rebel. He argued that firstborns identify with authority and tend to be conscientious and conservative, while later-born children are more open to new ideas, and more likely to rebel. His argument drew on a huge collection of historical examples and was widely discussed.

@list A03 living_room_day | The popular picture | Eldest: responsible, serious, bossy ;; Middle: peacemaker, flexible ;; Youngest: rebel, charming, carefree ;; Only child: mature, a little spoiled
It's a very tidy set of stereotypes. And that tidiness is part of the appeal. Each of us can find ourselves in the picture, and each of us can think of a family that fits. But as we've seen again and again, a story that fits can still be wrong.

@chapter A13 rooftop_dusk | The problem with the evidence
Much of the older research on birth order had a basic weakness. It compared people from different families, and it often relied on small samples, or on people's own descriptions of themselves and their siblings. The way we describe our own family roles is easily coloured by the stories that we have been told.

@char A10 attic_rain | A subtle trap | We play the part we're given
There's another problem. If everyone knows that the eldest is meant to be responsible, then parents and siblings may treat the eldest that way, and the eldest may come to describe themselves that way. In that case, a measured difference may reflect the expectations of the people in the family, and not any deeper effect of the order of birth.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Rohrer, Egloff & Schmukle | Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 2015 | Examining the effects of birth order on personal lives and personality
In 2015, Julia Rohrer and her colleagues in Germany tested the idea on a scale that had never been tried before. They analysed data from three large national surveys, in the United States, Britain and Germany, with more than twenty thousand people in total. And they compared siblings within the same families, to avoid mixing up differences between families.

@stat A14 conspiracy_board | People in the three surveys | Over 20,000 | from the United States, Britain and Germany
The results were clear. There were no meaningful differences in extraversion, emotional stability, agreeableness, conscientiousness or imagination between first-borns, middle-borns and later-borns. Whatever the stereotype, the data did not show a pattern in the way that people described themselves.

@list A12 living_room_day | What the large study found | No effects on four of the Big Five traits ;; Very small effects on imagination ;; Slight differences in self-described intellect ;; Nothing like the stereotypes
That last line is the main point. The differences were small, and they were nowhere near the dramatic character types that the popular picture suggests. A reasonable summary is that birth order has, at best, a tiny effect on personality.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Damian & Roberts | Journal of Research in Personality, 2015 | The associations of birth order with personality and intelligence in a representative sample of US high school students
Another large study came out in the same year. Rodica Damian and Brent Roberts looked at nearly four hundred thousand American high school students, and again found no effects of birth order on personality that were big enough to matter. The two studies, using different methods, came to the same conclusion.

@char A04 attic_rain | A caution | Absence of evidence, carefully
It's worth being careful about what this means. These studies don't say that families have no effect on personality. They say that the position in the birth order, taken alone, doesn't have a large, consistent effect. Other things, such as parenting, culture, genes, friends, and chance events, matter more.

@chapter A12 living_room_day | A small effect that is real
There's one area, however, where there does seem to be a real, if small, effect: intelligence test scores. In 2007, two Norwegian researchers, Petter Kristensen and Tor Bjerkedal, studied the test scores of more than a quarter of a million young men who'd been examined for military service.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Kristensen & Bjerkedal | Science, 2007 | Explaining the relation between birth order and intelligence
They found that the firstborns scored slightly higher than the second-born sons, by about two or three points on a scale where the average is a hundred. The second-borns, in turn, scored a little higher than the thirds. The differences were small, but with such a large sample, they were reliable.

@stat A06 cafe_sunset | Difference in IQ | About 2.3 points | between first-born and second-born brothers in a Norwegian sample of more than 240,000
It's a tiny gap, much smaller than the differences between individual people. And what's most interesting is what the researchers found about the cause. When an older brother had died in infancy, the next son was raised as the eldest, and his scores looked like a firstborn's. It seems to be the social role, not biology, that made the difference.

@char A10 attic_rain | An interesting hint | The role, not the order
This suggests that the effect, small as it is, relates to how children are raised. The eldest child may get more of the parents' attention in the early years, and may take on a teaching role with younger siblings. That could explain a slight edge in test scores, though it's not a big or dramatic effect.

@list A03 living_room_day | What we can fairly say | Birth order has almost no effect on personality traits ;; It has a very small effect on IQ test scores ;; The IQ effect seems linked to family roles ;; The stereotypes are much stronger than the data
It's a good reminder of the gap between what feels true and what is measured. The stereotypes are powerful, because they give us a shared language. But they describe our stories, more than our personalities.

@chapter A06 cafe_sunset | Why the myth lingers
Why do the stereotypes survive? One reason is that we love an easy explanation. Another is that they're partly self-fulfilling. If everyone treats the youngest as the baby, the youngest may play along. And there's confirmation bias, in which we notice the cases that fit and forget the ones that don't.

@char A01 cafe_sunset | An everyday test | Think of three families
Try thinking of three families that you know well. In how many of them does each child fit the stereotype? Most people find that it's a mixed picture. There are bossy youngest children and shy eldest children, and plenty of middle children who are anything but peacemakers.

@char A19 cabin_fireplace | A kinder idea | Children are individuals
There's a kind message hidden in the research. If birth order doesn't fix personality, then no child is stuck in a role. An eldest child who's been told that they're the responsible one can be silly, and a youngest child who's been labelled the baby can take charge. Children are individuals, and they can surprise us.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Rohrer et al. | Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 2015 | The same study, a further note
The authors also noted that the effect of labels can matter. If parents treat children differently because of their position, then that treatment may have consequences. So the stereotype, though it isn't true, can still shape how children feel about themselves, and that's a good reason to use it lightly.

@list A12 living_room_day | Ways to avoid labelling | Notice what each child is good at ;; Let roles change over time ;; Avoid jokes that fix a child as the baby or the boss ;; Ask children how they see themselves
These are small and gentle suggestions. They don't come from a particular study, but from the general finding that labels can stick. Giving each child room to be many things is likely to serve them well.

@char A15 cabin_fireplace | A gentle thought | You are more than your place
If you've grown up as the eldest, the middle or the youngest, you may have carried a role that was never really yours. The research is a permission slip. You can be whatever you want to be, whatever your place in the line.

@char A08 cabin_fireplace | Looking back | What the birth order studies taught us
So what did we learn? That the belief goes back to Adler, and was popularised by Sulloway. That studies of more than twenty thousand people found no meaningful personality effects. That there's a small IQ effect, linked to family roles. And that the stereotypes are more about stories than about people.

@char A12 living_room_day | Twins and siblings | Why siblings differ
If birth order doesn't explain much, why are siblings, who grow up in the same house, often so different? Behavioural geneticists such as Robert Plomin have found that much of the difference comes from genes, and from experiences that siblings don't share, such as different friends and teachers. The family environment, it seems, makes siblings less alike than people tend to think.

@quote A15 cabin_fireplace | Families are not formulas | A gentle thought
There's some peace in knowing that families can't be reduced to a formula. Every household is a small, shifting world, and every child brings their own temperament to it. The most useful thing the research can offer is permission to stop sorting children into types, and to look instead at the particular person in front of you.

@char A19 cabin_fireplace | A reflective moment | The family story
Is there a story that your family tells about you, perhaps something like the quiet one, the clever one or the difficult one? Consider whether it's really true, or whether it grew from a few moments long ago. You're allowed to rewrite it. The research suggests that those stories are more fragile than they seem, and that the person you are now is not bound by them.
