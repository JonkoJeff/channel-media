On the walk home from the choir, Ines puts on a slow, sad song. It's about someone leaving a small town, and it makes her throat tighten a little. She isn't sad about anything in particular. She just wants to hear it. And she's never been sure why that's allowed.

@char N02 living_room_day | A real puzzle | Seeking out sadness
It's a genuinely strange thing, when you think about it. Most of the time, we avoid feeling sad. We don't go out of our way to feel loss or loneliness. And yet, with music, many people actively choose it, and describe it as one of their favorite kinds of listening.

@study N07 cafe_sunset | Liila Taruffi and Stefan Koelsch | PLoS ONE, 2014 | Over 770 people described what sad music gave them; many reported nostalgia, peace and tenderness
In 2014, Liila Taruffi and Stefan Koelsch ran an online survey of more than 770 people about sad music. Many said that sad music didn't make them feel sad, or at least not only sad. They reported feelings of nostalgia, peacefulness, tenderness and being moved, and said that the music helped them to feel understood, or to feel something they couldn't otherwise express.

@char N19 cabin_fireplace | Sadness in a safe frame | The role of distance
One popular explanation is that sad music gives you the feel of sadness at a safe distance. In real life, sadness is attached to real losses. In music, it's a shape without a cause. You can feel the tenderness, the longing, even the tears, without having to deal with the loss that would normally come with them.

@char N10 autumn_overlook | A study of pleasure | Kawakami and colleagues
In 2013, Ai Kawakami and colleagues in Japan found that listeners often distinguished between the sadness they felt that came from the music, which they enjoyed, and the sadness they perceived in the music, which was more neutral. People rated the music as sad, but their own feelings were often mild and pleasant.

@char N23 cafe_sunset | A chemical idea | The prolactin proposal
In 2011, David Huron, whom we met earlier, proposed a chemical explanation. He suggested that sadness might trigger the release of a hormone called prolactin, which is involved in soothing and comfort, and which may produce a mild reward when there's no real loss to be dealt with. It's an intriguing idea, but it hasn't been confirmed, and other researchers have questioned it.

@photo lf48_b13_01.jpg | A rainy window at night
There's a gentle logic to it. A rainy window on a cold night is more beautiful from the inside, with a warm drink. Sad music may work a bit like that. You get the weather without the soaking.

@char N20 bedroom_night | The role of empathy | Feeling with the music
Another line of research has linked the enjoyment of sad music to empathy. Tuomas Eerola and colleagues in Finland found in 2016 that people who were more empathic were more likely to be moved by unfamiliar sad music. It's as if the music brings out the same part of us that responds to a friend's feelings, and we find that response rewarding.

@char N18 rooftop_dusk | Not for everyone, or every time | A caution
There's an important caution. Sad music isn't always helpful. Some research, including work by Sandra Garrido and Emery Schubert in Australia, suggests that people who tend to ruminate, going over the same sad thoughts again and again, may be more likely to feel worse after sad music, instead of being helped to move on. How you listen matters, not only what you listen to.

@char N02 cafe_sunset | A simple rule of thumb | Notice how you feel
If you find that sad songs leave you feeling calmer, more connected or a little lighter, they're probably doing you good. If they leave you feeling heavier and more alone, it may be worth switching to something else, or talking to someone. Nobody else can tell you which it is. Your own experience is the best guide.

@char N10 bedroom_night | Why chills and sadness meet | Moved by beauty
That also fits what we've seen about chills. Many of the strongest chills come from music that carries a note of longing or loss, which is another reason they're so closely tied to being moved. A joyful piece might lift us. A bittersweet one can break us open in a way that feels good.
