@chapter A02 cabin_fireplace | The body that stays still
Let's turn to a different question, and a quieter one. In a dream you can run, climb, swim or fall, and yet you lie perfectly still in the dark. How does your body manage to stay out of the story?

@char A10 attic_rain | A familiar contradiction | Running, and not moving
Perhaps you have had a dream in which you were running for a train, your legs pumping, your breath short, and then you woke to find yourself lying under a blanket, hardly moving at all. It is a strange thing, if you think about it. Your brain seemed to be sending the command to run, and your body, thankfully, did not obey.

@char A12 living_room_day | A quiet safeguard | Muscle atonia
Sleep researchers have a name for this: REM atonia. It means that during REM sleep, most of the muscles that move your arms and legs are switched off, so to speak. The eyes still move, as the first sleep researchers saw. But the large muscles that would carry out a dream are held quiet, and that is probably a good thing, for you and for anyone sleeping nearby.

@list A03 cafe_sunset | Key idea | REM sleep: the dream brain is busy ;; Atonia: the body's muscles are held quiet ;; Dream action: stays inside the dream ;; Waking: the muscles come back online
Notice how neatly this fits with everything else we have covered. The brain is highly active, painting a vivid world. The body is, in a sense, put on hold. A dream can run and fall and fight, while you stay safely in place. The question is how anyone found out that this is a deliberate arrangement, and not just an accident of tiredness.

@char A13 rooftop_dusk | A clue from the cats | A French neuroscientist
The answer began with a French neuroscientist, and with an unexpected finding in cats. It is a small piece of laboratory history, but it changed how we think about the sleeping body, and it is worth a moment of your attention.

@study A13 living_room_day | Michel Jouvet | 1965 | Lesions near the locus coeruleus in cats produced REM sleep without atonia
Michel Jouvet studied sleep in cats, and in 1965 his work showed something remarkable. When he made small lesions in a region of the brainstem near the locus coeruleus, the cats still went into REM sleep, but they lost the muscle paralysis that normally goes with it. They were, in effect, REM sleepers whose bodies were no longer held still.

@char A03 attic_rain | What the cats did | Oneiric behaviour
What did they do? Researchers describe it as oneiric behaviour, from the Greek word for dream. The cats moved during REM sleep, while they were still asleep, as though they were acting out what they were dreaming. No one can ask a cat what it dreams, of course, so we have to be careful. But the pattern was clear enough to suggest that the paralysis is made by the brain, and can be taken away.

@list A12 cabin_fireplace | What the cat experiments suggested | The paralysis in REM is produced by the brain itself ;; It depends on particular areas of the brainstem ;; Remove it, and sleeping animals move ;; The dreaming brain was always trying to act
This last point is the one that people find touching. It suggests that the brain really does send movement commands during a dream, which is why the body needs a safeguard in the first place. The stillness is not the absence of action. It is something your brain actively does, every night, so that the dream can stay a dream.

@char A05 cafe_sunset | An old puzzle, answered | Why we do not sleepwalk through every dream
For a long time, nobody had a good answer to a simple question: if dreams are so vivid, why doesn't everyone act them out every night? Jouvet's cats offered a first clue. The reason is not that we do not try. The reason is that something in the brainstem steps in and says, not now. Each night, in each period of REM sleep, that quiet instruction is given again, and in most people it is obeyed without fail.

@chapter A09 attic_rain | When the safeguard slips
Of course, nature is full of variation, and in some people this arrangement does not work in the usual way. The best known case is a condition with a rather long name, which doctors call REM sleep behaviour disorder.

@study A13 living_room_day | Schenck, Bundlie, Ettinger & Mahowald | Sleep, 1986 | Chronic behavioral disorders of human REM sleep: a new category of parasomnia
Carlos Schenck and his colleagues described patients who acted out their dreams during REM sleep, sometimes violently, because the usual muscle paralysis was missing. They proposed it as a new category of sleep disorder, in which the usual muscle paralysis of REM sleep fails. It was, in a sense, the human version of what Jouvet had seen in cats.

@char A03 cabin_fireplace | The reverse | Acting out the dream
For these patients, the dream is not contained. A person may move and act while dreaming about something quite different from the quiet room around them. The description is not far from the sleeping cats. And that is why the finding mattered so much: it showed that the stillness we take for granted is a real, separate mechanism.

@char A12 living_room_day | A note on the wider picture | Left to the specialists
Later research has linked REM sleep behaviour disorder with certain conditions of the brain that can appear in later life, and that is a topic for doctors, not for a sleepy evening. What matters for us is the principle. If someone you know acts out their dreams in a way that worries you, that is the sort of thing a doctor or a sleep specialist is well placed to look at, and nothing here replaces that advice.

@photo lf28_b06_01.jpg | Quiet, and perfectly still
It is worth looking at the stillness itself for a moment. Each night, through several periods of dreaming, a part of your brain gently takes your body out of the story and keeps it safe. You never feel it happening. You only notice it on the rare occasion when it does not run as expected, and that brings us to the opposite situation.

@chapter A11 autumn_overlook | When the stillness lingers
So far we have looked at a body that moves when it should be still. There is also the reverse: a mind that wakes up a little before the body does.

@char A01 cafe_sunset | An unsettling moment | Awake, but unable to move
Imagine opening your eyes in the early hours and finding that you cannot move. You are awake, you know where you are, but your arms and legs do not answer. After a while, the movement returns. This experience is called sleep paralysis, and it is a good deal more common than most people expect.

@char A13 attic_rain | The simplest explanation | Atonia that lingers
The usual account is straightforward. During REM sleep, the body is held quiet. If you wake up, or partly wake, while that quiet is still in place, you find yourself conscious in a body that has not yet been released. In other words, the safeguard stays on a little too long. The mind has come back, but the muscles have not, and the gap between the two can be alarming, especially the first time.

@study A04 attic_rain | Sharpless & Barber | Sleep Medicine Reviews, 2011 | Lifetime prevalence rates of sleep paralysis: a systematic review
Brian Sharpless and Jacques Barber gathered the results of 35 earlier studies, with a total of 36,533 participants, to ask how many people have experienced sleep paralysis at least once. This kind of review is useful, because any single survey can be skewed by who happened to answer. Putting many together gives a steadier picture.

@stat A14 conspiracy_board | People in the general population who have had sleep paralysis at least once | 7.6% | across 35 studies and 36,533 participants, Sharpless and Barber
About one person in thirteen, give or take. That is a lot more than most people guess. It means that if you have experienced it, you are far from alone, and if you have not, you probably know somebody who has.

@bars A19 cabin_fireplace | Lifetime sleep paralysis, rounded | percent of people | General population:8 ;; Students:28 ;; Psychiatric patients:32
The review also found that the figure differed between groups. In students it was 28.3 per cent, and among psychiatric patients 31.9 per cent. The authors treat these as lifetime rates, meaning at least one episode ever, and the groups were studied in different ways, so we should not read too much into the gap. But it does remind us that the experience is common, and more so in some groups than others.

@list A15 cafe_sunset | What is worth knowing | It is a recognised sleep phenomenon ;; It links to the muscle paralysis of REM sleep ;; It is reported by many ordinary people ;; Worries about it are best taken to a doctor
This is the reassuring side of the science. Sleep paralysis is a recognised sleep phenomenon that many ordinary people report. It seems to happen when the brain and body fall slightly out of step. If it happens often, or troubles you, a doctor is the right person to talk to, and there is no shame in asking.

@char A01 autumn_overlook | A pause for you | Have you felt it?
If you have ever experienced sleep paralysis, you may remember it very clearly. People often remember that moment for years. Knowing that it comes from a simple timing gap, and not from anything mysterious, can take some of the fear out of the memory. If you have never had it, you can simply file this away as a curious fact about how well your body and mind are usually coordinated.

@char A05 cabin_fireplace | A kinder way to see it | Mind first, body second
There is something almost graceful in the whole picture. Your mind and body are two systems, each with its own timing. Most nights they arrive at morning together. Now and then one is a few moments ahead of the other, and the strangeness we feel is the sound of that small gap.

@char A12 rooftop_dusk | Three things in one night | Side by side
It may help to put these three pieces side by side, because together they tell the story of a night.

@list A03 living_room_day | The body in three states | Normal REM: the dream brain is active, the body is still ;; Lost atonia: the body moves with the dream ;; Lingering atonia: the mind is awake, the body is still
In ordinary REM sleep, the dream and the stillness work together. If the stillness is lost, the body joins in the story. If the stillness lingers, the mind has left the story, but the body has not yet followed. The same mechanism is at the centre of all three, and that is what makes it so interesting.

@char A16 cafe_sunset | Why this matters for dreams | A dream you cannot act on
Here is why this belongs in a story about why dreams feel real. A dream that cannot move your body can only be experienced. You live inside it, and nothing in the room tells you otherwise. Because you cannot test it by getting up, the dream stays as it is, and it is real enough while it lasts.

@list A10 cabin_fireplace | Back to the dream | A convincing scene ;; A quiet inner critic ;; A body that cannot move ;; No way to test it
Put the whole chapter into one picture. The dreaming brain builds a convincing scene, and the quiet critic does not interrupt, as we saw before. Now add a body that cannot move, so there is no way to test the scene by acting on it. The dream has no seams you can pull at. It is sealed on every side, and that is a large part of why it feels like real life while it lasts. Only waking, with its movement and its light, gently breaks the seal.

@char A15 autumn_overlook | A thought for the day | Rest is an active thing
We tend to think of sleep as the brain switching off. The scans from earlier, and the cats, tell a different story. At night your brain is busy, and your body is gently kept out of the way. Rest is not an absence of activity. It is a carefully managed arrangement, and it works almost every night without you noticing.

@char A18 rooftop_dusk | Looking ahead | Sounds and touches in the story
There is one more door we can open. Your body is still, but your senses are not entirely closed. A sound, a smell or a touch can slip into your sleeping mind and become part of the dream, and that is where we go next.
