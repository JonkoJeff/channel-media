@chapter A03 bedroom_night | Growing into it
A family has boarded the train, travelling late after some celebration. A small boy of perhaps two sits on his father's knee. The father gives a gigantic yawn, right beside the child's face. The boy studies it with great interest, pokes a finger toward his father's mouth, and doesn't yawn at all. Oskar, three seats away, yawns instead.

@char A10 attic_rain | Yawning versus catching | Two different ages
This small scene shows something researchers have measured. Spontaneous yawning is present from before birth. Contagious yawning seems to arrive years later. A toddler can yawn perfectly well, and often does, when sleepy. What he seems much less likely to do is yawn because somebody else did. The equipment for the movement is in place before the susceptibility is.

@study N29 living_room_day | Molly Helt, Inge-Marie Eigsti, Peter Snyder and Deborah Fein | Child Development, published 2010 | Yawning during a story session
In a study published in 2010, Molly Helt and her colleagues at the University of Connecticut examined this in one hundred and twenty typically developing children, aged from one to six. While spending time with each child, an adult yawned several times, in a natural way, and the researchers noted whether the child yawned within the following minute and a half.

@bars A12 cafe_sunset | Children who caught a yawn, out of 20 | children | Age 1:0 ;; Age 2:1 ;; Age 4:7
Among twenty one-year-olds, none caught the adult's yawns. Among twenty two-year-olds, just one did. Only a couple of the three-year-olds did. Then came a clear step up: seven of the twenty four-year-olds yawned in response. By five and six, the figures were around the same level or a little higher, roughly four in ten.

@char N09 cabin_fireplace | A turning point near four | When minds meet
So somewhere around the age of four, catching yawns starts to become common. That's also a period when children are making big strides in understanding other people, such as grasping that someone else can believe something that isn't true. It's tempting to connect the two. The timing is suggestive, though happening at the same age doesn't show that one produces the other.

@char N10 rooftop_dusk | Other explanations | Attention and faces
There are other things changing at that age too. Children become better at attending to faces for longer, at reading expressions, and at noticing what adults are doing without being distracted by everything else in the room. A two-year-old busy with a toy may simply not take in the yawn. Researchers have to work to separate these possibilities.

@photo lf54_b11_01.jpg | Wooden toys on a rug
A few wooden toys scattered on a soft rug. A very young child's world is full of things to examine close up, and an adult's yawn is just one more of them. Later, other people's faces take on a special pull, and what happens on those faces begins to find its way into the child's own behaviour more easily.

@study N20 autumn_overlook | Atsushi Senju and colleagues | Biology Letters, published 2007 | Contagious yawning and autism
Researchers have also looked at children with autism. In a study published in 2007, Atsushi Senju and colleagues showed video clips to twenty-four autistic children and twenty-five typically developing children. The clips of yawning brought out more yawns in the typically developing group than in the autistic group, while ordinary control clips showed no such difference.

@char N01 bedroom_night | The first reading | And why it was too quick
Some commentators leapt from results like this to a sweeping claim about empathy. That was unfair, and later work gave good reason for caution. The Connecticut study also found that autistic children caught yawns less often, about half as often as others in their second experiment. But before saying what that means, we have to ask a basic question: where were the children looking?

@study A03 attic_rain | Atsushi Senju and colleagues | Journal of Autism and Developmental Disorders, published 2009 | What happens when attention is guided
In a further study published in 2009, Senju's team worked with thirty-one autistic and thirty-one typically developing children, and this time guided them to look at the eyes of the faces on the screen. Under those conditions, the two groups yawned to a similar degree in response to the yawning clips. The earlier gap wasn't there.

@stat N27 rooftop_dusk | When guided to look at the eyes | Similar | contagious yawning in autistic and typically developing children
That result suggests that, at least in part, the difference may come from where attention naturally goes. If you're not looking at a face, or at the part of the face that carries the signal, there is less to catch. It's a good example of how an early finding can look very different once someone checks a simple step along the way.

@list A12 autumn_overlook | Reading these studies fairly | Group averages are not individuals ;; Many autistic children do catch yawns ;; Attention changes the result ;; A yawn response is not a measure of caring
A few things should stay clear. These are group averages. Many individual children in every group behaved unlike the average. Guiding attention changed the picture. And whether a child yawns after watching a video tells us nothing reliable about how much that child cares about the people in their life. Caring shows itself in many other ways.

@char A10 living_room_day | The same lesson for adults | Looking first
There's a general lesson here for all the contagion research. Before a yawn can be caught, it has to be noticed. Anything that changes how much we look at one another, such as tiredness, a phone screen, shyness, culture or simply the layout of a room, could change how often yawns pass across it. Perception comes first.

@char N09 cafe_sunset | The girl with headphones | Eyes down
On Oskar's train, the girl with headphones who didn't catch his yawn was reading something on her phone the whole time. She may be immune. She may be unmoved by strangers. Or she may simply never have seen it. From where Oskar sits, there's no way to tell, and the same problem faces any researcher who doesn't check.

@char N10 cabin_fireplace | The boy again | Years to come
The small boy has now fallen asleep against his father's shoulder, mouth open. In a few years, if he's like many children, a yawn across a dinner table will begin to reach him. He won't notice the day it starts. Nobody marks it in a baby book. It slips in quietly among all the other ways of tuning in to people.

@list N20 attic_rain | Where we are | Contagious yawning is rare in toddlers ;; It becomes common around age four to six ;; Autistic children yawned less to videos in some studies ;; Guiding attention to the eyes removed the gap in one study
So here's the developmental picture. In one careful study, contagion was rare before four and clearly more common from then on. Some studies found less contagious yawning in autistic children, and at least one found the difference disappeared when children were guided to look at the eyes. Attention is part of the story.

All along the way, one word has been hovering: empathy. The idea that we catch yawns because we feel with one another is warm and appealing. It appears in countless headlines. At the next stop, we'll weigh it properly, with observations of real people in daily life, a large pair of student studies, and a note of healthy doubt.
