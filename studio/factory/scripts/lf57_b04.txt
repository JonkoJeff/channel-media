@chapter A12 living_room_day | The singing experiments
Here's the problem researchers faced. For about a hundred years after Darwin, almost nobody measured blushing at all. One paper from 1990 begins by saying exactly that: that little work on the psychophysiology of blushing had been done since Darwin's 1872 observations. The blush was famous, and almost completely unmeasured.

@char N07 street_day | A clever trick | Watch yourself sing
The team behind that 1990 paper was led by Don Shearn, and they found a lovely way to make people blush on demand. First, they recorded each volunteer singing. Then, the next day, they brought the volunteers back and played the recording to them, while the volunteers sat there wired up, watching themselves sing.

@study A01 cafe_sunset | Shearn, Bergman, Hill, Abel and Hinds | Psychophysiology, 1990 | 32 students watched a recording of their own singing, then a clip from a famous thriller
There were thirty-two undergraduates in the study, sixteen women and sixteen men. To compare the blush with plain excitement, the researchers also showed them a tense clip from Alfred Hitchcock's film Psycho. So each person had two experiences: one that was frightening, and one that was simply, awkwardly, about themselves.

@list N09 cabin_fireplace | What they measured | Colour in the cheek ;; Colour in the ear ;; Cheek temperature ;; Sweat on the fingers
They measured four things. The colour of the cheek and of the ear, using a small light sensor that picks up changes in blood volume in the skin. The temperature of the cheek. And the tiny changes in sweat on the fingers that signal general arousal, the kind of measure sometimes used in lie detector machines.

@char A03 living_room_day | The result | Your own voice wins
The result was clear. Cheek colour, ear colour, cheek temperature and finger sweat were all significantly greater while people watched their own singing than while they watched the thriller. A famous horror scene, with its shrieking violins, was less physically stirring than hearing your own slightly wobbly voice. Elena, I think, would understand.

@char N20 rooftop_dusk | Why singing works | A small, safe exposure
Singing works so well because it's exposing without being harmful. You're being heard in a very personal way, your breath, your pitch, your effort, and almost nobody thinks of their own recorded voice as quite right. Yet nothing bad happens. Nobody is judged or punished. It's attention, pure and simple, and that seems to be enough to switch on the blush.

@photo lf57_b04_01.jpg | Playing a recording back
A quiet room, a recorder, a voice played back. It sounds like the gentlest experiment imaginable. And yet sitting through your own singing turned out to be one of the most reliable ways ever found to make a person's cheeks go red.

@char A13 autumn_overlook | Seen, or not seen? | What observers noticed
There was another interesting detail. The researchers also asked whether other people could actually see the blushes. Only the moments of strongest cheek colour were judged as blushing by observers. In other words, a lot of what happens in a blush is felt from the inside but isn't obvious from the outside. That gap between what you feel and what others see will come up again and again tonight.

@char N01 cabin_fireplace | More often for women? | A small difference
In that study, observers judged blushes more often for the women than for the men. Researchers have reported differences like this in several studies, but they're careful about what it means. It might be partly about skin, partly about expectations, and partly about who feels more watched. It's a pattern worth noticing, not a law of nature.

@char A10 attic_rain | Other ways to make a blush | Reading aloud, praise
After that, singing became a favourite tool, alongside other gentle exposures: reading a passage aloud, being praised in front of others, being stared at in silence, or being asked to talk about something personal. Each of them puts a person in a little spotlight, and the face responds. Volunteers in these studies often laugh at themselves, which is a very human way of coping.

@char N10 bedroom_night | The body's two signals | Warm face, damp hands
What's striking about the 1990 study is that blushing came along with signs of general arousal, the damp fingers, as well as the colour. So a blush isn't only a matter of the face. It's part of a whole-body response, a little like a mild version of the alarm reaction, focused on the face. Elena's racing pulse after her solo fits that picture well.

@char A19 cafe_sunset | Elena puts on her coat | The hall empties
In the church hall, the chairs are stacked and the tea urn has been switched off. Elena finds her coat on the back of a radiator, where it's become pleasantly warm. She pulls it on, wraps her long green scarf twice around her neck, and says goodbye to the conductor, who tells her, with a grin, that she should do more solos.

@char N29 living_room_day | And yes | One more blush
And yes, she blushes again. Just a little this time. She laughs, says maybe, and heads for the door. The thought of doing it again is both frightening and rather tempting, which is something people who blush easily often say about the things that make them blush.

@char A15 street_day | Into the brain | Thirty years later
The singing experiments told us a lot about the skin. But they couldn't tell us much about what was happening in the brain at the moment of a blush. For that, researchers would need a brain scanner, and a way to measure cheek temperature inside it. In 2024, a team in Amsterdam managed exactly that, and they used singing too.
