The park path climbs a gentle slope towards the far gate. At the top of the slope there's a small wooden shelter, the kind with a bench and a roof, where people wait out summer rain. Elena stops beside it for a moment and looks back down the hill at the dark river and the lamps along its bank, small and golden in the distance.

@char N07 cafe_sunset | The feeling of being on show | As if under a spotlight
You probably know the feeling she had on stage. When you're blushing, or when you've made a small slip, it can feel as if a bright light were shining on you, as if everyone in the room had stopped to stare. Psychologists have a name for that feeling. They call it the spotlight effect.

@study A12 living_room_day | Thomas Gilovich, Victoria Medvec and Kenneth Savitsky | Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 2000 | Students wore an embarrassing T-shirt into a room full of other students, then guessed how many had noticed
In a well-known study published in 2000, Thomas Gilovich, Victoria Medvec and Kenneth Savitsky asked university students to put on a T-shirt that most of them found embarrassing, with a large picture of a singer who wasn't exactly fashionable among students. Then each one walked into a room where other students were sitting, stayed briefly, and left.

@char N27 autumn_overlook | The guess | About half
Afterwards, the students in the T-shirts were asked to guess how many of the people in the room had noticed the shirt. On average, they guessed about half. It felt to them as if the shirt was all anyone could have seen. After all, they'd been thinking about nothing else the whole time they were in the room.

@char A02 cabin_fireplace | The reality | About a quarter
But when the researchers asked the people in the room, only about a quarter had noticed the shirt at all. The wearers had roughly doubled the size of their audience in their own minds. The spotlight they'd felt was much brighter, from the inside, than anything the people around them actually saw.

@char N29 living_room_day | Why it happens | We start from ourselves
Why would we get this so wrong? The researchers suggested that we start from our own experience, which is full of the embarrassing detail, and then don't adjust enough for the fact that other people are busy with their own thoughts. Everyone in that room was thinking about their own day, their own worries, their own faces, not about a stranger's shirt.

@study A05 rooftop_dusk | Thomas Gilovich, Kenneth Savitsky and Victoria Medvec | Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 1998 | People who felt nervous or hid something overestimated how much others could read it on them
A related study, by the same three researchers, published in 1998, looked at what they called the illusion of transparency. People who were nervous, or who were hiding something, tended to overestimate how easily others could read it on their faces. We feel as if our inner states are leaking out for everyone to see. Mostly, they aren't, or not as much as we fear.

@char N07 cafe_sunset | Blushing fits the pattern | Felt more than seen
That fits beautifully with what the blushing researchers found. Remember the singing study from 1990, where only the strongest flushes were seen as blushes by observers. And the study comparing fair and darker skin, where the same blood flow felt quite different depending on how visible people believed it was. A blush is usually felt far more strongly than it's seen.

The view from the top of the park, the river and its little lamps far below. From up here, they look tiny. A lot of our blushes are like that when seen from outside, far smaller and softer than they feel from where we're standing.

@study A01 living_room_day | Kenneth Savitsky, Nicholas Epley and Thomas Gilovich | Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 2001 | People predicted how harshly others would judge their small public mishaps
And there's one more finding from this group that's worth carrying home. In a study published in 2001, Kenneth Savitsky, Nicholas Epley and Thomas Gilovich looked at small public mishaps and slips. People tended to predict that others would judge them more harshly than those others actually did. We expect a frown, and we usually get a shrug.

@char N27 cabin_fireplace | A kinder audience | Than we imagine
Put all of this together, and a rather comforting picture appears. Other people notice our blushes and our slips less than we think. When they do notice, they're usually kinder about them than we expect. And a blush after an honest slip tends to make them kinder still. The audience in our heads is much harsher than the audience in the room.

@char A12 street_day | What Elena's audience saw | Probably very little
So what did Elena's audience actually see? Some of them, the ones sitting close, probably noticed her pink cheeks. Many of them were looking at the conductor, or at their own child in the choir, or thinking about getting home before the frost got worse. And those who did notice almost certainly thought, how sweet, she's shy, and then forgot about it.

@char N29 living_room_day | The last puzzle | Where it all begins
Elena smiles at that thought, and carries on towards the gate. There's one last thing she wonders about as she goes. Babies cry and smile and laugh very early in life. But nobody has ever seen a newborn blush with embarrassment. So when does it begin? And what has to happen in a child's mind before the first blush can appear?
