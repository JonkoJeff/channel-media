@chapter A06 cafe_sunset | The myth of the silent crowd
The story of Kitty Genovese has been retold in textbooks for sixty years. Thirty-eight people watched a murder from their windows and did nothing. It's a powerful tale. The trouble is that much of it appears to be wrong, and the way it was corrected is a lesson in itself.

@char A12 living_room_day | The first report | A headline that stuck
Two weeks after the killing, The New York Times published a front page article that stated that thirty-eight respectable, law-abiding citizens had watched a killer stalk and stab a woman in three separate attacks, and that not one person telephoned the police. The article was widely read, and it shaped how a generation thought about city life.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Manning, Levine & Collins | American Psychologist, 2007 | The Kitty Genovese murder and the social psychology of helping: the parable of the 38 witnesses
Rachael Manning, Mark Levine and Alan Collins went back to the court records and later accounts. They found that the number thirty-eight could not be supported. Few people had a clear view of the attacks, some heard only fragments they took for a quarrel, and at least one witness did call the police. The picture of a crowd of callous watchers was far too simple.

@list A03 living_room_day | What the later research found | The count of thirty-eight was not supported by the records ;; Most neighbours did not see the whole attack ;; At least one person called the police ;; A neighbour, Sophie Farrar, went to comfort the victim
The last of these details is the one that stays with people. A neighbour, Sophie Farrar, reached Kitty Genovese in the final moments and held her. The tale of total indifference left out this act, and many others like it. The story endured partly because it was simple and shocking, and not because it was accurate.

@chapter A13 rooftop_dusk | Why the myth lasted
Why did the false version spread so widely, and why did it last? Psychologists point out that it fitted what many people already believed about big cities, and it offered a dramatic lesson. A vivid story is often remembered long after the corrections appear, and the corrections reach far fewer readers.

@char A04 attic_rain | A lesson in itself | Vivid beats accurate
The case is a reminder that our picture of human nature is shaped by stories that may not be true. It also shows how a good research question can come from a flawed starting point. Darley and Latané's experiments were sound, even though the news story that inspired them was not.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Fischer, Krueger, Greitemeyer, Vogrincic, Kastenmüller, Frey, Heene, Wicher & Kainbacher | Psychological Bulletin, 2011 | The bystander-effect: a meta-analytic review on bystander intervention in dangerous and non-dangerous emergencies
Peter Fischer and his colleagues combined the results of 105 studies, covering more than seven thousand participants. They confirmed that the presence of other people reduced the chance of helping, on average. But they also found something the early studies missed: in dangerous situations, where the need was clear and the risk to the helper was real, the effect was much weaker, and sometimes reversed.

@char A12 living_room_day | The correction | Danger changes the maths
That is an important refinement. In ambiguous situations, where it's not obvious that help is needed, a crowd makes people hesitate. In clear emergencies, where the danger is obvious and help is plainly needed, groups often act. The bystander effect is real, but it's not universal.

@chapter A06 cafe_sunset | What the cameras show
Recently, researchers have found a new kind of evidence: footage from security cameras. Instead of recreating emergencies in the laboratory, they could watch real ones, in real streets, in several countries.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Philpot, Liebst, Levine, Bernasco & Lindegaard | American Psychologist, 2020 | Would I be helped? Cross-national CCTV footage shows that intervention is the norm in public conflicts
Richard Philpot and his colleagues analysed CCTV footage of 219 public conflicts, in Amsterdam, Cape Town and Lancaster. In nine out of ten of them, at least one bystander intervened in some way. And the more bystanders present, the more likely it was that someone stepped in, which is the opposite of the classic bystander effect.

@stat A14 conspiracy_board | Public conflicts where at least one bystander intervened | 91% | in CCTV footage from Amsterdam, Cape Town and Lancaster
This is a remarkable finding. When people can see that something is wrong, and the danger is clear, they usually help. The finding doesn't erase the earlier research, but it places it in a wider picture. Both hold: in ambiguous situations, crowds can freeze us. In plain trouble, crowds often move.

@list A12 living_room_day | Two kinds of situation | Ambiguous: Is something wrong? ;; Ambiguous: Is it my place to act? ;; Clear: Someone is plainly in danger ;; Clear: Intervening is a recognised duty
The distinction explains much. When we aren't sure it's an emergency, we look to others for cues, and if they look calm, we conclude that all is well. Psychologists call this pluralistic ignorance. When it's plainly an emergency, that uncertainty disappears.

@char A01 cafe_sunset | The hopeful part | People step in
It's worth dwelling on this. For decades, textbooks suggested that bystanders are mostly passive. The newer evidence says the opposite for clear emergencies: most people do something, and often someone does. It doesn't mean that nobody ever fails to help. It means that helping is more common than the story told us.

@list A03 living_room_day | Words to keep from this chapter | Pluralistic ignorance ;; Meta-analysis ;; CCTV evidence ;; Ambiguous emergency
Four terms from this chapter. Pluralistic ignorance is when everyone privately worries but publicly looks calm, so that each person concludes there's nothing wrong. A meta-analysis combines the results of many studies. CCTV evidence is footage of real events. And an ambiguous emergency is one where it's unclear whether help is needed.

@list A12 living_room_day | A small habit to try | When unsure, ask out loud ;; Say, Are you okay? ;; Point to a specific person if you need help ;; Don't wait to see what others do
Here's a small habit that follows from the research. When you're unsure whether someone needs help, ask them. A simple, Are you okay, resolves the ambiguity that keeps crowds frozen. And if you're the one who needs help, point at a specific person and say, you, in the blue jacket, please call an ambulance. Naming one person ends the diffusion of responsibility, because it turns everyone's problem into one person's task. Picture a busy station platform, a man collapses, and fifty people hesitate. One woman says, you, please call for help, and within seconds, three people are moving. That is the research in action.

@list A12 living_room_day | Pause and reflect | Where have I seen a crowd freeze? ;; Did anyone speak first? ;; What made it easier or harder to speak? ;; What would I say now?
Here is a pause to think about the crowd. Where have you seen a group freeze when something unusual happened? It might have been on a train, in a shop, in a lecture hall or at a party. Notice whether anyone spoke first, and what it was that made speaking so hard. Often, it is not fear of the situation but fear of looking foolish. We worry that we will be wrong, that it will turn out to be nothing, that the others will think we are making a fuss. Psychologists call this evaluation apprehension, and it is one of the reasons why a calm crowd is so persuasive. If you can imagine the sentence you would now say, perhaps simply, is everyone all right, then you have already begun to prepare. The silence in a crowd is rarely a decision. It is a pause that someone has to end, and it can be you.

The correction of the Genovese story is also a reminder to hold our own certainties lightly. The most repeated stories about human nature are not always the truest ones, and checking them is a form of respect for the people they describe.

@char A15 cabin_fireplace | A gentle thought | The crowd is not your enemy
If the old story left you with a bleak view of other people, put it down. The best evidence suggests that most people help when they can see that it is needed, and that the crowd, more often than we feared, contains a helper. You do not have to resolve every question this research raises. It is enough to notice the silence in a crowd the next time you meet one, and to be the person who ends it, gently and without fuss, even if only by asking a simple question.

@char A08 cabin_fireplace | Looking back | What the myth taught us
So what did we learn? That the famous story of thirty-eight silent witnesses did not stand up. That the bystander effect is real in ambiguous situations, but weak or reversed in clear danger. That cameras show people helping in nine of ten public conflicts. And that naming a person can break the silence.
