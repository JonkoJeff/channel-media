Some weeks are simply harder than others, and for Ines, this is one of them for Ines. At her new job, a project has landed all at once. There are long meetings, and a lot of new names and faces to learn, and she gets home well after dark, with her shoulders up near her ears. She eats standing at the counter, barely tasting it. And that night, without really deciding anything, she scrolls. She is in bed, and the phone is in her hand, and forty minutes have gone before she notices. @chapter A12 cabin_fireplace | What a tired mind falls back on
As it happens, researchers have asked this very question. If willpower runs low, which behaviors do people rely on? A team of researchers, Neal, Wood and Drolet, wondered whether the answer might be habits, since a habit doesn't need much effort or attention to run. And they wondered something even more interesting. Perhaps a tired mind would lean on its good habits and its not-so-good ones in exactly the same way.

@study A15 cafe_sunset | Neal, Wood and Drolet | Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, published 2013 | When willpower is low, do strong habits take over?
Their paper describes five studies. In the first, students at UCLA reported every week on ordinary things, like which breakfast foods they ate, and which sections of the newspaper they read. Some of those were strong habits for them, and some were not. And two of the weeks they followed were exam weeks, when students tend to be busy, anxious, and tired.

@stat A10 cabin_fireplace | Students followed through the term | 65 | UCLA students in the first study
Sixty-five students took part in that first study, reporting week by week over about ten weeks, with the exam weeks of the term set against the quieter ones. It's a very ordinary setup, and that's its charm. No lab, no equipment. Just people noting down what they had for breakfast and what they read, and the researchers looking quietly to see what changed when the pressure rose.

@list A19 cabin_fireplace | In the exam weeks | Strong habits showed up more ;; Good ones, too ;; Bad ones, too ;; Weak habits barely changed
And here is what they saw. In the exam weeks, the students did more of the things that were strong habits for them. That held for habits that fit their goals, and for habits that did not. The weak habits barely moved. So the pressure didn't push everyone toward the unhealthy choices. It pushed people toward whatever they already did most often, whether that was good for them or not.

@char A13 living_room_day | A snack after an exam | Same person, different mind
Another study was set around a real two-hour midterm exam, with students from an MBA program. Some chose a snack before the exam, and some chose one after it. There were healthy snacks and unhealthy snacks on offer. After the exam, people leaned more heavily on their usual favorites, whichever kind they were. And how much they liked the snacks hadn't changed at all. The tired mind wasn't wanting differently. It was simply repeating.

@quote A18 cabin_fireplace | A tired mind repeats what it knows. | A gentle summary of the finding
That may be the most useful way to hold the finding. A tired mind repeats what it knows. It isn't being lazy, and it isn't being weak. It's using the cheapest tool it has, which is a pattern it has already built, much as a tired traveler follows a path they know. If that pattern is a kind one, a walk before work, a glass of water on the desk, then the tired mind is going to use it. If it's the old scroll, then that is what it uses.

@char A12 street_day | A fair word of caution | Averages, not rules
It helps to remember what studies like these are, and what they aren't. They describe what tends to happen on average, across many people. Some of us reach for the shoes on a hard week, and some of us never do, and both are completely ordinary. And the stress in a laboratory is brief and mild, nothing like a truly difficult stretch of life. So we'll hold these findings lightly, as a helpful lens and nothing more.

@chapter A20 cafe_sunset | What stress does to us
There is a second line of research that points the same way, and it comes from a different angle altogether. Instead of asking what people do in a busy term, a pair of researchers, Lars Schwabe and Oliver Wolf, asked what happens in the body and the mind when someone is under stress. They wanted to find out whether stress might tip us away from choosing, and toward doing what we have always done.

@study A03 cafe_sunset | Schwabe and Wolf | Journal of Neuroscience, published 2009 | Does stress push people toward habit?
Here is how they set it up. Before learning anything, volunteers either went through a stressful procedure or a calm one. Then they learned to choose between two actions, and each action tended to bring a different drink, chocolate milk or orange juice. Afterwards, one of those drinks was made less appealing, on purpose, and the researchers watched whether people would still choose it. That, you may remember, is how scientists tell a goal from a habit.

@char A10 attic_rain | The ice water | Three minutes of cold
The stressful procedure was a cold one. For three minutes, or for as long as they could bear it, volunteers held a hand in ice water, while being filmed and watched by a stranger. The calm group held a hand in warm water, unwatched. Later, each person ate chocolate pudding or oranges until they truly did not want any more.

@char A19 attic_rain | The test | Would they still choose it?
Then came the test. The calm group chose the action tied to the drink they had gone off less often. But the stressed group chose it just as often as the other one, despite having eaten their fill. The authors wrote that stress promotes habits at the expense of goal-directed performance. Their hands kept doing what they had practised, a little like Ines' hand finding the old light switch.

@bars A12 cabin_fireplace | Could they name every link? | percent of people | Calm group:58 ;; Stressed group:28
There was one more detail. The researchers also asked people which action had led to which outcome. Fifty-eight percent of the calm group could name every link correctly. In the stressed group, it was twenty-eight percent. So stress seemed to do two things at once. It made people lean toward habit, and it made their clear knowledge of what leads to what a little hazier, which is how tired evenings can feel too.

@char A15 autumn_overlook | Back to Ines | Both habits, one hard week
Ines doesn't know about the ice water, or the exam weeks. But she begins to understand her own hard week a little better. The scroll came back because it was the strongest pattern on that tired evening. The walk came back because, after several weeks, it had begun to be a pattern too. Her hand went to the shoes the way it once went to the light switch, and that gives her a rather lovely feeling, a bit like finding that a friend has quietly been on her side all along.

@char A19 living_room_day | Not a verdict | A tired week is only a tired week
There's a gentleness in all of this. A hard week is not a test of her character. It is a moment when the mind simply uses whatever has been practised most. So the question for Ines changes. It isn't, why am I so weak? It becomes, what would I like to have practised the most, for the days when I can't think? And that's a much kinder question to carry into bed.

@list A13 cafe_sunset | Kind things for tired days | Keep the shoes by the door ;; Keep the phone out of reach ;; Make the good thing easy ;; Forgive the tired evening
She makes a small list on a scrap of paper, just for herself. Keep the shoes where they are, by the door. Plug the phone in across the room on very tired nights. Make the good thing easy, and leave the hard thing for a better day. And, last of all, forgive the tired evening, because a tired evening is part of every week, and it has never meant that the work is undone.

@char A10 rooftop_dusk | The week eases | Rain on the glass
By Friday, the project has eased a little. The meetings thin out, and the light outside the office window is long and golden. Ines walks home the slow way, and the flat feels familiar when she opens the door. She puts the kettle on. Outside, the first rain of the evening starts up softly on the glass, and she lets herself listen to it, and does not reach for anything at all. The week has been a hard one, and she has come through it with her walk, and her kindness toward herself, both still there. That feels like quite enough for one evening.
