@chapter N20 cafe_sunset | Chewing gum and the inner voice
Later, Ruth puts down her book and goes to tidy her bag for tomorrow. At the bottom, among receipts and a hair clip, she finds a forgotten packet of mint chewing gum. As she unwraps a piece, the supermarket chorus pops back into her head, cheerful as ever. And, oddly enough, the gum in her hand connects to one of the most unexpected experiments in all of earworm research.

@study N09 living_room_day | C. Philip Beaman, K. Powell and E. Rapley | Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology, 2015 | Want to block earworms from conscious awareness? B(u)y gum!
In 2015, C. Philip Beaman and his colleagues K. Powell and E. Rapley, at the University of Reading, published a study in the Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology with a playful title: Want to block earworms from conscious awareness? Buy gum! It was the same Philip Beaman who had co-written the natural history of earworms in 2009, now testing a very practical idea.

@char N10 living_room_day | The idea | The inner voice
The thinking behind the experiment comes from research on memory and the inner voice. When we rehearse something in our heads, a phone number, a shopping list or a tune, we often use a kind of silent speech, quietly moving the muscles of the mouth and throat or at least preparing to. If an earworm relies partly on this silent singing, then keeping the mouth busy might interfere with it.

@char N20 cafe_sunset | Why gum | Busy jaws
Chewing gum is a simple way to keep the mouth and jaw busy without making a sound or needing much thought. So Beaman and his colleagues asked: if people chew gum after hearing a catchy tune, will they hear it less in their heads?

@char N09 living_room_day | The experiments | Catchy tunes, then gum
They ran three experiments. In each, volunteers listened to catchy tunes, and then spent time either chewing gum or not chewing gum, while reporting whenever they heard the music in their heads or thought about it. Some volunteers were asked to try to suppress the tune, and others were allowed to express it freely, so the researchers could see how gum worked in both situations.

@list N27 rooftop_dusk | What the three experiments found | Gum reduced conscious thoughts of the tune ;; Gum reduced hearing it in the head ;; Tapping a finger worked less well than chewing
The results were consistent. In the first experiment, chewing gum reduced how often the tune came into conscious awareness, both when people were trying to suppress it and when they weren't. In the second, chewing reduced the experience of actually hearing the music in the head. And in the third, chewing was compared with tapping a finger, and tapping was less effective.

@char N10 cabin_fireplace | Why tapping matters | A fair comparison
The third experiment is important because it rules out a simple alternative explanation. Maybe any repetitive movement would distract people from the tune. But tapping a finger, which is also a repetitive movement, didn't work as well as chewing. That supports the idea that it's specifically the mouth, the equipment of the inner voice, that matters.

@photo lf53_b14_01.jpg | A packet of mint gum on a wooden table
A packet of mint gum on a table. It's a wonderfully ordinary tool. If chewing really interferes with the silent rehearsal of music, it might help people who are troubled by repetitive earworms. It won't remove a song from memory, of course. But it might reduce how often the song breaks through into conscious awareness.

@char N20 cafe_sunset | The size of the effect | A modest help
It's worth being realistic about this. The effect of gum was real but modest. Chewing didn't make earworms disappear completely, it made them less frequent. And the experiments took place in a laboratory, with tunes chosen by the researchers. In everyday life, with personal favourites and many triggers around us, the effect may be smaller or larger.

@char N09 living_room_day | The inner voice | Singing without sound
This research reveals something interesting about earworms themselves. When we hear a song in our heads, we may not just be listening to an inner recording. We may also be, in a way, silently singing along. The earworm seems to involve the same systems we use for inner speech, which may be why something that occupies the mouth can get in the way.

@char N10 living_room_day | A link to repetition | Singing along inside
That fits with the 2023 study of repeated choruses, where volunteers' desire to sing along grew as the songs became familiar. One possible interpretation is that a song becomes an earworm partly as we learn to sing it inwardly. The more easily we can sing it in our heads, the more easily it can start by itself.

@list N20 cafe_sunset | Other ways to busy the voice | Talking, reading aloud
If this idea is right, other activities that occupy the inner voice might help too, such as talking with someone or reading aloud. Many people do notice that their earworms fade during a good conversation. That's not something these particular experiments tested, so it's a reasonable guess rather than a finding. But it fits the picture of the inner voice as one of the earworm's main stages.

@char N09 cabin_fireplace | Ruth chews | A small experiment of her own
Ruth, without knowing any of this, chews her piece of gum as she packs her bag. After a minute or two, she realises the chorus has gone quiet. She has accidentally run her own small version of the Reading experiment. When the gum loses its flavour and she throws it away, she wonders whether the chorus will come back. For now, it stays away.

@char N10 living_room_day | Not for bedtime | Better earlier
A word of caution, though. Gum is not much use at bedtime, when we'd rather be still and relaxed. And chewing gum isn't for everyone, especially people with jaw problems or other reasons to avoid it. As with all the strategies we've met tonight, it's an option, not a rule. Some people may find it helpful. Others may prefer simply to let the tune play.

@char N20 cafe_sunset | The bigger lesson | Earworms have a mechanism
The bigger lesson of the gum experiments is that earworms aren't a mysterious force. They have a mechanism, or at least part of one, and that mechanism can be nudged. A tune in the head draws on memory, attention and the inner voice. Change any of those, by absorbing your attention, busying your inner voice or reducing exposure, and the earworm changes too.

@list N09 living_room_day | Looking back | Chewing gum and the inner voice
So, in this chapter, we've seen that Beaman, Powell and Rapley, at the University of Reading, ran three experiments published in 2015, and found that chewing gum reduced how often people thought about and heard a catchy tune, while tapping a finger worked less well. The results suggest that earworms rely partly on the silent singing of the inner voice.
