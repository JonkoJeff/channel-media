@chapter A12 cafe_sunset | One world, many versions
Most of us carry a quiet assumption through life. When you and a friend look at the same sunset, hear the same song, or read the same page, you're both receiving the same world. The differences are only in what you think about it afterwards.

@char A10 living_room_day | The belief | Everyone sees the same world
It feels obvious. The sunset is out there. The song is out there. We all have eyes and ears that work in roughly the same way, so we must be seeing and hearing roughly the same thing. But tonight we've already met a few cracks in that idea. The dress that some people saw as blue and others as white. Russian speakers who sort blues a little differently. People who see more colours, or fewer.

@char A13 attic_rain | Some cracks run deeper | Coloured letters
Now let's look at people whose inner worlds are different in a way you might never guess, unless they told you.

@chapter A05 autumn_overlook | Letters with colours
Imagine reading this sentence and seeing every letter tinted with its own colour. Not on the page, but in your mind. The letter A is always red. The number seven is always a soft green. Tuesday is always yellow.

@char A03 cabin_fireplace | Synaesthesia | A blending of senses
That's an experience called synaesthesia, from Greek words meaning sensing together. In synaesthesia, one kind of experience automatically brings another along with it. Letters and numbers can have colours. Days of the week can have colours or even positions in space. For a few people, sounds have colours, or words have tastes.

@study A18 living_room_day | Simner and colleagues | Published 2006 in the journal Perception | Tested large groups of people for many kinds of synaesthesia, checking that their links stayed consistent over time
For a long time, synaesthesia was thought to be very rare. In 2006, the psychologist Julia Simner and her colleagues published a study in the journal Perception that went looking for it carefully, in large groups of ordinary people. They didn't just ask. They tested people with the same questions again later, because a real synaesthete gives the same colour for the same letter, again and again, months apart.

@stat A03 cafe_sunset | Some form of synaesthesia | about 4% | of people tested (Simner et al., 2006)
They found that some form of synaesthesia showed up in roughly four people in every hundred. That's not rare at all. It means that in a full classroom, a busy train carriage or a small office, there may well be someone whose letters, days or sounds have colours, sitting quietly beside you.

@photo lf30_b14_01.jpg | Letters with their own colours
And many synaesthetes don't realise that their experience is unusual until much later in life. Some tell the story of mentioning, as a child or a teenager, that a certain letter is obviously blue, and being met with puzzled faces. Until then, they had simply assumed everyone saw it that way, just as most of us assume everyone else sees the world as we do.

@chapter A10 cabin_fireplace | When the brain fills the gap
There is another way to see how much of the world comes from the inside. It's what happens when input from the eyes goes missing.

@study A19 living_room_day | Charles Bonnet | Geneva, 1760 | Described the vivid visions of his grandfather, Charles Lullin, whose eyesight was failing
In 1760, the Swiss naturalist and philosopher Charles Bonnet wrote about his grandfather, Charles Lullin. Lullin's eyesight was badly failing because of cataracts. And yet he began to see things that weren't there. Patterns, people, birds, buildings, appearing in front of him, clear and detailed. What's striking is that Lullin knew they weren't real. His mind was clear. He simply saw them.

@char A13 attic_rain | Charles Bonnet syndrome | Not madness, a missing input
Today, this is known as Charles Bonnet syndrome. It can happen to people with sight loss, and it's thought to be common among them, though many people never mention it, because they worry others will think they're losing their minds. They aren't. The leading explanation is that when the visual parts of the brain stop receiving their usual input, they don't fall silent. They keep producing images of their own.

@quote A18 cabin_fireplace | When the eyes go quiet, the brain keeps painting | The leading explanation, in one line
It's as if the brain's picture making machinery can't bear an empty canvas. When the eyes go quiet, the brain keeps painting. And that tells us something about everyone. Even when your eyes work perfectly, a great deal of what you see is painted from the inside.

@chapter A12 autumn_overlook | Lines that change length
Even in simple illusions, people's worlds can differ, and the reason may be the world they grew up in.

@study A10 living_room_day | Franz Carl Muller-Lyer | Germany, 1889 | Two lines of equal length look different when their ends carry arrowheads pointing in or out
In 1889, the German psychiatrist Franz Carl Müller-Lyer published a famous illusion. Two lines of exactly the same length. One has arrowheads pointing outwards at each end, the other has them pointing inwards, like little fins. To most of us, the line with the fins looks clearly longer. Even when you measure them, and you know they're the same, the illusion doesn't go away.

#ai lf30_b14_02.jpg | Editorial still-life of a wooden carpenter's ruler and two thin brass rods lying side by side on a pale drafting table, soft daylight, calm minimal mood, no people, no text, no logos

@photo lf30_b14_02.jpg | Equal lines, unequal looks
One idea is that your brain reads those arrowheads as corners. In a room, the corner that bulges toward you has lines spreading one way, and the corner that's further away has lines spreading the other way. So your brain treats one line as nearer and one as further, and adjusts its size accordingly.

@study A19 cabin_fireplace | Segall, Campbell & Herskovits | Published 1966, The Influence of Culture on Visual Perception | Tested illusions with people from many cultures around the world
In 1966, the researchers Marshall Segall, Donald Campbell and Melville Herskovits published a book called The Influence of Culture on Visual Perception. They had tested illusions like this one with people from many different societies around the world. And they found that people differed in how strongly they fell for the Müller-Lyer illusion.

@list A03 cafe_sunset | The carpentered world idea | Straight walls and square corners ;; Brain learns to read corners ;; Illusion grows stronger ;; Fewer right angles, weaker illusion
Their suggestion was called the carpentered world hypothesis. People who grow up surrounded by straight walls, rectangular rooms and square corners may learn to read lines and angles as clues to depth. And so the illusion catches them more strongly. People who grew up with fewer right angles around them seemed less affected. The idea is still debated, and other explanations have been proposed, but it points to something striking. Even the way you see a pair of lines may have been shaped by the rooms you grew up in.

@char A18 living_room_day | Your own version | Shaped by your life
So the world you see is shaped by your eyes, but also by your brain's wiring, by what you've learned, and even by the places you grew up in. Two people can stand side by side, look at the same scene, and experience versions of it that differ in small ways they might never discover, unless they stop and compare notes.

@list A03 cafe_sunset | Things that shape your version | Your eyes and their cells ;; Your brain's own wiring ;; The words you learned ;; The world you grew up in
Let's gather those influences together. Your eyes, with their own mix of colour cells. Your brain's particular wiring, which in some people links letters to colours. The words you learned, which may nudge how you sort what you see. And the world you grew up in, with its rooms, its corners and its light.

@chapter A05 autumn_overlook | The myth versus the science
So let's set this last belief beside the evidence.

@list A13 attic_rain | One world, many versions | Belief: we all see the same world ;; Science: about 4% have synaesthesia ;; Science: missing input brings visions ;; Science: culture may shape illusions
The belief says we all receive the same world. The science says that some people's letters and days have colours, that a brain without input starts painting its own pictures, and that even simple lines may look different depending on where you grew up.

@char A12 living_room_day | Smart guesses | Not broken, just personal
And notice that none of this means anyone's brain is broken. An illusion isn't a failure. It's what happens when a clever guessing system, one that usually works brilliantly, meets a picture designed to trick it. Each of our brains has learned its own way of guessing.

@char A10 cabin_fireplace | A bridge onward | Why does it feel so real?
We've now tested twelve beliefs about how we perceive the world. And together they leave us with the question we asked right at the start of tonight. If the brain is guessing all of the time, why does the world feel so solid, so obvious, so completely real?
