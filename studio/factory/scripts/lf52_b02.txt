@chapter N19 cafe_sunset | How common is it?
Elena doesn't mention her moment on the stairs to her friend, who is busy putting the kettle on. She assumes it's an odd thing that only happens to her. But when she finally brings it up later that evening, her friend laughs and says, oh yes, that happens to me too. So how common is it, really? Researchers have been asking that question for well over a century.

@char N10 living_room_day | The survey approach | Just ask people
The simplest way to find out is to ask. Dozens of surveys, going back to the early twentieth century, have asked groups of adults whether they have ever had the feeling of déjà vu. The questions differ, the groups differ, and the countries differ. And yet the results, taken together, are remarkably consistent.

@stat N02 cabin_fireplace | Adults who say they've had déjà vu | About two thirds | the typical figure across surveys, though single studies range widely
Alan Brown's 2003 review pulled the survey results together. He found that roughly two thirds of adults say they have experienced déjà vu at least once, although single surveys give figures from about thirty percent up to over ninety, depending on the wording of the question and the people asked. For a feeling most of us never discuss, that's an astonishing number.

@char N09 cafe_sunset | Why the range is so wide | Words matter
Why such a wide range? Part of the reason is wording. If you ask, have you ever felt as if you were reliving a moment, many people say yes. If you ask for a technical description, with the definition spelled out, fewer do. People also differ in how readily they recognise a fleeting feeling as déjà vu, and in how willing they are to admit to something that sounds odd.

@char N19 living_room_day | Not a rare event | Once in a while, for most
Among those who do report it, the typical frequency is not very high. A few times a year is a common answer, and some people report it more often, particularly in their teens and twenties. A small number say they have it every week. But the main message from the surveys is that for most people, it's an occasional, brief, unremarkable event, much like a hiccup of the mind.

@study N10 cabin_fireplace | Sno and Linszen | American Journal of Psychiatry, 1990 | The déjà vu experience: remembrance of things past?
Two Dutch psychiatrists, Herman Sno and Don Linszen, wrote an influential review of the subject in 1990. They collected what was then known, and they noted that the feeling is reported far more often by healthy people than by patients, which is one reason that most researchers now treat it as part of normal memory rather than as a symptom.

@list N27 rooftop_dusk | Who reports it more | People who are younger ;; People who travel often ;; People with more years of education ;; People who recall their dreams ;; People who are tired or stressed
Surveys keep finding the same small set of links. Déjà vu is reported more by younger adults, by people who travel often, by people with more years of schooling, by people who remember their dreams, and, in many studies, when people are tired or stressed. We'll look at several of these more closely, and we'll be careful about what they do and don't mean.

@char N10 cafe_sunset | Age | Peak in youth
Age is one of the clearest patterns. Reports rise during the teenage years, are common in the twenties, and then slowly decline over the decades that follow. Very young children rarely report it, though it's not clear whether they have it less, or simply lack the words and the concept to describe it. Researchers who study childhood memory say that this question is still open.

@char N09 living_room_day | A caution | Correlation isn't cause
A word of caution about those links. A correlation tells us that two things tend to go together. It doesn't tell us that one causes the other. Do people who travel more get more déjà vu because new places create more near-misses of familiarity? Or do people who are curious and open to experience both travel more and notice their own mental states more? Surveys alone can't settle that.

@char N19 street_day | How long does it last | Seconds
How long does an episode last? Brown's review concluded that it's typically very short, in the range of ten to thirty seconds. Some people describe it as lasting only a moment, and a few say it lingers for a minute or more. People also commonly report that it fades as they try to hold on to it, like a dream on waking.

@curve N02 cafe_sunset | The feeling over a lifetime | Age | How often people report it | 3 | It rises in youth and drifts down with the years
If you drew the pattern across a life, it would look something like a gentle hill. It begins to rise through childhood, peaks in the late teens or twenties, and drifts downward through middle age and beyond. That's a rough shape rather than an exact law, but it's what most of the surveys show, and it's one clue we'll return to when we talk about the brain.

@char N10 cabin_fireplace | One clue | The shape of the hill
It's a useful clue because it narrows down the explanations. A theory of déjà vu has to explain why it's common in people who are young and healthy, why it fades with age, and why it's linked to travel and new situations. Any explanation that only worked for rare illnesses, or only for the supernatural, wouldn't fit that shape at all.

@char N19 cafe_sunset | Does it happen to everyone? | Not quite
So does it happen to everyone? Not quite. A minority of adults, perhaps a third or so, say they've never had it. That's another useful fact, because it suggests that the experience isn't simply a built-in quirk that every mind produces on schedule. Some minds seem more prone to it than others, for reasons that are still being worked out.

@char N09 cafe_sunset | The people who never have it | A quiet group
It's also worth noticing the people who never have it. Some may simply forget, or fail to label the feeling. Some may have a habit of dismissing odd mental moments and moving on. And some, researchers suspect, may truly have a memory system that doesn't produce the same near-miss of familiarity. We know remarkably little about this quiet group.

@char N10 living_room_day | Back to Elena | Normal, and in good company
For Elena, the news is reassuring. What happened to her on the stairs is one of the most common odd experiences a human mind can have. It doesn't mean she's unwell, and it doesn't mean she's special. It means that her memory system, like almost everyone's, occasionally fires a false alarm of familiarity.

@char N02 rooftop_dusk | Looking back | What we set out to learn
So where do we stand? We've seen that roughly two thirds of adults say they've felt déjà vu, that single surveys vary widely, and that reports are highest in youth and decline with age. We've seen the links to travel, education, dreams and tiredness, and we've been careful not to read them as causes.
