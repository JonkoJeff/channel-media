You step into a place you have never been, and for a few seconds it feels as if you have lived this exact moment before. Why? In this calm, sourced deep dive we follow one imagined woman, Elena, through a stranger's hallway, and look at what psychology and neuroscience say about déjà vu. We cover where the word comes from (Émile Boirac, 1876), how common it is and who reports it, the old explanations from Plato to Bergson and Freud, the two parts of recognition (familiarity and recollection), illusions of familiarity and fluency, the layout theory tested by Anne Cleary with virtual reality, the experiment showing the feeling of prediction is not a prediction, brain stimulation studies in patients, the link with epilepsy kept in proportion, unusual cases, jamais vu and the Capgras delusion, a scanner study suggesting déjà vu may be your brain checking its memory, how memory is rebuilt rather than replayed, what makes déjà vu more or less likely, and a short toolkit for the next time it happens. We finish with a slow wind-down. This video is for learning and is not medical advice; if episodes are frequent, repeat, or come with other symptoms such as unusual smells or tastes, fear, or gaps in memory, please talk to a doctor.
