@chapter A12 cafe_sunset | A mathematician on a bus
Henri Poincaré was one of the great mathematicians of his time. Near the end of his career, he did something quite unusual for a mathematician. He described, in plain words, how his ideas actually arrived. His account appears in a book called Science and Method, published in 1908. And it's still one of the most quoted descriptions of creative insight ever written.

@char A13 attic_rain | Days at the desk | Getting nowhere
The problem he was working on was deep and difficult, a family of mathematical objects he called Fuchsian functions. You don't need to know anything about them tonight. All that matters is that he sat at his desk, day after day, trying different approaches, and getting nowhere. It sounds a lot like Mira and her sketches. Long, conscious effort, with very little to show for it.

@char A10 cabin_fireplace | One sleepless night | Ideas colliding
He describes one evening when, against his habit, he drank black coffee and couldn't sleep. Ideas rose up in crowds, he wrote. He felt them collide, until pairs of them seemed to lock together. By the next morning, he had made real progress. It's a vivid picture of the mind at work, ideas bumping into each other in the dark, a few of them fitting.

@quote A13 attic_rain | Ideas rose in crowds. | Henri Poincaré, describing a sleepless night
Notice something in that description. Even on that sleepless night, Poincaré wasn't forcing the ideas. They seemed to move on their own, rising and colliding, while he watched. He was almost a spectator of his own mind. It's a picture we'll meet again tonight, the sense that part of our thinking happens in a place we can watch, but can't quite steer.

@char A20 autumn_overlook | A trip away | Leaving the work behind
Then he left home to go on a geological trip, a kind of outing to study rocks and landscapes. And here's the important part. The journey made him forget his mathematical work. He had other things to think about. New places, new company, travel arrangements. The problem that had filled his days was set aside, not on purpose exactly, but simply because life had moved on to other things.

@photo lf38_b04_01.jpg | The step of a bus
And then came the moment he described so famously. Arriving in the town of Coutances, he and his companions got on an omnibus to go somewhere. And at the very moment he put his foot on the step, the idea came to him. A connection between the functions he had been struggling with and a different area of geometry. He hadn't been thinking about it at all. It simply arrived.

@quote A13 cafe_sunset | At the moment when I put my foot on the step, the idea came to me. | Henri Poincaré, Science and Method, 1908
What's lovely about his account is how calm it is. He didn't leap off the bus shouting. He says he didn't even check the idea right away. He took his seat, carried on the conversation he'd been having, and simply felt a perfect certainty that the idea was right. Later, back home, he checked it at leisure, and it was. Illumination, and then, calmly, verification.

@char A05 rooftop_dusk | Not just once | A pattern
And it didn't happen just once. Poincaré describes other moments like it. An idea arriving while he walked by the sea. Another while he was busy with something else entirely. Again and again, after long periods of conscious work that seemed to lead nowhere, a key idea arrived during a pause, or a change of scene, often with that same quiet sense of certainty.

@char A18 living_room_day | His explanation | Work going on unseen
Poincaré had his own explanation for all of this, and it's one of the most important parts of his account. He wrote that these sudden illuminations were a sign of long unconscious work that had come before. The idea only seemed to come from nowhere. In fact, he believed, his mind had been quietly working on the problem the whole time, even while he was looking at rocks.

@char A12 cafe_sunset | But not without effort | The conscious work matters
And he added something just as important. This unconscious work, he said, only seems to happen after a period of conscious effort. You can't skip the hard part. The unseen work needs material to work with. Without those frustrating days at the desk, there would have been nothing for his mind to combine on the step of the bus. Preparation, then incubation, then illumination.

@list A13 autumn_overlook | Poincaré's pattern | Long conscious effort ;; A break, or a change of scene ;; A sudden idea, with certainty ;; Careful checking afterwards
You can see why Wallas found this story so useful. It follows his four stages almost exactly. Long conscious effort. A break, or a change of scene. A sudden idea, arriving with a feeling of certainty. And then careful checking afterwards. Poincaré's bus step is the same moment as Mira's shower. Different centuries, different problems, but the same quiet shape underneath.

@char A15 living_room_day | A word of caution | About certainty
There's one gentle caution worth adding. That feeling of certainty, the sense that this must be right, is lovely, but it isn't always reliable. Poincaré himself mentioned that not every sudden idea turned out to be correct. Some flashes feel wonderful and then fall apart when you check them. That's exactly why verification matters. The feeling of an aha is a promise, not a proof.

@char A10 bedroom_night | Mira's certainty | And her doubts
Mira knows this feeling too. Right now, the jar of sounds feels perfect. But a quiet part of her remembers other ideas that felt perfect at midnight and looked rather silly at breakfast. She doesn't let that spoil tonight. She simply knows that tomorrow, with a cup of tea and fresh eyes, she'll find out whether this one is the real thing.

@char A03 cabin_fireplace | Why this matters | For the rest of us
Poincaré's story matters for ordinary people, not just mathematicians. It suggests that being stuck isn't wasted time. It suggests that taking a break isn't laziness. And it suggests that the moment an idea arrives might depend, in part, on what you do after you stop trying. A trip, a walk, a bus ride, a shower. All of them ordinary. All of them, perhaps, part of the work.

@list A12 living_room_day | Ordinary places for big ideas | A bath ;; The step of a bus ;; A walk by the sea ;; A shower ;; A quiet bed at night
Look at the places where these ideas arrived. A bath. The step of a bus. A walk by the sea. A shower. A quiet bed at night. Not one of them is a laboratory or a library. They're the most ordinary places in the world, places where the mind isn't being asked to do anything in particular. That's a pattern worth noticing, and we'll come back to it.

@char A19 attic_rain | Old accounts | And new evidence
Of course, Poincaré's account is one man's memory of his own mind. It's a wonderful clue, but it isn't an experiment. To find out whether stepping away really helps, and why, scientists would need to test it with lots of people, under careful conditions. And over the last century, that's exactly what they've done. We'll get to their results soon.

@char A12 rooftop_dusk | First, though | Why do we get stuck?
But first, there's a question that comes before all of this. Why do we get stuck in the first place? Why did Mira keep drawing foxes and lighthouses for three weeks, when the answer was a little girl with jars? Why did Poincaré's mind need a bus ride? It turns out that being stuck has its own quiet logic, and psychologists have studied it with some very clever little puzzles.

One of those puzzles involves nothing more than a candle, a box of tacks and some matches. It's been around for decades, and it's still used today.
