@chapter A12 cafe_sunset | Other people
So far tonight, we've mostly looked at motivation as something that happens inside one head. Your drives, your goals, your habits of mind. But very little of what you do happens alone. And it turns out that other people change your motivation in ways you might not expect.

@char A20 street_day | Never quite alone | Someone is always watching
Think about how you walk when someone you know is walking beside you. Or how you tidy the kitchen when a friend is about to visit. Something shifts. You speed up, or you try a little harder, or sometimes, you freeze. Psychologists have been studying that shift for well over a century.

@study A05 autumn_overlook | Norman Triplett | Published in 1898 | Noticed that cyclists rode faster with others than alone, then tested children winding fishing reels
It began with bicycles. In 1898, an American psychologist called Norman Triplett looked at cycling records and noticed something. Riders seemed to go faster when they raced against others, or were paced, than when they rode alone against the clock.

@char A13 cafe_sunset | Into the lab | Fishing reels
So he brought the idea indoors. He had children wind fishing reels as fast as they could, sometimes alone, and sometimes side by side with another child. Many of them wound faster when someone else was winding next to them. It's often called one of the first experiments in social psychology.

@char A04 attic_rain | A quiet caveat | A smaller effect than legend says
It's worth adding a caveat, though. When later researchers looked again at Triplett's own numbers, the effect in his data turned out to be fairly modest, and not every child sped up. Some actually did worse. That small detail, as you'll see, was a clue.

@char A10 living_room_day | The puzzle | Faster, or slower?
For decades, the findings looked messy. Sometimes an audience made people better. Sometimes it made them worse. A person might run faster with people watching, and then fumble a hard puzzle in front of the same crowd. It looked like there was no rule at all.

@study A11 rooftop_dusk | Robert Zajonc | Published in Science, 1965 | Proposed that the presence of others boosts whatever response comes most easily, helping simple tasks and hurting hard ones
In 1965, the psychologist Robert Zajonc offered a tidy answer. He suggested that having others around raises our arousal, and arousal pushes us toward whatever response comes most easily. If the task is simple, or well practised, the easy response is usually the right one, so you do better. If the task is new and hard, the easy response is often wrong, so you do worse.

@char A17 neon_alley_rain | A strange test | Cockroaches
And then, a few years later, Zajonc and his colleagues tested the idea on an unlikely audience. Cockroaches. They had cockroaches run away from a light, either down a simple straight runway, or through a more complicated maze. Sometimes other cockroaches watched from little boxes along the side.

@char A06 street_day | The result | Same pattern
With an audience, the cockroaches ran the simple runway faster. But in the complicated maze, they were slower. The same pattern as people. It suggested that this effect is old, and simple, and doesn't need a mind that worries about what others think.

@char A15 cafe_sunset | What it means for you | Practise alone, perform together
There's a gentle lesson in that. If something is still new and hard for you, it may help to practise it somewhere quiet, without an audience. Once it's familiar, other people can lift you rather than rattle you.

@char A03 living_room_day | The other side | When a group makes us lazy
But company doesn't always push us to try harder. Sometimes it does the opposite. And this story starts with a rope.

@study A13 rooftop_dusk | Max Ringelmann | Rope-pulling experiments, reported in 1913 | Found that people in a group pulled with less force each than they did on their own
In the late 1800s, a French agricultural engineer called Max Ringelmann had men pull on a rope, alone or in groups, while he measured the force. As the groups got bigger, the total force went up, but not by as much as you'd expect. Each person, on average, was pulling a little less hard than they did on their own.

@char A04 bedroom_night | Why? | Two possible reasons
There were two possible explanations. Maybe people simply got in each other's way, pulling at slightly different moments. Or maybe, when you're one of many, you quietly ease off. Ringelmann couldn't tell the two apart.

@study A16 street_day | Bibb Latané, Kipling Williams & Stephen Harkins | Published in 1979 | Asked people to clap and shout as loud as they could, alone or in groups, and found each person made less noise in a group
In 1979, Bibb Latané, Kipling Williams and Stephen Harkins found a clever way to separate them. They asked people to clap and shout as loudly as they could, alone or in groups. Then they blindfolded people and played noise through headphones, so each person only believed others were shouting with them, when in fact they were alone.

@char A14 cafe_sunset | The result | Effort fades in a crowd
Even when they were really shouting alone, people who believed they were part of a group made less noise. It wasn't just about getting in each other's way. Something in them eased off. The researchers gave this a name. Social loafing.

@list A12 living_room_day | When loafing fades | Your part can be seen ;; The task matters to you ;; You care about the group ;; The group is small
Later, a large review of many studies, by Steven Karau and Kipling Williams, found that loafing isn't fixed. It tends to shrink when each person's contribution can be seen, when the task feels meaningful, when people care about the group, and when the group is small. In other words, people work harder when their effort clearly counts.

@char A01 autumn_overlook | A surprising twist | Sometimes the weakest tries hardest
And sometimes, a group can make a person try much harder than they ever would alone. This one was first noticed nearly a hundred years ago, by a German researcher called Otto Köhler, who watched people lifting weights and rowing in teams.

@study A11 rooftop_dusk | Norbert Kerr & Deborah Feltz, and colleagues | Studies of the Köhler effect, 2000s and 2010s | Found that when a weaker member's effort decides how the team does, that person often works harder than they do alone
In more recent studies, Norbert Kerr, Deborah Feltz and their colleagues tested it with exercise. People held tiring exercises, sometimes on their own, and sometimes as a team with a partner who was a little stronger, often a partner on a screen. When the team's result depended on the weaker member holding on, those people often lasted longer than they did alone.

@char A15 cafe_sunset | Why it works | Not wanting to let someone down
It seems to come from two places. Wanting to keep up with someone a little better than you. And not wanting to be the reason the team falls short. That's worth knowing. A slightly stronger partner, and a task where your part truly matters, can be a quiet engine for motivation.

@char A10 living_room_day | The last kind | What everyone else is doing
There's one more way other people move us, and it's the most invisible. We look at what others do, and we quietly treat it as a guide for what we should do.

#ai lf32_b13_01.jpg | Editorial still-life of a neatly folded white towel hanging on a rail in a calm hotel bathroom, soft morning light, clean tiles, peaceful mood, no people, no text, no logos

@photo lf32_b13_01.jpg | A small card on a towel rail
Picture a hotel bathroom. On the towel rail, there's a small card asking you to reuse your towel. You've probably seen one. Most of them talk about the environment. And for a team of researchers, that little card became an experiment.

@study A05 autumn_overlook | Noah Goldstein, Robert Cialdini & Vladas Griskevicius | Published in 2008 | Found hotel guests reused towels more often when a card said most other guests did
In 2008, Noah Goldstein, Robert Cialdini and Vladas Griskevicius changed the wording of those cards in a hotel. Some rooms got the usual message about helping the environment. Other rooms got a card saying that most guests who stayed at the hotel reused their towels.

@char A06 street_day | The result | We follow the crowd
The second card worked better. More guests reused their towels when they were told that most other guests did. And when the card said that most guests who had stayed in that very same room reused their towels, it worked a little better still. People weren't just following others. They were following people like them, in the same place.

@study A13 rooftop_dusk | P. Wesley Schultz and colleagues | Published in 2007 | Told households how their energy use compared with their neighbours; heavy users cut back, light users crept up, unless a smiley face was added
A year earlier, P. Wesley Schultz, Cialdini and their colleagues found a twist. They told households how much energy they used compared with the average for their neighbourhood. Homes that used more than average cut back. But homes that used less than average started using more, drifting up towards the norm.

@char A15 cafe_sunset | The fix | A small smiley face
The fix was charmingly simple. When the low-using homes also got a small smiley face next to their number, a sign of approval, they didn't drift upwards. Knowing what others do pulls you towards the middle. Knowing what others approve of can keep you where you are.

@char A13 rooftop_dusk | A quiet power | Norms you never chose
It's a small finding, but it says something big. A lot of what feels like your own choice is gently shaped by what you think everyone else is doing. Sometimes that pulls you up. Sometimes it holds you back. Simply noticing it gives you a little more choice.

Other people shape what we do in the moment. But a huge part of daily life isn't decided in the moment at all. It just happens. Let's turn to the quiet machinery of habit.
