@chapter N10 living_room_day | Alone with our thoughts
Anton has been sitting without his phone for nearly an hour now, and it's occurred to him that this is unusual. In the old days, he thinks, a quiet evening like this would have been the most normal thing in the world. Now it feels almost like an experiment.

@char N02 cafe_sunset | A modern question | Can we just sit?
That thought leads to a question that a team of psychologists set out to test directly. If a wandering mind is so natural, and so full of plans and memories, then being left alone with it should be a pleasant thing. A few quiet minutes, with nothing to do, to enjoy the company of your own thoughts. Is it?

@char N19 autumn_overlook | A simple design | An empty room
The design of the experiment was simple, and a little unusual. Volunteers were asked to sit alone in a plain room, with no phone, no book, no pen, no music, and nothing to do, for a short period, and then to say how they felt. No tricks. Just the room, and the mind.

@study N07 cafe_sunset | Timothy Wilson and colleagues | Science, 2014 | Across eleven studies, people sat alone with their thoughts for 6 to 15 minutes and rated the experience, and in one study could give themselves a mild electric shock
In 2014, the psychologist Timothy Wilson and colleagues at the University of Virginia and Harvard published the results in Science, in a paper called Just think. Across eleven studies, they asked people to sit alone with their thoughts for between six and fifteen minutes. The people they asked included students, and also members of the wider community, ranging in age from their late teens to their seventies.

@char N23 rooftop_dusk | The first result | Not very enjoyable
On average, people did not enjoy it. Many said it was hard to concentrate, and that their minds wandered. And they rated the experience as less enjoyable than other activities, such as reading or listening to music, which they did in similar conditions. It seems that, left to ourselves, most of us find our own company a little less rewarding than we'd imagined.

@char N10 living_room_day | A cleverer twist | A small electric shock
Then came the twist, which got the paper into the news. In one study, the volunteers first experienced a mild electric shock, and many said they'd pay money not to feel it again. Then they were left alone in the room for fifteen minutes, with a button they could press to give themselves the shock, if they wanted. They were told that it was entirely up to them.

@bars N02 cabin_fireplace | Pressing the button | Percent who shocked themselves at least once | Men:67 ;; Women:25
The result startled the researchers. A majority of the men, about two thirds, and about a quarter of the women, gave themselves at least one shock during the quiet period. Some pressed it more than once. For some people, even something unpleasant seemed preferable to nothing at all.

@char N19 street_day | A careful reading | It's a striking result, not a verdict
It's important not to over-read it. The study was small, and it involved a deliberately odd situation, in a laboratory. The people who pressed the button were a minority in some groups, and the study didn't claim that people in general would choose pain over peace. What it shows is how uncomfortable an empty, unstructured stretch of time can feel.

@char N09 cafe_sunset | The same finding at home | A check outside the lab
The researchers also tested the idea at home. They asked people to spend a quiet fifteen minutes alone in their own homes, without distractions, and report afterwards. About a third admitted that they'd cheated, by getting up or picking up a phone. And the people who didn't cheat enjoyed it, on average, no more than those in the lab.

@char N02 autumn_overlook | A deeper reason | Thinking takes effort
The authors suggested a reason. Entertaining ourselves with our own thoughts takes effort. It requires us to choose what to think about, to sustain it, and to keep ourselves from slipping into worry. For many of us, that's a skill that gets little practice, now that something more entertaining is nearly always at hand.

@list N11 cafe_sunset | Why quiet can feel hard | There is nothing external to hold attention ;; The mind drifts to worries as easily as to pleasures ;; Entertaining yourself takes practice ;; Something more interesting is usually within reach
Four possible reasons come out of that discussion. Nothing outside is holding attention. The mind can drift to a worry as easily as a pleasure. Entertaining yourself is a skill that takes practice. And something more interesting is usually in your pocket.

@char N10 cabin_fireplace | A related idea | Boredom
There's a close connection here to boredom. In 2012, John Eastwood and colleagues published a paper in Perspectives on Psychological Science, in which they defined boredom as the aversive state of wanting to be engaged in a satisfying activity but being unable to. In that view, boredom is not an absence of stimulation, so much as a mind that wants to be engaged and can't find a way.

@quote N27 rooftop_dusk | Boredom is a mind that wants to be engaged and can't find a way. | a summary of Eastwood and colleagues, 2012
That's a helpful way to think about it, because it explains why the same quiet room can feel peaceful on one evening and restless on another. It depends on whether the mind has found something it wants to follow.

@char N19 living_room_day | The hopeful side | It can be learned
The hopeful part is that this seems to be a skill, and skills can grow. Many people find that quiet time becomes easier with practice, whether it's a regular walk without headphones, a few minutes of sitting at the window, or simply looking at the sky. The first few times may feel awkward. Later, the mind learns that it has somewhere to go.

@char N09 cabin_fireplace | Anton's confession | A little restless himself
Anton admits, in his own quiet way, that he reached for his phone twice this evening, and put it back both times. He didn't feel brave or disciplined. He just felt that, for tonight, he'd prefer to find out where his own thoughts would go. And, to his surprise, they've gone to some rather pleasant places.

@char N10 attic_rain | A gentle experiment | If you like
If you'd like to try it yourself sometime, there's no need to make it a test. Just a few minutes, perhaps with a cup of tea, and nothing to do. Notice what comes. Let it come and go. And if it's uncomfortable, that's allowed too. It doesn't mean you're doing it wrong.
