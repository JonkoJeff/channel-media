Earlier tonight, back on that platform with Jonah, we asked a curious question. Can believing something about stress change what it does to you? Now we can finally look at the answer. And it starts with a very large survey of ordinary people in the United States.

@study A03 cafe_sunset | Abiola Keller and colleagues | Health Psychology, 2012 | Nearly 29,000 adults: stress, beliefs about stress, and later health records
In 2012, Abiola Keller and her colleagues at the University of Wisconsin looked at data from almost twenty nine thousand adults. Years earlier, as part of a national health survey, these people had answered two simple questions. How much stress had they experienced in the past year? And how much did they think stress affected their health?

@char A13 living_room_day | Following up | Years later
The researchers then linked those answers to national records of who had died in the years that followed. It's a sombre measure, but a very solid one. And what they found surprised a lot of people.

@stat A10 cafe_sunset | High stress plus the belief that it was harming them | 43% | higher risk of dying early
People who reported a lot of stress, and who also believed that stress was affecting their health a lot, had a forty three percent higher risk of dying early, compared with people who reported little stress.

@char A12 living_room_day | The surprise | The other high-stress group
But here's the part that caught everyone's attention. People who reported just as much stress, but who did not believe it was harming their health much, did not show that higher risk. In fact, they had some of the lowest risk in the whole study.

@char A03 cafe_sunset | Belief alone | Not stress alone
In other words, in this data, stress on its own wasn't the strongest warning sign. Stress combined with the belief that it was doing harm was. The researchers estimated that over the years of the study, many deaths in the United States were linked with that combination, not with stress by itself.

@char A19 cabin_fireplace | Careful now | A survey, not an experiment
Now, we need to be careful. This was a survey, not an experiment. Perhaps people who felt stress was damaging them were already unwell, and could feel it. Perhaps they had harder kinds of stress. A finding like this can't prove that a belief, on its own, changes health. It raises a question. So researchers went looking for experiments.

@chapter A20 cafe_sunset | Two short films
One of the best known came from Alia Crum, Peter Salovey and Shawn Achor, published in 2013.

@study A10 living_room_day | Alia Crum, Peter Salovey and Shawn Achor | Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 2013 | Short videos framing stress as helpful or as harmful
They worked with employees of a large company during a stressful period. Some watched a few short videos explaining that stress can be enhancing, that it can sharpen focus, deepen connections and help people grow. Others watched videos explaining that stress is debilitating and harmful. A third group watched no videos at all.

@char A05 autumn_overlook | Same stress, different story | A small shift
Over the next weeks, the people who had seen the stress-is-enhancing videos reported fewer psychological symptoms, like anxiety and low mood, and said their work was going better, than the people in the other groups. Nothing about their workload had changed. Only the story they had heard about what stress is for.

@chapter A12 cafe_sunset | Knots into bows
Another line of research looked at the physical feeling of stress itself. The pounding heart, the butterflies, the dry mouth. What if those sensations aren't a sign that something is going wrong?

@study A03 rooftop_dusk | Jeremy Jamieson and colleagues | Journal of Experimental Social Psychology, 2010 | Students told that a racing heart can help them did better on practice maths questions
In 2010, Jeremy Jamieson and his colleagues worked with students preparing for the GRE, a big entrance exam for graduate school in the United States. Before a practice test, half the students were told something simple. People think feeling anxious during a test makes them do worse, but research suggests that arousal doesn't hurt, and can even help. If you feel anxious, that could be helping you.

@char A13 cabin_fireplace | A better score | On the maths section
The students who heard that message did better on the maths questions than the students who hadn't. And when the researchers later asked about the real exam, taken weeks afterwards, the same group reported better maths scores there too. The researchers gave their paper a lovely title. Turning the knots in your stomach into bows.

@quote A15 autumn_overlook | Turning the knots in your stomach into bows | Title of Jamieson and colleagues' 2010 paper
It's a gentle image, and a useful one. The knots are still there. The same physical feeling, the same racing heart. But tied in a different way, they can hold something together instead of pulling it tight.

@char A10 living_room_day | Inside the body | A more efficient heart
In later work, published in 2012, Jamieson and his colleagues found that rethinking stress like this changed the body's response as well. People who saw their racing heart as fuel tended to show a more efficient pattern, with the heart pumping strongly and the blood vessels staying more relaxed, rather than the tighter pattern linked to feeling threatened.

@chapter A15 autumn_overlook | I am excited
And then there's a very small sentence, studied by Alison Wood Brooks, published in 2014.

@study A20 cafe_sunset | Alison Wood Brooks | Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, 2014 | Saying "I am excited" before a nerve-racking task
Brooks asked people to do things that make most of us nervous. Sing in front of others. Give a short speech on camera. Solve hard maths problems under time pressure. Just before starting, some people were asked to say out loud, I am anxious. Others said, I am calm. And others said, I am excited.

@char A12 living_room_day | Excited, not calm | A shorter step
In her studies, the people who said I am excited tended to do better, and felt more excited, than the people who tried to tell themselves to calm down. Brooks's idea was that anxiety and excitement feel very similar in the body. Both are high energy. So it may be an easier step to move from anxious to excited, than to try to jump all the way down to calm.

@list A05 autumn_overlook | The stress mindset, in short | Stress can be a signal, not only a warning ;; A racing heart can be fuel ;; Excited is closer to anxious than calm is
So, the idea in short. Stress can be read as a signal that something matters, and not only as a warning that something's wrong. A racing heart can be fuel for the task. And when you're nervous, excited may be a closer and kinder place to aim for than calm.

@chapter A19 cabin_fireplace | The doubts left in
Now, here's the honest part. These findings are encouraging, and they've become very popular. But in science, popular and settled are not the same thing.

@char A13 attic_rain | Smaller than they looked | Not for everyone
When other researchers have tried similar mindset interventions in larger groups, the effects have often been smaller than in the first studies, and some attempts haven't found much at all. A belief can't make a dangerous situation safe, or turn years of hardship into something easy. And no one should feel that their stress is their own fault for thinking about it the wrong way.

@char A10 living_room_day | What survives | A modest, useful idea
What seems to survive is something more modest. How we interpret the feeling of stress can shift, a little, how we respond to it. Not a magic trick. More like adjusting the angle of a sail, when you can't change the wind.

@list A05 autumn_overlook | What a belief can't do | Make a dangerous situation safe ;; Remove real hardship ;; Make anyone to blame for their stress
So let's be clear about what a belief can't do. It can't make a dangerous situation safe. It can't remove real hardship, like illness, loss or loneliness. And it never makes anyone to blame for the stress they feel. A kinder story about stress is a small tool, not a test you can fail.
