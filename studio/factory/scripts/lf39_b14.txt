@list A15 autumn_overlook | What we learned, part one | What a chill is ;; Who feels them ;; The reward system ;; The dopamine clue
First, we learned what a chill actually is. Then we asked who feels them, and who doesn't. After that, we looked inside the brain, at its reward system, the same one that responds to food and other basic pleasures. And then we followed the dopamine clue, which showed that music doesn't only please us. It makes us wait, expect and want what's coming next.

@char A05 rooftop_dusk | The body's part | Tiny muscles, old reflexes
A chill is a wave of tingling or shivers, often down the neck, back and arms. Sometimes it brings goosebumps, as tiny muscles at the base of each hair pull tight, controlled by the body's alert system. Furry animals use the same reflex to fluff up their fur. Not everyone gets chills. People high in openness to experience tend to report them more often. And a small minority feel little pleasure from music at all.

@stat A12 living_room_day | Musicians in the first chills brain scan | 10 | each one bringing their own music, in 2001
The brain story began with just 10 musicians. In 2001, Anne Blood and Robert Zatorre at McGill University in Montreal scanned them while they listened to music that reliably gave them chills. As the chills grew stronger, activity rose in the brain's reward and emotion areas, and fell in the amygdala. The same systems that respond to food were responding to a song. It was a small study, but it opened a door.

@char A13 cafe_sunset | Waiting and arriving | Two moments, two places
Then, in 2011, Valorie Salimpoor and her colleagues saw dopamine being released while people listened to music they loved. And they found two moments. The caudate was most active in the seconds before the peak, during the waiting. The nucleus accumbens was most active at the peak itself. Later, in 2019, a study by Laura Ferreri and colleagues found that raising dopamine increased the pleasure people got from music, and dampening it reduced it.

@list A15 autumn_overlook | What we learned, part two | What in the music ;; Expectation and surprise ;; Sad songs ;; Songs and memory ;; Every culture sings
In the second half of our evening, we turned to the music itself. We asked what in the sound sets off a chill. We explored expectation and surprise, and the brain as a quiet prediction machine. We wondered why sad, bittersweet songs move us so much. We followed an old song back into memory. And finally, we found that people sing in every society on the list.

@char A18 attic_rain | Inside the music | Moments, not switches
Chills often come at certain moments. A voice entering, a new section beginning, a sudden rise in loudness, a harmony nobody expected. But they're not automatic. The same listener doesn't always shiver at the same spot, and different people shiver at different spots. Chills depend on the listener as much as the music. That's why Theo's shiver was his own, even in a hall full of people hearing the same notes.

@char A19 bedroom_night | Expecting, missing, remembering | Feelings woven together
The brain is always guessing what comes next. Music plays with those guesses, building them up, delaying them, then fulfilling or breaking them. Some researchers think a quick jolt of surprise, followed by a sense of safety, may help explain the shiver, though that's still a hypothesis. Bittersweet songs seem to bring more chills. Tears are a different peak, a calmer one. And songs from our past carry our memories with them.

@quote A13 cabin_fireplace | A shiver is the brain leaning in to listen. | Tonight's thought
Here's a small thought to carry with you. A shiver is the brain leaning in to listen. It's not a mistake, or a strange little glitch. It's a sign that something in the sound reached past the noise of the day and touched you. Your expectations, your memories, your body and your feelings all came together, just for a moment, in one quiet wave.

@char A03 autumn_overlook | Beyond music | Other doors to the same shiver
And it isn't only music that does this. Researchers call these moments musical chills, or more widely, aesthetic chills, and they can come from many places. A powerful speech. A scene in a film. A wide view in nature, like a valley opening out below a hill. A moving moment between people. The shiver seems to be one of the ways we notice that something matters to us.

@char A12 street_day | Theo's walk | A different kind of shiver
A breeze moves down the street, and Theo feels a small shiver of a different kind, just the cool night air. He smiles. It's funny how the same body can shiver for a cold wind and for a single voice in a quiet hall. One is simply the weather. The other is something much harder to name, something that belongs to him alone.

@list A15 autumn_overlook | Small things to try | Listen without distractions ;; Notice the moment before ;; Share music with someone
If you'd like to take something from tonight, here are three small things to try. First, now and then, listen to a piece of music without doing anything else at all. Second, notice the moment before the moment, the waiting, the hush, the feeling of something about to arrive. And third, share music with someone. Sing, or simply listen together, side by side.

@char A20 cafe_sunset | Just listening | No screen, no hurry
Listening without distractions sounds simple, but it's rare these days. So often, music plays in the background while we scroll, cook or work. Try, once in a while, to give a song your whole attention. Close your eyes if you like. Let it unfold from beginning to end. You may notice details you've never heard before, and perhaps even a shiver of your own.

@char A10 bedroom_night | The moment before | And the sharing
And notice the waiting. Remember that the brain's reward system was already busy before the peak, in those quiet seconds of anticipation. The hush is part of the music too. Then, when you can, share it. Play a song for a friend. Sing along with someone, even badly. Music has always been something people do together, from lullabies to choirs to friends in a kitchen.
