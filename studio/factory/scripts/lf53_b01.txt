@char N20 cafe_sunset | The scientific name | Involuntary musical imagery
Researchers have a more careful name for this. They call it involuntary musical imagery, often shortened to I N M I. Musical imagery means hearing music in your mind when no music is playing. Involuntary means it arrives on its own, rather than because you decided to imagine a song. The everyday word, earworm, is the one most people use, and it's the one we'll mostly use tonight.

@char N09 living_room_day | Two kinds of hearing | On purpose, or on its own
It helps to separate two things. If I asked you to imagine the opening of a song you know well, you could probably do it. That's voluntary musical imagery, and you're in charge of it. An earworm is different. It starts by itself, it tends to repeat a short section, and it can be hard to stop. The two use much of the same mental machinery, but they feel very different.

@list N27 rooftop_dusk | What an earworm usually is | A short fragment, not a whole song ;; A tune you already know ;; Repeated in a loop ;; Started without choosing it
Here's the shape of a typical earworm, as researchers describe it. It's usually a short fragment, often a chorus or a hook, rather than a whole song. It's nearly always a tune the person already knows. It repeats, sometimes for minutes and sometimes for much longer. And it starts on its own. Ruth's chorus ticks every one of these boxes.

@char N07 cafe_sunset | Not a problem to fix | Mostly harmless
One thing worth knowing from the start: for most people, earworms are an ordinary part of mental life, not a sign that anything is wrong. Researchers study them because they're a wonderful window into how memory works, not because they're a disorder. Ruth's chorus is mildly annoying, perhaps, but it's also a small demonstration of something remarkable that her brain does every single day.
