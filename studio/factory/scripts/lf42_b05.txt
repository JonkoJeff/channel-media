Some weeks have passed since the move, and the leaves outside Ines's window have turned from green to gold. She has been thinking about mornings. The flat is close to a park she hasn't visited yet, and she keeps picturing herself there, in a warm coat, with a hot drink, watching the light come up. So one evening she makes a decision. Starting tomorrow, she will walk before work, every single morning.

@char A12 street_day | The sincere version | She really means it
And she means it. That matters. This isn't one of those vague wishes made half-heartedly. She sets out her coat. She finds her scarf. She looks up how long the walk to the park takes, and works out the time she'd need to leave. She goes to bed feeling lighter than she has in weeks, with the quiet glow that comes from a good decision, freshly made and fully believed. It's one of the nicest feelings there is, and it deserves to be enjoyed rather than doubted.

@char A10 bedroom_night | The alarm | Quarter to seven
The alarm goes off at quarter to seven, in the grey half-dark. Ines opens one eye. The room is warm, and the world outside the duvet is not. She thinks of the park, of the scarf, of the plan. She still believes in all of it. And her hand, without a great deal of discussion, reaches out and presses snooze. Ten minutes later, it happens again. By then, the walk has quietly turned into a walk some other morning. There's no drama in it. The day simply begins, a little later than planned, and a little more like yesterday.

@char A19 cabin_fireplace | No villain here | Same person, same plan
What's interesting is that nothing about Ines changed overnight. The Ines who made the plan and the Ines who pressed snooze are the same person, with the same wishes. She didn't stop caring. She didn't turn lazy between ten at night and seven in the morning. Something else is going on, and it's so common that psychologists have given it a long and patient study. It's often called the gap between intending and doing. If you've ever felt it, you are in very good company.

@chapter A03 living_room_day | A natural assumption
Now, let's go a little deeper into that gap. A very natural idea goes like this. If we want people to do something, we should change what they intend. Help them see the reasons, build their confidence, make the goal feel important, and the behavior will follow. It's a hopeful idea, and it's behind a great many health campaigns, classroom talks and well-meaning pep talks. But is it actually true? A group of researchers decided to look at the evidence. It sounds so reasonable that for a long time few people thought to check.

@quote A13 attic_rain | I really intend to. | Said by nearly everyone, sincerely
We say it with such honesty. I really intend to. And in that moment it's completely true. The difficulty is that an intention is a statement about the future, made by a mind that is calm, rested and comfortable. The moment of action belongs to a different mind, sitting in a different situation, perhaps cold, sleepy or distracted. Those two minds don't always agree, even when they live in the same body.

@study A18 living_room_day | Webb and Sheeran | Psychological Bulletin, published 2006 | A medium-to-large change in intention led to a small-to-medium change in behavior
Forty-seven experiments sit behind this next study. Webb and Sheeran gathered studies in which researchers had actually managed to change people's intentions, and then checked what people did afterward. The answer was sobering, and also quite kind. A medium-to-large change in intention led to only a small-to-medium change in behavior. People did move, but much less than their intentions had.

@stat A15 cafe_sunset | Experiments combined | 47 | tests of changing intentions
Here is what they brought together. Forty-seven experimental tests, each one designed so that intentions were changed on purpose, rather than simply measured. That's important. It means the question was not whether people who want things tend to do them, which is easy to find. It was whether changing the wanting itself would change the doing. The answer was yes, a little, and a good deal less than you might hope. The researchers didn't treat this as bad news. They treated it as a clue.

@char A12 cafe_sunset | Reading it gently | Intention is a start
It would be easy to take that the wrong way. The finding is not that intentions don't matter. They clearly do, and they moved behavior in these experiments. The finding is that they are only part of the picture. Wanting to walk each morning is a lovely beginning, and a necessary one. But it seems not to be the whole machine, which is why even the most sincere plans can still end with a hand on the snooze button. And that's a relief, in its way, because it means the problem isn't us.

@list A10 attic_rain | Everyday reasons a plan slips | We forget it at the key moment ;; Something easier is already there ;; The old routine starts first ;; We are tired when it counts
These are not findings from that study, just ordinary reasons that anyone can recognize. We forget the plan at the very moment it's needed. Something easier is already waiting, like a warm bed. The old routine starts before the new one has a chance. And the moment of action often arrives when we're tired. None of these is a character flaw. They're simply what mornings are like. Anyone who has tried to leave the house early on a cold day knows exactly what we mean.

Picture what Ines left by the door the night before. A pair of shoes with the laces loosened. A scarf folded over the top. It looks so ready. So full of promise. And in the grey light of morning, it stays exactly as she left it, patient and uncomplaining, with no hint of blame. The plan is still good. It's just waiting for something more than a good intention to carry it out. Perhaps a little help from the room around it.

@chapter A19 rooftop_dusk | The snooze button is a habit too
There's another way to see her morning, and it connects with everything we've explored so far. Pressing snooze is itself a habit. It has a cue, the alarm. It has an action, the thumb on the screen. And it has a reward, a few more minutes of warmth. It has been practiced hundreds of times, in the same room, at the same hour. So it's no wonder it can feel as if it happens by itself.

@char A13 cabin_fireplace | Two things at once | A plan versus a path
So in the half-dark, Ines isn't really choosing between her plan and her laziness. She's meeting an old, well-worn path with a brand new wish. The path has years of repetition behind it. The wish has one evening of sincere resolve. Seen that way, it's less a failure of will and more a mismatch of strength, and a mismatch is something we can work with, rather than something to feel bad about. It's a bit like trying to turn a large ship with a very small paddle. The paddle isn't wrong. It just needs a better plan.

@char A15 living_room_day | A kinder question | Not why, but how
Which suggests a kinder question. Instead of asking, why can't I just do what I mean to do, we might ask how a good intention could be given some help. Perhaps by making it easier to start. Perhaps by giving the plan a cue of its own, in a place where cues already exist. We'll come to those ideas later. For now, it's enough to simply notice how much more gently this question treats us. Sometimes just changing the question is the kindest thing we can do.

@quote A10 cabin_fireplace | Intention is the seed, not the tree. | A gentle way to remember it
It's only a small saying, and not from any study. But it can be a comfortable thing to remember. The intention is the seed, and a seed is a wonderful thing to have. Nobody blames a seed for needing soil, and light, and time. It simply means the work isn't finished when the decision is made. It's only just beginning, and it can begin quite softly.

@list A19 living_room_day | Tonight's quiet takeaways | Intentions matter, but they're only a start ;; Changing intentions changed behavior less than expected ;; Sincere people still hit snooze ;; Habits can help a plan along
Let's gather a few small thoughts. Intentions matter, but they're only a start. In a large collection of experiments, changing what people intended changed what they did, though by less. Sincere, kind and capable people still press snooze. And habits, which can work against a plan, can also be turned into a quiet ally. Nothing here asks you to try harder. It asks you to look a little more closely. And you can do that tomorrow, or the next day, or whenever you wake.

@char A03 attic_rain | Back to Ines | Quarter past seven
Ines is sitting on the edge of her bed at quarter past seven, with the alarm finally silent. She isn't cross. She laughs a little, in fact, at how well the snooze button knows her. She looks at the scarf by the door and thinks, not tomorrow, but not never either. Something in this old room, she suspects, is as much a part of her mornings as she is. And so she starts wondering about the room itself.

@char A18 cabin_fireplace | The room itself | Where habits live
That thought turns out to be a very useful one. Because if habits are triggered by what surrounds us, then the surroundings may matter more than we've been giving them credit for. The bed, the light, the sounds of the street, the little sequence of things that happens each morning. Perhaps they're not just the stage where we act. Perhaps they're part of what tells the hand what to do next.
