@list N29 street_day | Two questions, not one | Why do we yawn at all ;; Why does another person's yawn set ours off
It helps to separate two questions that often get tangled together. The first is why we yawn at all, on our own, when nobody else is doing it. Researchers call that spontaneous yawning. The second is why seeing, hearing or even reading about a yawn can make us yawn. That's called contagious yawning, and it seems to follow some rules of its own.

@char A03 living_room_day | Spontaneous yawns | The old, private kind
The spontaneous kind appears very early in life and is shared widely across the animal world. Dogs do it on the rug. Cats do it after a nap. Many other animals with backbones open their jaws wide in ways that look remarkably familiar. Whatever this movement does, it seems to be old, far older than human language, trains, classrooms or parents' evenings.

@char A12 cafe_sunset | Contagious yawns | The social kind
The contagious kind is different. It shows up much later in childhood, it varies a great deal from one adult to another, and it has been clearly seen in only a limited number of animal species. That contrast is one of the most interesting clues we have. It suggests that catching a yawn asks something extra of the brain, on top of simply being able to yawn.

@stat A10 cabin_fireplace | A commonly quoted range | 40-60% | of adults are often said to catch yawns from videos
You'll often read that somewhere between forty and sixty percent of adults catch a yawn when they watch other people yawning. Treat that as a rough range rather than a law of nature. As we'll see, the figure moves quite a lot depending on how long people watch, how it's measured, who is in the room, and how old the watchers are.
