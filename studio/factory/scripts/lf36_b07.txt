Before we answer that question about another reading, let's go back a few hours in Sam's day. Because there's something he hasn't really noticed yet. Around midday, a meeting ran long. Then there was an email that couldn't wait. And somewhere between the two, lunch quietly disappeared. He had a coffee. He meant to grab something later. Later never came.

@char A13 living_room_day | Running on empty | And not noticing
So when that message from his brother arrives on the bus, Sam isn't just reading it with his eyes. He's reading it with a body that hasn't eaten properly since breakfast. And that, it turns out, may matter a lot more than any of us like to admit. Hunger doesn't announce itself as hunger. Sometimes it arrives dressed up as something else.

@char A10 cabin_fireplace | A word for it | Hungry plus angry
There's even a word for this now. Hangry. Hungry and angry, folded into one. It sounds like a joke, the kind of thing you say about a friend who gets snappy before dinner. But a few researchers decided to take the joke seriously. And what they found was more interesting than the word suggests.

@study A12 cafe_sunset | Bushman, DeWall, Pond and Hanus | Married couples, followed for 21 days | published in 2014
In 2014, the psychologist Brad Bushman and his colleagues Nathan DeWall, Richard Pond and Michael Hanus published a study in the journal PNAS. They followed one hundred and seven married couples for twenty-one days. Every morning and every evening, each person measured their own blood sugar, their glucose level.

@char A05 autumn_overlook | A rather strange tool | A doll and some pins
And then came the unusual part. Each person was given a little doll, and told that it represented their husband or wife. Each night, in private, they could stick pins into that doll, anywhere from none at all to fifty-one, depending on how angry they felt with their partner that day. It's an odd image, I know. But it was a simple, private way of asking, how annoyed are you tonight?

@stat A13 cafe_sunset | Bushman and colleagues, 2014 | 51 | pins each person could put into a doll of their spouse, every night for 21 days
Here's what they found. On evenings when people's glucose was lower, they tended to put more pins into the doll. And this held even after the researchers took into account how happy people were in their marriage. So it wasn't simply that unhappy couples had both low sugar and lots of pins. Lower evening glucose went along with more anger, in otherwise ordinary relationships.

@char A03 living_room_day | Then, in the lab | Louder and longer
Later, the couples came into the lab and played a game against their spouse, where the winner could blast the other with an unpleasant noise through headphones. It was a lab game, of course. But the people whose average evening glucose had been lower chose louder and longer blasts for their husband or wife.

@char A18 cafe_sunset | Not the whole story | But a real piece
Now, this doesn't mean that low blood sugar makes anyone a bad partner. It means that our patience may lean, a little, on the body's fuel. Self-control takes effort, and a tired, hungry body has less to give. Which brings us to a second study, and to a twist I find quite lovely.

Picture a simple bowl of soup at the end of a long day. Nothing fancy. Just something warm. It's strange to think that something this small might change how a text message sounds. But hold that thought, because that's exactly the question the next researchers asked.

@study A12 living_room_day | MacCormack and Lindquist | University of North Carolina | published in 2019
In 2019, two psychologists, Jennifer MacCormack and Kristen Lindquist, published a set of experiments in the journal Emotion, with a wonderfully honest title. Feeling hangry? When hunger is conceptualized as emotion. They wanted to know not just whether hunger makes us crankier, but when, and for whom.

@list A15 cafe_sunset | The first experiments | More than 400 adults online ;; Some hungry, some not ;; Ambiguous images ;; Rate how they feel
First, they ran experiments online with more than four hundred adults in the United States. People were shown an image that set a mood, sometimes pleasant, sometimes negative, and then a strange, ambiguous picture that could be read either way. Then they were asked how they felt about that ambiguous picture.

@char A10 autumn_overlook | Only after a nudge | Not all the time
The hungry people didn't rate everything more negatively. That's important. They only rated the ambiguous pictures more harshly when they'd just seen something negative. Hunger on its own wasn't the spark. But when there was already a little shadow in the room, hunger seemed to make that shadow look darker.

@char A13 living_room_day | Sound familiar? | An ambiguous message
And think about Sam. You forgot again? That's an ambiguous message if ever there was one. It could be teasing. It could be tired. It could be hurt. And it arrived after a long, frustrating day, in a stomach that had been empty since the morning. A small shadow, and a hungry reader.

@study A12 cafe_sunset | MacCormack and Lindquist | More than 200 students in the lab | published in 2019
Then came the lab experiment. More than two hundred university students came in. Some had fasted beforehand, and some had eaten. They worked on a computer task, and near the end, the computer crashed. Then a researcher came in and, rather unfairly, suggested it was the student's fault.

@char A05 autumn_overlook | Blamed for a crash | That wasn't theirs
Afterwards, the students filled in questionnaires about how they felt, and how they rated the researcher. And the hungry students did report feeling worse. They judged the researcher more harshly too. So far, that's what you'd expect. But there was one more group in the study, and they're the most interesting of all.

@list A15 cafe_sunset | The hidden difference | Hungry and not noticing ;; More unpleasant feelings ;; Harsher judgement ;; Hungry and noticing ;; No bigger effect
Before the crash, some of the students had been asked to stop and think about their own emotions for a moment. Just to notice them. And for those students, hunger didn't have the same effect. The hungry students who weren't paying attention to their feelings were the ones who got more upset. The hungry students who had paused to notice their feelings did not.

@quote A10 cabin_fireplace | Noticing the feeling changed what the feeling did. | The twist in the hangry study
That's the twist. Being hungry wasn't enough on its own. What seemed to matter was whether people mistook that hunger for something else. If you don't notice you're hungry, the unpleasant feeling in your body goes looking for a reason. And very often, the nearest reason is the person in front of you.

@char A03 living_room_day | Where the feeling lands | On whoever is closest
In other words, the body says, something is wrong. And the mind, trying to be helpful, points at the first likely culprit. The researcher. The partner. Or a brother who sent a short message at exactly the wrong moment.

@quote A18 cafe_sunset | When did I last eat? | A quiet question for the bus
So here's a small question Sam could ask himself, thumb still hovering over send. Not, is my brother being unfair? But, when did I last eat? It sounds almost too simple to matter. And yet, if those students are anything to go by, just noticing it might take some of the heat out of the moment.

@char A12 cabin_fireplace | Berkowitz again | Any unpleasant feeling
If this sounds familiar, it's because we met the idea a little earlier tonight. Leonard Berkowitz suggested that frustration feeds anger mainly when it feels unpleasant, and that almost any unpleasant feeling can add fuel. Hunger is one of the most ordinary unpleasant feelings there is. It's low, and quiet, and easy to overlook. Which is exactly what makes it so good at hiding inside a mood. You don't feel hungry, exactly. You just feel that everyone is being a little bit difficult today.

@list A15 autumn_overlook | Not an excuse | The feeling of anger ;; The reason for anger ;; Not always the same thing
This isn't about excusing anything. Sometimes people really do treat us badly, whether we've eaten or not. But it's worth knowing that the feeling of anger and the reason for anger aren't always the same thing. Sometimes the reason is a person. And sometimes the reason is lunch.

@char A13 living_room_day | And one more thing | Five hours of sleep
And lunch isn't the only thing Sam skipped. Remember how his night went. About five hours of sleep, and a morning that started too early. Hunger is one quiet influence on our tempers. Tiredness is another. So let's follow Sam a little further back, to the night before.
