@chapter A13 autumn_overlook | Laughter at the wrong moment
Nadia remembers that assembly very clearly, even now. She was about fourteen, and the head teacher was giving a solemn speech about the school's history. A pigeon had got into the hall and was walking slowly across the stage behind him. Nobody else seemed to notice. Nadia did, and from that moment, she was lost.

@char A12 street_day | The giggles | The more you try, the worse it gets
You may know the feeling. The more she tried not to laugh, the worse it got. She bit her lip, stared at her shoes, thought about sad things. Her shoulders shook. Her eyes streamed. Her friend beside her caught it too. It was agony and joy at the same time, and it's one of the most familiar, and least studied, experiences of human laughter.

@char A03 bedroom_night | Why holding it in fails | Pressure builds
Why does trying to stop make it worse? Researchers don't have a firm answer. One idea is that the effort of holding it in keeps our attention fixed on the very thing that's funny. Another is that a serious setting makes a harmless absurdity even more out of place, adding to the sense of something wrong but safe. Either way, it's a reminder that laughter isn't fully under our control.

@char A04 neon_alley_rain | Laughter isn't only joy | Nervous laughter
And laughter doesn't only come from amusement. People laugh when they're nervous, embarrassed, relieved or even frightened. Most of us have heard someone laugh at bad news, or giggle in a tense situation, and felt confused by it. This nervous laughter seems to come from a different place than the joyful kind, though it can sound very similar.

@study A19 bedroom_night | Stanley Milgram | Journal of Abnormal and Social Psychology, 1963 | Behavioral study of obedience
One of the most famous examples comes from a very serious study. In 1963, the psychologist Stanley Milgram published his first report on obedience, in which volunteers were told to give what they believed were electric shocks to another person. No real shocks were given, but the volunteers didn't know that. Many found it deeply stressful.

@stat A10 rooftop_dusk | Volunteers who showed nervous laughter | 14 of 40 | in Milgram's first obedience report
Milgram reported something he hadn't expected. Fourteen of the forty volunteers in that first report showed clear signs of nervous laughter and smiling, even though they were obviously distressed. A few had laughing fits they couldn't control. Afterwards, they insisted they hadn't found anything funny at all. The laughter seemed to be a sign of tension, not amusement.

@char N01 attic_rain | A release valve? | Or a social signal
Why would people laugh when they're upset? One suggestion is that nervous laughter might help to calm us, or signal to others that we're not a threat, or that we don't want to make a situation worse. Another is that it's simply an overflow of strong feeling. Researchers are still working this out, and the answer may be different in different situations.

@study A20 cafe_sunset | Itzhak Fried and colleagues | Nature, 1998 | Electric current stimulates laughter
Some of the strangest evidence comes from inside the brain itself. In 1998, the neurosurgeon Itzhak Fried and colleagues reported on a teenage girl who was having brain surgery to treat her epilepsy. As part of the careful preparation for surgery, doctors gently stimulated small areas of her brain with weak electric currents, to map what each area did.

@char N29 street_day | A laugh on demand | And a reason invented
When they stimulated one particular spot near the top of the brain, the girl laughed. With stronger currents, the laughter became fuller and more infectious. And each time, she offered a reason. She said things like, you guys are just so funny, standing around. Once, she said a picture of a horse she was looking at was funny. But the reason came after the laugh.

@char N14 neon_alley_rain | The mind explains itself | A story after the fact
That small report says something important. The laughter came first, set off directly by the electrical stimulation. The explanation came afterwards, as her mind tried to make sense of what her body was doing. It's a striking example of something psychologists have seen in many settings: we often explain our own feelings after the fact, and believe our explanations.

An empty school hall at night, its stage lit by a single lamp, rows of chairs in shadow, a few feathers on the floor. It reminds Nadia of that long-ago assembly and the pigeon. Halls like this, built for serious occasions, are often where the most helpless, forbidden giggles of childhood happen.

@char N09 rooftop_dusk | When laughter becomes a symptom | Rare, but real
In rare cases, laughter can be a sign that something is wrong. Some neurological conditions can cause sudden bursts of laughter or crying that don't match how a person feels, and some kinds of seizure involve laughter. These are uncommon, and they're very different from ordinary giggles. If laughter ever seems out of control, or unconnected to any feeling, that's a reason to see a doctor.

@list N20 cabin_fireplace | The many faces of laughter | Joy and play ;; Politeness and friendliness ;; Nerves and embarrassment ;; Relief after tension ;; Rarely, a medical symptom
So laughter has many faces. It can come from joy and play. From politeness and friendliness. From nerves and embarrassment. From relief after tension. And, rarely, from a medical condition. The same sound can carry very different meanings, which is why the context, and the person, matter so much when we try to understand it.

@char A03 cafe_sunset | Laughing at serious moments | Why it isn't disrespect
This also helps explain something that can feel uncomfortable: laughter at funerals, in hospitals or in moments of grief. People sometimes feel ashamed of laughing at such times. But laughter in hard moments is common, and it isn't usually disrespect. Often it's a release, or a shared memory of someone loved, or simply the body's way of coping with too much feeling at once.

@char A15 cabin_fireplace | Nadia and the pigeon | A memory she treasures
Nadia, as it happens, was kept behind after that assembly and given a short talk about respect. She nodded seriously the whole time, and then laughed all the way home with her friend. Thirty years later, the two of them still mention the pigeon whenever they meet. Forbidden laughter, it seems, can turn into a lifelong bond.

@char A13 bedroom_night | Out of our control | And that's part of the gift
There's something oddly reassuring in all this. Laughter isn't fully under our control. It bursts out at the wrong moments, it sometimes comes from nerves rather than joy, and it can even be set off by a gentle electric current. But that's also part of what makes it so honest. A real laugh is hard to fake, and hard to hide.

@char A04 attic_rain | Next question | Laughter that spread through a region
And laughter that's out of control can spread, too, sometimes far beyond a single room. There's one remarkable episode in history, in East Africa in the 1960s, when laughter seemed to spread from a single school to whole villages. It's a strange, sometimes misunderstood story, and it's where we're going next.
