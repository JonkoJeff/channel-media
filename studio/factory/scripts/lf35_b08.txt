@chapter A19 rooftop_dusk | Do interruptions spoil things?
Imagine you're in the middle of something lovely. A favourite programme, a warm bath, a long phone call with an old friend. And then, right in the middle, it's interrupted. Most of us would say that ruins it a little. Good things, we feel, should flow on without a break. That's our next myth. Interruptions spoil good things.

@char A13 living_room_day | Nora's habit | The window, all the time
Nora had the opposite of an interruption. Her window was always there. Every morning, every evening, every grey afternoon, the sea filled the glass. And that, strangely, might have been part of the problem. A pleasure that never pauses is a pleasure the brain can fully get used to. There was never a moment when the view went away, and so there was never a moment when it came back.

@char A03 cafe_sunset | Adaptation needs continuity | A smooth road
Remember how adaptation works. The brain notices change and quietly files away what stays the same. A steady pleasure is, in a sense, the easiest thing in the world to adapt to. So what would happen if you broke that steadiness, even for a moment? Would it spoil the pleasure, as most of us expect? Or might it reset the clock?

@study A18 cafe_sunset | Leif Nelson and Tom Meyvis | Published 2008 | Interrupted consumption: disrupting adaptation to pleasant experiences
In 2008, two researchers, Leif Nelson and Tom Meyvis, published a set of experiments on exactly this question. They called their paper Interrupted consumption. In one study, people enjoyed a massage from a massage chair. For some of them, the massage ran without a pause. For others, it was briefly interrupted in the middle, and then started again.

@char A10 living_room_day | The surprising result | The break helped
You'd expect the uninterrupted massage to be the more pleasant one. It was smoother, and in total it gave just as much massage time. But people whose massage had a short break enjoyed it more. The interruption seemed to undo some of the adaptation that had built up, so that when the massage resumed, it felt fresh again.

@list A12 cafe_sunset | What else they found | Breaks in a television programme made it more enjoyable ;; People predicted the breaks would make things worse ;; The effect was for pleasant experiences that people adapt to
They found something similar with television. People watching a programme with short breaks enjoyed it more than people who watched it straight through. And here's the familiar twist. When people were asked to predict, they expected the breaks to make the experience worse. Once again, our intuitions about happiness pointed in the wrong direction.

@char A19 attic_rain | Why it works | Resetting the baseline
Why would this happen? Think of it like stepping out of a warm bath for a moment and then sliding back in. For a few seconds, the warmth feels wonderful again. The pause didn't spoil the pleasure. It reminded the body that the pleasure was there. The interruption gave adaptation a nudge backwards.

@photo lf35_b08_01.jpg | A small square of chocolate
Now, let's try a slightly bolder idea. If a short break can freshen a pleasure, what about giving something up altogether for a while? It's an idea with a long history in many traditions. And in 2013, two psychologists decided to test it in the most delicious way imaginable. With chocolate.

@study A13 cabin_fireplace | Jordi Quoidbach and Elizabeth Dunn | Published 2013, Social Psychological and Personality Science | Give it up: a strategy for combating hedonic adaptation
The researchers were Jordi Quoidbach and Elizabeth Dunn, and their paper was called Give it up. Students came into the lab and ate a piece of chocolate, rating how much they enjoyed it. Then they went home for a week with one of three sets of instructions.

@list A10 living_room_day | Three groups, one week | Give up chocolate completely ;; Eat as much chocolate as you like ;; No instructions at all
Some were asked to give up chocolate completely for the week. Some were given a large supply of chocolate and encouraged to eat as much as they liked. And others got no special instructions at all. One week later, everyone came back to the lab and tasted another piece of the same chocolate.

@char A12 autumn_overlook | The result | Absence made it sweeter
The students who had given up chocolate for a week enjoyed that second piece more than the others did. The ones who had been eating plenty enjoyed it the least. Same chocolate, same lab, a very different pleasure. A short absence had made it new again. Abundance had quietly worn it down.

@char A03 cabin_fireplace | Why absence works | Room to want again
Why would a week without chocolate make chocolate taste better? Part of the answer is adaptation again. Every piece you eat makes the next one a little more ordinary, a little more expected. A week away lets that expectation fade. And part of it may be anticipation. When something is missing for a while, you start to look forward to it, and that looking forward becomes part of the pleasure when it finally returns.

@quote A18 cafe_sunset | Temporarily giving up something you enjoy may help you enjoy it more. | The idea tested by Quoidbach and Dunn, 2013
It's worth stating the idea plainly, because it runs against so much of what we're told. Temporarily giving up something you enjoy may help you enjoy it more. Not forever, and not as a punishment. Just long enough to let the brain notice it again.

@chapter A05 cabin_fireplace | The pleasure of not knowing
There's one more surprising way to keep a good feeling alive a little longer. It has to do with mystery. Most of us like to understand the good things that happen to us. We like to know why. But understanding, it turns out, may be one of the things that helps a good feeling fade.

@study A18 living_room_day | Wilson, Centerbar, Kermer and Gilbert | Published 2005, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology | The pleasures of uncertainty
In 2005, Timothy Wilson, David Centerbar, Deborah Kermer and Daniel Gilbert published a paper called The pleasures of uncertainty. In one study, people sitting in a university library were unexpectedly handed a small card with a tiny gift from a stranger. For some, the card clearly explained who had sent it and why. For others, the reason was left unclear.

@char A13 cabin_fireplace | Mystery lingered | The good mood lasted
A few minutes later, the people who didn't know why they'd been given the gift were in a better mood than the people who did. The researchers' explanation was simple. When we understand a pleasant event, we file it away and move on. When something stays a little mysterious, we keep thinking about it, and the warm feeling lingers. And, once again, people didn't predict this. Most of us would rather know.

@list A12 cafe_sunset | Why mystery lingers | We keep wondering about it ;; The moment stays unfinished ;; The warm feeling gets more time
So a little mystery seems to keep a good moment alive. We keep wondering about it. Who was it? Why me? The moment stays slightly unfinished, like a story without its last page, and the mind keeps returning to it. And each time it returns, the warm feeling gets a little more time to linger before it is filed away.

@char A20 rooftop_dusk | Nora tries an experiment | Curtains closed
So Nora decided to try a small experiment of her own. For one week, she kept the curtains closed in the mornings. She made her tea in the half dark, got ready for the day, and left without looking at the sea at all. It felt a little silly. But at the end of the week, on a Sunday morning, she opened the curtains wide.

@char A02 autumn_overlook | The sea came back | Almost like the first night
And there it was. The light on the water, the long line of the horizon, a few gulls drifting past. Not quite the same as her very first night, but closer than it had felt in months. A short break had made the view hers again. The pause hadn't spoiled anything. It had given her something back.

@list A12 living_room_day | Small ways to break adaptation | Pause a pleasure in the middle ;; Give something up for a short while ;; Leave a little mystery in good moments ;; Return to things after a break
So here are a few small, research based ideas. Let a pleasure pause in the middle, rather than rushing through it. Give up something you love for a little while, and then come back to it. Leave a little mystery in good moments instead of explaining them away. Each of these gently pushes back against the quiet work of adaptation.

@char A10 cabin_fireplace | Not a perfect fix | Effects are modest
Of course, these were small experiments, with things like massages and chocolate and television, and the effects were real but modest. They aren't magic tricks that will make every pleasure last forever. Adaptation is a deep part of how the brain works, and that's a good thing. But they show that adaptation isn't a one way street. Sometimes, with a small pause, a faded joy can come back.

And there's one more habit that tries to do this every single day. It's so familiar that many people roll their eyes when they hear it. It's printed on mugs and greeting cards, and repeated so often that it can sound like a cliché. Counting your blessings. Being grateful. But is gratitude really just a nice saying? Or does the science say something more?
