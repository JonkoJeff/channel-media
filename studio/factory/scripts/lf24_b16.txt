@chapter A13 rooftop_dusk | What actually helps
We've looked at the pull of the feed, the reward of a like, the habit of comparison, the cost of interruption, the spread of stories, and the toll on sleep. Now the practical question. What can a person do about it, tomorrow morning, that the evidence actually supports? The good news is that the answers are modest, and doable.

@char A12 living_room_day | A shift in thinking | From willpower to design
The most important idea in this chapter is that the answer isn't mainly about willpower. The research on habits and self-control suggests that people who do well don't resist temptation harder. They arrange their lives so that there's less to resist. That's a more hopeful message, because design can be changed in minutes.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Wood, Quinn & Kashy | Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 2002 | Habits in everyday life: thought, emotion, and action
Wendy Wood and her colleagues asked people to keep diaries of what they did, at random times during the day. They found that about forty-three percent of everyday actions were performed almost daily in the same context, and often while thinking about something else. A large part of what we do is habit, set off by place, time and cue, more than by a decision.

@stat A14 conspiracy_board | Everyday actions that were habitual | About 43% | in a diary study, performed almost daily in the same setting
If so much of life is habit, then the cue matters. A phone on the bedside table is a cue. A notification is a cue. The sofa after dinner is a cue. Change the cue, and you change the habit more reliably than by a good intention. This is the foundation for the first few tools.

@chapter A06 cafe_sunset | Tool one: make it harder
The first tool is friction, which we met at the start of the evening. Add a little effort to the things you want to do less. Log out of the apps after each use. Move them off the home screen. Keep the phone in another room while you work, or while you sleep. None of this is dramatic, and each step takes only a minute.

@list A03 living_room_day | Friction you can add | Remove the apps from your home screen ;; Log out after each session ;; Charge the phone outside the bedroom ;; Turn off non-essential notifications ;; Use a plain alarm clock
The evidence for each is partly indirect, from the work on habits, attention and sleep we've seen. But the logic is consistent, and many people find a noticeable difference within days.

@chapter A12 living_room_day | Tool two: decide in advance
The second tool is a plan. In 2006, Peter Gollwitzer and Paschal Sheeran reviewed ninety-four studies of a technique called implementation intentions. The idea is to turn a vague goal into an if-then plan: if this situation happens, then I'll do this specific thing.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Gollwitzer & Sheeran | Advances in Experimental Social Psychology, 2006 | Implementation intentions and goal achievement: a meta-analysis of effects and processes
Across ninety-four independent tests, people who made if-then plans were considerably more likely to reach their goals than those who only set an intention, with a medium-to-large effect. The plan seems to hand control of the action to the situation, so that you don't have to decide in the moment, when you're tired or tempted.

@list A12 living_room_day | If-then plans for the feed | If I feel the urge to check, then I'll take three slow breaths first ;; If it's after ten at night, then the phone goes in the kitchen ;; If I open the app without a reason, then I'll close it and stretch ;; If I want to share something, then I'll wait a minute first
Plans like these are simple, specific and kind to yourself. They don't rely on an iron will. They give your future, slightly tired self a script to follow.

@chapter A13 rooftop_dusk | Tool three: set limits and take breaks
The third tool comes from the experiments we saw earlier. Hunt's team found that limiting use to ten minutes per platform helped students feel less lonely and less depressed. Allcott's team found that four weeks without Facebook brought small improvements in mood and more time with family and friends. Neither is a miracle, but both were real.

@char A04 attic_rain | A sensible approach | Try it as an experiment
You don't need to commit to a lifelong change. Try a two-week limit, or a weekend off, and notice what changes. Keep a note of your mood, your sleep and your time. If you feel better, you've learned something about your own life that no study could tell you. If you don't, you've lost nothing.

@char A01 cafe_sunset | A note on kindness | Be gentle on slips
When you slip, which you will, the research on habits suggests that a single slip makes little difference. It's the way you talk to yourself afterwards that counts. Harsh self-criticism tends to make people give up. A calm, curious response, such as noticing what led to the slip, is more likely to help you try again.

@chapter A06 cafe_sunset | Tool four: choose your feed
The fourth tool is about what you see. The studies of comparison and contagion suggest that who you follow matters. Mute accounts that leave you feeling small. Follow people who teach you things or make you laugh. And make a habit of reaching out to the people who matter, since active, personal use was the kind most linked to wellbeing.

@list A03 living_room_day | Shaping the feed | Mute or unfollow accounts that leave you drained ;; Follow people you can learn from ;; Send one real message a day ;; Pause before you share anything that stirs strong emotion
These are cheap and quick, and they work in the direction that the evidence points. They don't promise that your feed will be perfect. They shift the balance a little, day after day.

@photo lf24_b16_01.jpg | A calm morning table
A calm morning, a notebook, and a phone left face down by the window. This is what all of the research tends to point towards: not an escape from technology, but a life arranged so that the technology serves you, and the people and things that matter come first.

@list A03 living_room_day | Your first week | Day one: turn off non-essential notifications ;; Day three: charge the phone outside the bedroom ;; Day five: make one if-then plan ;; Day seven: review what changed and keep what helped
If all of this feels like a lot, here is a week to try. On the first day, turn off notifications you don't need. On the third, move the phone's charger out of the bedroom. On the fifth, write one if-then plan for a moment when you tend to scroll. And on the seventh, look back, and decide what to keep. It's gentle on purpose. The research on habits suggests that small, specific and repeatable changes outlast big dramatic ones, and that they leave you in charge.

@list A12 living_room_day | Words to keep from this chapter | Implementation intention ;; Habit cue ;; Friction ;; Self-monitoring
Four terms from this final chapter. An implementation intention is an if-then plan. A habit cue is a trigger that sets off a routine. Friction is the effort that stands between you and an action. And self-monitoring means observing your own behaviour, which is the first step to changing it.

@char A19 cabin_fireplace | A fair last word | No villain, no saviour
After sixteen chapters, here is a fair summary. Social media isn't a poison and it isn't a cure. It's a powerful set of tools built on old human instincts: for rewards, for belonging, for comparison, for news. It can feed us or drain us, depending on how it's built and how we use it, and that gives us more power than the headlines suggest.

@char A15 cabin_fireplace | A gentle thought | You are allowed to log off
You are allowed to put the phone down and look out of the window. You are allowed to be unreachable for an hour. And you're allowed to use these tools with pleasure and without guilt, for the things that they do well. What the evidence asks is only that you stay awake while you do it.

@char A08 cabin_fireplace | Looking back | What the whole evening taught us
So what did we learn? That feeds pull on very old systems of reward, belonging and comparison. That the average harm in large studies is small, though not for everyone. That attention, sleep and relationships are the places where it matters most. And that small changes in design and habit beat willpower.
