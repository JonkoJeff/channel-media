It's half past ten. Ruth has brushed her teeth, Biscuit is curled up in his basket at the foot of the bed, and the lights are off. The room is dark and quiet, and Ruth lies back on the pillow, ready to sleep. And then, of course, in the stillness, it begins. Not the folk tune this time. The supermarket chorus, bright and bouncy, as if it had been saving itself for this exact moment.

@char N20 living_room_day | The bedtime earworm | A familiar frustration
Many people know this experience. You're tired, the day is over, the house is quiet, and a song starts looping in your head just as you're trying to drift off. It's one of the most frustrating times to have an earworm, because there's nothing to distract you, and the harder you try to ignore it, the more awake you feel. For a long time, nobody had studied it scientifically.

@study N10 bedroom_night | Michael Scullin, Chenlu Gao and Paul Fillmore | Psychological Science, 2021 | Bedtime music, involuntary musical imagery, and sleep
That changed in 2021, when Michael Scullin, Chenlu Gao and Paul Fillmore, at Baylor University in Texas, published a study in Psychological Science called Bedtime music, involuntary musical imagery, and sleep. It combined a survey with a night in a sleep laboratory, and it produced some surprising results.

@char N09 living_room_day | The survey | Two hundred and nine people
The first part was a survey of two hundred and nine people. They answered questions about their sleep quality, how much music they listened to, when they listened to it, and how often they had earworms at night. The researchers wanted to know whether people who often had bedtime earworms also slept worse.

@stat N20 cafe_sunset | More likely to report poor sleep | 6x | with night earworms once a week or more
The answer was striking. People who had earworms at night once a week or more were about six times more likely to report poor sleep quality than people who rarely had them. That's a big difference. Of course, a survey like this can only show a link, not a cause. Poor sleepers might simply notice their earworms more. So the team moved into the laboratory.

@photo lf53_b15_01.jpg | A quiet bedroom at night with moonlight
A quiet bedroom at night, lit only by moonlight. This is where the bedtime earworm does its work. Without the distractions of the day, there's nothing else for the mind to attend to, and the hearing system, with no real sound to process, is free to replay whatever music is closest to hand. For Ruth tonight, that's the supermarket chorus.

@char N10 bedroom_night | A common habit | Music before bed
Think about evening habits for a moment. Many people listen to music in the evening, and some use it deliberately to help them fall asleep. Most people think of music as relaxing, and for some, it may well be. But music is also the raw material of earworms, which raises a fair question about music right before bed.

@char N09 attic_rain | The researchers' question | Does bedtime music cause earworms?
So Scullin and his colleagues asked a more specific question. If someone listens to catchy music just before going to sleep, does it make them more likely to have an earworm at night? And if they do have an earworm, does it actually change their sleep, as measured objectively in a laboratory, not just as they remember it in the morning?

@study N20 living_room_day | The sleep laboratory | Baylor University, 2021 | Fifty volunteers, monitored overnight
For the laboratory part of the study, fifty volunteers came to spend a night in the sleep lab. Before bed, they listened to three popular songs, either the original versions with lyrics or instrumental versions without lyrics. The songs were Shake It Off by Taylor Swift, Call Me Maybe by Carly Rae Jepsen, and Don't Stop Believin' by Journey.

@char N10 bedroom_night | Monitoring sleep | Polysomnography
Through the night, the volunteers' sleep was measured using polysomnography, a standard method in sleep research that records brain waves, eye movements, muscle activity and breathing. This gives a detailed, objective picture of how long it takes someone to fall asleep, how often they wake, and how much time they spend in each stage of sleep, from light to deep.

@char N09 attic_rain | Asking about earworms | At bedtime and in the night
The volunteers were also asked whether they had experienced an earworm while trying to fall asleep, and whether they woke up in the night or in the morning with a song in their heads. Some did both. The researchers could then compare the sleep of people who had a bedtime earworm with the sleep of people who didn't.

@list N27 rooftop_dusk | The songs played before bed | Shake It Off ;; Call Me Maybe ;; Don't Stop Believin'
The three songs were chosen because they're popular and very catchy. Each volunteer heard them in one form only, either the familiar version with the singer's voice and lyrics, or an instrumental version with the voice removed. That comparison, words versus no words, turned out to be one of the most interesting parts of the study.

@char N10 living_room_day | Ruth's own lab | No electrodes required
Ruth, of course, isn't wearing any electrodes. But she's having exactly the kind of night that the Baylor researchers were studying. She listened to the full song on her phone earlier this evening, which, as we'll see, may not have been the best idea right before bed. And now the chorus is playing as she tries to fall asleep.

@char N09 bedroom_night | A pause | Lying still
Ruth turns onto her side and pulls the blanket a little higher. She notices the chorus, but she doesn't fight it. She has had a long day, and she's tired, and a part of her is curious, in a sleepy sort of way, about how long it will keep going. Biscuit sighs in his basket. The room is warm and quiet.
