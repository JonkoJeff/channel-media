It's late, and the house is quiet, and suddenly the inner voice brings back something you said at a party three weeks ago. Why did I say that? What must they think of me? I always do this. The words are in your own voice, and yet they sound like someone who is very hard to please.

@char A13 cabin_fireplace | Round and round | The same loop
Most of us know this side of the inner voice. It replays, it judges, it predicts the worst. And it tends to go round in circles. Psychologists call this repetitive, stuck kind of thinking rumination, and the late Susan Nolen-Hoeksema spent much of her career showing that dwelling over and over on problems and feelings tends to make low moods deeper and longer, not lighter.

@char A10 living_room_day | The trouble | Too close to see
Part of the trouble is closeness. When you're inside a worry, you see it from just one point of view, your own, from very close up. It fills the whole frame. It's a bit like trying to read a page held against your nose. Everything is there, but nothing makes sense.

@chapter A03 autumn_overlook | A step back
The psychologist Ethan Kross, at the University of Michigan, has spent years studying what happens when people take a small step back from their own experience. He calls this self-distancing.

@char A18 cabin_fireplace | Easier for others | Wise about friends
You've probably noticed that it's often easier to give good advice to a friend than to yourself. When a friend tells you about a problem, you can see it calmly and clearly. When the same problem is yours, you lose that view. Kross wondered whether the inner voice could be used to borrow some of that outside view, and one of the simplest tools turned out to be a word you use every day: your own name.

@study A03 autumn_overlook | Ethan Kross and colleagues | Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 2014 | Using your own name or you instead of I helped people handle social stress
In 2014, Kross and his colleagues published a paper in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology called Self-talk as a regulatory mechanism: how you do it matters. It reported a series of studies with 585 people in all.

@stat A12 cafe_sunset | People across the studies | 585 | in seven studies of distanced self-talk (Kross et al., 2014)
In the studies, people had to face stressful social situations, such as making a good first impression on a stranger, or giving a short speech with very little time to prepare. Before or after, some reflected on their feelings using I and me. Others reflected using their own name, or you, as if talking to someone else.

@list A15 living_room_day | What distanced self-talk did | Less distress ;; Better performance, judged by raters ;; Less dwelling afterwards ;; Stress seen as a challenge, not a threat
The results were striking. People who used their name or non-first-person words felt less distress. In the first impression and speech tasks, outside raters judged their performance as better. After the speech, they dwelled less on how it went. And when people thought about stressful events that were coming up, they saw them more as a challenge and less as a threat.

@char A20 cafe_sunset | Even for anxious people | It still worked
One more finding stood out. The effect was not weaker in people who were more socially anxious to begin with. That matters, because it suggests the tool can help even those who find these situations hardest.

@photo lf29_b11_01.jpg | A little distance
Think of it like stepping back from the edge of a lake to see its whole shape. Up close, all you see is water. From a little distance, you can see the shore, the hills, and the path around it. Using your own name in your head seems to give you that view of your own life, just for a moment.

@chapter A16 rooftop_dusk | Watching the brain
Kross and the psychologist Jason Moser then wanted to see what this looked like in the brain, and whether it was hard work.

@study A10 living_room_day | Moser, Kross and colleagues | Scientific Reports, 2017 | Third-person self-talk reduced emotional brain activity within about a second, with no extra effort
In 2017, they published two complementary experiments in the journal Scientific Reports. In one, led by Moser at Michigan State University, people looked at neutral and upsetting pictures while their brain activity was recorded with EEG, and reacted to them using either first-person or third-person self-talk. In the other, led by Kross at the University of Michigan, people recalled painful memories in a brain scanner, again using first- or third-person language.

@list A12 cafe_sunset | What the brain showed | Emotional activity dropped within about a second ;; Less activity in a region linked to painful reflection ;; No sign of extra mental effort
In the picture experiment, emotional brain activity dropped within about one second when people used third-person self-talk. In the memory experiment, there was less activity in a brain region often linked to reflecting on painful experiences. And in both, there was no sign that the third-person version took any more mental effort. Moser described it as giving people a tiny bit of psychological distance from their experiences.

@char A19 cabin_fireplace | Still early days | Honest limits
As always, it's worth being careful. Kross himself said these findings needed more research before anyone could be sure. Brain studies like these involve small groups and particular tasks. But the overall picture, across behaviour and brain recordings, is consistent: a small change in the words you use to talk to yourself can change how you feel.

@chapter A05 autumn_overlook | Naming the feeling
There's one more piece of research that belongs here, because it's about the simplest self-talk of all: a single word for a feeling.

@study A03 autumn_overlook | Matthew Lieberman and colleagues | UCLA, 2007 | Putting a feeling into words reduced activity in the amygdala
In 2007, Matthew Lieberman and his colleagues at the University of California, Los Angeles, published a study in Psychological Science. Thirty adults lay in a brain scanner and looked at pictures of faces showing emotions. Under each face, they either chose a word that described the emotion, such as angry or scared, or chose a name, like Harry or Sally, that matched the face's gender.

@char A02 living_room_day | The result | Words as brakes
When people labelled the emotion, activity in the amygdala, a region closely involved in emotional reactions, went down. Matching a name did not have that effect. At the same time, a region in the right front of the brain, the right ventrolateral prefrontal cortex, was more active.

@quote A12 cafe_sunset | When you put feelings into words, you seem to be hitting the brakes on your emotional responses. | Matthew Lieberman, UCLA, 2007
Lieberman summed it up with an image that's easy to remember. Putting feelings into words seems to be like hitting the brakes on our emotional responses. You don't have to solve the feeling. Just naming it, this is worry, this is sadness, seems to take a little of its force away.

@chapter A12 cabin_fireplace | The hidden reason
So now we can gather up the answer to the question in tonight's title. Why do we talk to ourselves?

@list A20 cabin_fireplace | Why we talk to ourselves | It holds words in memory ;; It guides us step by step ;; It focuses and motivates ;; It can calm and comfort ;; It began as other people's voices
Because the inner voice is one of the mind's most useful tools. It holds words and numbers in memory for a few seconds. It guides us through tasks, step by step, the way it guided us as children. It helps us focus and keep going. It can calm us down, especially when we speak to ourselves with a little distance or name what we feel. And it began as the voices of the people who looked after us, which may be why it can sound so much like them.
