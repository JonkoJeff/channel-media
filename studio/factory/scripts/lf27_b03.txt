@chapter A12 living_room_day | The seminary students
In 1970, at Princeton Theological Seminary, a group of trainee priests agreed to take part in what they were told was a study of religious education. They had no idea that the real experiment would take place on a short walk across campus, and that it would become one of the most quoted studies in social psychology.

@char A04 attic_rain | The setup | A parable and a doorway
The researchers were John Darley and Daniel Batson. They asked forty seminary students to prepare a short talk. Half were told to talk about job opportunities for seminary graduates. The other half were assigned the parable of the Good Samaritan, the story of a stranger who stops to help a man beaten and left by the road.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Darley & Batson | Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 1973 | From Jerusalem to Jericho: a study of situational and dispositional variables in helping behavior
After preparing, each student was sent to another building to give the talk. On the way, they passed a man slumped in a doorway, head down, eyes closed, coughing and groaning. He was an actor. The researchers noted whether each student stopped to help. The key variable was how much of a hurry the student was told he was in.

@list A03 living_room_day | Three levels of hurry | High hurry: You're late, they were expecting you a few minutes ago ;; Medium hurry: They're ready for you, so you'd better head over ;; Low hurry: It'll be a few minutes before they're ready, but you might as well go
The rest of the study was careful. Before the walk, the students also filled in questionnaires about their religious motivations, so the researchers could ask whether a religious person was more likely to help. They could also check whether having just thought about the Good Samaritan made a difference.

@stat A14 conspiracy_board | Students who stopped to help, by hurry | 63% / 45% / 10% | for low, medium and high hurry
The results were clear. Of the students who were not in a hurry, sixty-three percent stopped. In the medium group, forty-five percent. Among those told they were late, only ten percent. Whether they had been preparing a talk on the Good Samaritan made no significant difference.

@char A12 living_room_day | The sting | Stepping over the victim
Some of the students in the high hurry group, the report notes, literally stepped over the victim as they hurried to give their talk about helping a stranger. It's a vivid detail, and it has been repeated for fifty years. It's also easy to misread.

@chapter A13 rooftop_dusk | What the study does and doesn't show
The popular reading is that being in a hurry makes people uncaring. But the researchers themselves were more careful. They suggested that the hurried students were not callous. Many seemed conflicted, torn between a commitment to the people waiting for them and the stranger in the doorway. Hurry changed which duty seemed most urgent.

@char A04 attic_rain | A caveat | Forty is a small number
It's also worth remembering how small the study was. Forty students, in one place, on one day. A result like this is a striking demonstration, not a settled fact. Later work on time pressure and helping has been mixed, so it's best to treat the result as a vivid demonstration of one mechanism, and not as a law.

@list A12 living_room_day | Why hurry matters | It narrows attention to the task at hand ;; It makes other demands feel more urgent ;; It reduces the chance of noticing a need ;; It makes stopping feel expensive
These are plausible mechanisms. The notice stage is especially important. You can't help someone you haven't registered. People in a hurry often fail to take in what's around them, and not because they decided not to care.

@char A01 cafe_sunset | A practical thought | Slow down on purpose
It suggests a simple practice. If kindness depends partly on having a little slack in our day, then giving ourselves slack is a way of making kindness possible. Leaving ten minutes early, or building small gaps into a schedule, are not just productivity habits. They are ways of keeping room in the day for another person.

@chapter A06 cafe_sunset | Character or situation?
This study is often cited as proof that situations matter more than character. That's a bit too strong. The researchers did also measure each student's religious outlook beforehand, and found that those measures told them far less about who stopped than the amount of hurry did. Character wasn't irrelevant. It was simply outweighed by the situation in this particular test.

@list A03 living_room_day | Words to keep from this chapter | Situationism ;; Dispositional factors ;; Conflict ;; Noticing
Four terms from this chapter. Situationism is the idea that circumstances drive behaviour. Dispositional factors are the traits and values a person brings. Conflict is the pull of competing duties. And noticing is the first step of helping: seeing that there is a need.

@list A12 living_room_day | A small experiment for your week | Pick one day and leave early for everything ;; Walk without your phone for part of it ;; Notice how many people you pass who might need something ;; Write down what you noticed that evening
Here is a small experiment, inspired by the study. Pick one day this week and leave early for everything, so that you're not rushing. Walk for part of it without your phone in your hand. And notice how many moments there are in which someone might need something: a question, a door held, a smile. Picture this scene: a woman walking to the bus stop, with ten spare minutes, sees a tourist studying a map and says, can I help you find something? She'd have walked past on any other morning. The difference wasn't her character. It was ten minutes. That's what the research suggests, and it's an encouraging thought.

@list A12 living_room_day | Pause and reflect | When am I most rushed? ;; What do I miss when I rush? ;; Where could I leave a gap? ;; Who might benefit from my spare minutes?
Take a moment to think about your own mornings and afternoons. When are you most rushed? And what do you miss when you rush? Perhaps the colleague who looked tired, the neighbour who waved, or the stranger with a puzzled look. The study of the seminary students suggests that hurry doesn't switch off kindness so much as shrink our attention to the next task. It is a little like walking with a torch that lights only the path in front of us. Everything outside the beam goes unseen. If you can find even one place in your day to leave a small gap, such as walking to the station five minutes early, or leaving a meeting without rushing to the next, you widen the beam. Notice who is in it. You may find that the person who needed help was there all along, waiting for someone with a moment to spare.

@photo lf27_b03_01.jpg | A doorway on the way
A doorway on a campus path. It's easy to imagine the students passing it, thinking of their talks. The experiment was built on this simple fact: help is often needed in the middle of something else, and what we do depends on what else is on our minds. It is worth repeating that the seminary students were not villains. They were ordinary, thoughtful people with a schedule, and the same pressure acts on all of us. That is why the remedy is gentle: leave a little room, and look up.

@char A15 cabin_fireplace | A gentle thought | Be kind to the hurried
If you've ever hurried past someone and felt bad about it afterwards, be kind to yourself. The research suggests that the hurry, and not a flaw in you, did most of the work. And it suggests what to do next time: make a little room. You do not need a perfect schedule for this. A single unhurried morning, once in a while, is enough to remind you what you tend to miss, and what becomes possible when there is a little room in the day for other people.

@char A08 cabin_fireplace | Looking back | What the seminary students taught us
So what did we learn? That helping fell from sixty-three percent to ten percent as hurry increased. That thinking about the Good Samaritan did not change it. That hurry creates conflict rather than callousness. And that a little slack in the day makes room for kindness.
