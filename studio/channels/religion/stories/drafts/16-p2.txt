CHAPTER: Luke: The Road to Emmaus
Luke tells the story differently again. The women come to the tomb with spices, find the stone rolled away, and go in, but they do not find the body. Suddenly two men in dazzling clothes stand beside them and ask why they look for the living among the dead. || Women at the Empty Tomb, Luke 24, William Adolphe Bouguereau || wikimedia
The women return and tell the eleven apostles and all the others. Luke says bluntly that these words seemed to them an idle tale, and they did not believe the women. Peter, however, runs to the tomb, stoops to look in, and sees only the linen cloths. He goes home amazed at what had happened. || Peter and John at the Tomb, Eugene Burnand || wikimedia
Then comes one of the most loved passages in the Gospels. That same day, two followers of Jesus are walking to a village called Emmaus, about seven miles from Jerusalem, discussing everything that has happened. A stranger joins them and asks what they are talking about. || Road to Emmaus, Robert Zünd || wikimedia
One of them, named Cleopas, is astonished that the stranger seems not to know. They tell him about Jesus of Nazareth, a prophet mighty in word and deed, handed over and crucified, and add sadly that they had hoped he was the one who would redeem Israel. || The Supper at Emmaus, Caravaggio, National Gallery London || wikimedia
Hoped is a telling word. It is the past tense of disappointment. Luke does not hide that these were people who believed that the movement had failed. The stranger then explains to them, from Moses and all the prophets, what the scriptures say about the Messiah, and why he had to suffer. || Road to Emmaus, Duccio || wikimedia
When they reach the village, they urge the stranger to stay with them, as it is nearly evening. At the table, he takes bread, blesses it, breaks it and gives it to them, and their eyes are opened. They recognize him, and he vanishes from their sight. || Supper at Emmaus, Rembrandt, Musée Jacquemart-André || wikimedia
They say to each other that their hearts were burning within them while he talked on the road. At once they return to Jerusalem and find the eleven gathered, who tell them that the Lord has risen indeed and has appeared to Simon. Then Jesus himself stands among them. || Appearance of Christ to the Disciples, Duccio || wikimedia
They are startled and frightened, thinking that they see a ghost. Luke has Jesus invite them to look at his hands and feet, and to touch him. He asks for something to eat, and they give him a piece of broiled fish, which he eats in front of them. || Christ appears to the Apostles, Duccio, Maestà || wikimedia
Luke seems to be answering a question that worried early Christians: was this a spirit, or a real person? His answer is that the risen Jesus is recognizably the same, yet also strangely different, since his closest followers do not at first know him. That tension runs through all of the stories. || Luke 24:36-43 || text-card
Luke ends his Gospel with Jesus leading the disciples out to Bethany, lifting his hands and blessing them, and being carried up into heaven. In the book of Acts, Luke gives a longer account: forty days of appearances and teaching, and then the ascension from the Mount of Olives. || Ascension of Christ, Giotto || wikimedia
CHAPTER: John: The Garden and the Doubter
John's Gospel brings us to the tomb through the eyes of a single woman. Mary Magdalene comes while it is still dark, sees that the stone has been removed, and runs to Simon Peter and the other disciple, saying that they have taken the Lord out of the tomb, and she does not know where they have laid him. || Mary Magdalene at the Tomb, John 20, Ary Scheffer || wikimedia
Peter and the disciple whom Jesus loved race to the tomb. The other disciple arrives first but waits, and Peter goes in and sees the linen wrappings lying there, and the cloth that had been around the head rolled up in a place by itself. Then the other disciple goes in, and John says that he saw and believed. || Peter and John Running to the Tomb, Eugene Burnand, Musée d'Orsay || wikimedia
The disciples go home, but Mary remains outside the tomb, weeping. As she looks in, she sees two angels in white, and when she turns, she sees a man standing there and does not realize that it is Jesus. She supposes that he is the gardener, and asks him where he has taken the body. || Noli me tangere, Titian, National Gallery London || wikimedia
Jesus says one word: Mary. She turns and says in Aramaic, Rabboni, which means teacher. It is among the most tender moments in the whole New Testament, and John builds it on a simple human fact: she knows him by the way he says her name. || Noli me Tangere, Fra Angelico, San Marco || wikimedia
He tells her not to cling to him, for he has not yet ascended to the Father, and sends her to tell his brothers. Mary Magdalene goes and announces to the disciples that she has seen the Lord. Later Christian tradition would give her the title apostle to the apostles. || Mary Magdalene Announcing the Resurrection, Gospel illumination || wikimedia
That evening, John says, the disciples are together with the doors locked for fear. Jesus comes and stands among them and says, peace be with you. He shows them his hands and his side, and the disciples are glad. One of the twelve, Thomas, is not there. || Christ appears to the disciples, locked doors, John 20 || text-card
When the others tell him they have seen the Lord, Thomas says that unless he sees the mark of the nails and puts his finger in it, he will not believe. A week later, Jesus appears again, and invites him to touch his hands and his side. Thomas answers with the shortest confession in the Gospels: my Lord and my God. || The Incredulity of Saint Thomas, Caravaggio, Potsdam || wikimedia
Jesus then says that those who have not seen and yet have believed are blessed. John closes the chapter with a note that Jesus did many other signs not written in this book, and that these are written so that you may believe, and that by believing you may have life. || John 20:30-31 || text-card
Then comes a surprise. John's Gospel seems to end there, and yet there is an entire further chapter, in which the disciples are back in Galilee, fishing at the lake. Most scholars think it was added later, by the author or by a close circle of his followers. || Codex Sinaiticus, Gospel of John || wikimedia
In that last chapter, Jesus appears on the shore at dawn, having lit a charcoal fire, and the disciples recognize him after an enormous catch of fish. Over breakfast he asks Peter three times whether he loves him, which many readers see as a gentle answer to Peter's three denials. || Christ at the Sea of Galilee, Duccio || wikimedia
CHAPTER: Mary Magdalene: Witness and Legend
Mary Magdalene is named in every Gospel account of the empty tomb, which is unusual for such a detailed list of names. She takes her name from Magdala, a fishing town on the western shore of the Sea of Galilee, and Luke says that she was among the women who supported Jesus' ministry out of their own means. || Magdala, Sea of Galilee, excavations || wikimedia
Luke also says that seven demons had gone out of her. That phrase has often been read as a sign of illness or deep distress, and nothing in the Gospels says that she was a sinful woman or a prostitute. That identification came later, from a sermon of Pope Gregory the Great in the year five hundred and ninety-one. || Pope Gregory the Great, Homily 33 || text-card
Modern scholars, and the Catholic Church itself, have set that old confusion aside. In 2016, Pope Francis raised her memorial in the Roman calendar to the rank of a feast, the same level as the apostles, calling her the apostle of the apostles, a title that goes back to medieval writers. || Mary Magdalene, Carlo Crivelli || wikimedia
Outside the Gospels, she appears in other early writings. In the Gospel of Mary, a text from the second century which survives in Coptic and Greek fragments, she is shown comforting the other disciples after the resurrection and sharing teachings she received from the Savior, though Peter doubts her. The text reflects later debates rather than early memory. || Gospel of Mary, Papyrus Rylands 463 || wikimedia
What matters for our question is simple. If the story had been invented to persuade, the argument goes, a male leader or a famous apostle would have been put at the tomb. Instead, all four Gospels give the first place to Mary and her companions, and the earliest creed in Paul leaves them out entirely. || Mary Magdalene, Titian, Palazzo Pitti || wikimedia
Whatever one makes of the stories, the Gospel writers seem to have kept this detail even when it was awkward. Their respect for the women's witness is part of what gives these chapters their distinctive tone: not the polished account of a movement's founders, but the memory of people who were afraid, confused and astonished. || Easter morning, Mary Magdalene and the Angel, Ary Scheffer || wikimedia
CHAPTER: Four Gospels, One Morning
Put the four accounts side by side, and one thing is plain. They agree on a few essentials. Jesus was buried on Friday. Women went to the tomb on the first day of the week. The tomb was found empty. And the first witnesses were women, led by Mary Magdalene. || Four Evangelists, Book of Kells || wikimedia
But they differ on many details. How many women went? Mark says three, Matthew two, Luke names several, and John names only Mary Magdalene, though she says we do not know where they have laid him. How many angels? One young man in Mark, one angel in Matthew, two men in Luke, two angels in John. || Women at the Tomb, comparative table || text-card
They differ on where Jesus appeared first, in Galilee or in Jerusalem. Matthew and Mark point to Galilee. Luke places the appearances in and near Jerusalem and has the disciples told to stay in the city until they are clothed with power. John has both. || Map: Jerusalem and Galilee, first century || map
How should we think about such differences? Believers have long proposed harmonizations, arranging the pieces into a single timeline. Many historians prefer to treat each Gospel as a separate telling, shaped by its author's purpose and by the memories that had been passed along in the community. || Gospel harmony, Diatessaron, Tatian || wikimedia
There is an old observation among scholars of ancient history that independent eyewitness reports of any dramatic event usually differ in the details, even when they agree on the core. Perfect uniformity would suggest coordination. Variation, they note, is what we would expect from separate lines of memory. || Gospel manuscript, Papyrus 75 || wikimedia
But there is a different observation too. The Gospels were composed decades after the events, in Greek, by authors who were not themselves eyewitnesses in the way a modern reporter would be. That makes the earlier evidence all the more interesting, and so we turn to the earliest of all. || Greek papyrus, Gospel of Luke, Papyrus 75 || wikimedia
