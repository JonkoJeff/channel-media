@chapter A12 living_room_day | Deliberate practice and the ten thousand hour myth
Almost everyone has heard the rule. To become an expert at anything, you need ten thousand hours of practice. It's a tidy number, and it has appeared in books, in speeches and in countless articles. It suggests that mastery is simply a matter of putting in the time, and that anyone can get there if they just keep going.

@char A04 attic_rain | A good question | Where did it come from?
So where did it come from? It's worth tracing, because the real story is more interesting than the slogan, and much more useful. It begins in Berlin in the early 1990s, with a psychologist called Anders Ericsson and two colleagues.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Ericsson, Krampe & Tesch-Römer | Psychological Review, 1993 | The role of deliberate practice in the acquisition of expert performance
They studied violin students at the Music Academy of West Berlin. The teachers sorted the students into groups: the best, the good, and those training to be music teachers rather than soloists. Then the researchers interviewed the students about their practice, going back year by year since they'd first picked up the violin.

@char A12 living_room_day | What they found | The best practised the most
The students judged to be the best had, by the age of twenty, accumulated on average around ten thousand hours of practice. The good group had accumulated considerably less, and the future music teachers, less again. The difference was not in talent as a single quality. It was in how much serious practice they'd done, and how they did it.

@char A04 attic_rain | Where the myth began | Averages, not a rule
Notice what the number was. It was an average, for one group of musicians, by one age. It was not a threshold. Some of the students in the best group had fewer than ten thousand hours, and some in lower groups had more. The number was a description of the best group, and the popular version turned it into a prescription.

@chapter A13 rooftop_dusk | What deliberate means
The word that matters in Ericsson's work is deliberate. He didn't mean just any practice. A person who plays the same piece through for the thousandth time, on autopilot, is not doing what he meant. He described deliberate practice as something rather different.

@list A03 living_room_day | The ingredients of deliberate practice | A specific, well-defined goal for each session ;; Full attention, not autopilot ;; Immediate feedback, from a coach or from yourself ;; Working just beyond your comfort zone ;; Repeating and refining until it improves
That is much harder than it sounds. Deliberate practice isn't enjoyable in the same way as playing for fun. It asks you to focus on the exact thing you can't yet do, and to fail at it again and again. In the Berlin study, the best violinists said practice wasn't the most enjoyable part of their day. But they did it with focus, usually in the morning, and they took naps afterwards.

@photo lf20_b07_01.jpg | An empty music stand
Ericsson also noticed that even the best performers could only keep this up for a limited time. They practised in sessions of about an hour, rarely more than four or five hours in a day. Quality of attention seems to be the limit, rather than simple willpower.

@chapter A12 living_room_day | The popular version
In 2008, the writer Malcolm Gladwell turned the idea into a bestseller. In his book Outliers, he used the ten thousand hour figure, and linked it to examples such as the Beatles in Hamburg and Bill Gates, as a rule of thumb for success.

@char A04 attic_rain | The reaction | Ericsson pushed back
Ericsson, for his part, was not happy with the summary. In his own book, Peak, he pointed out several problems. The ten thousand figure was an average. It applied to violinists by a certain age, and in other fields the number was very different. The Beatles' hours were mostly playing live gigs, which is not the same as deliberate practice. And above all, he said, it wasn't the hours that mattered, but what you did in them.

@stat A14 conspiracy_board | The number of hours | An average | of one group of violin students by age twenty, not a threshold for mastery
It's useful to hold on to this, because the myth does damage in two directions. It can discourage people, who think they'll never find ten thousand hours. And it can mislead those who do put in the time, but practise passively and wonder why they're not improving.

@chapter A06 cafe_sunset | How much does practice matter?
Now, a natural question. If practice matters so much, does it explain most of the difference between experts and everyone else? That has been tested directly, and the answer is more complicated.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Macnamara, Hambrick & Oswald | Psychological Science, 2014 | Deliberate practice and performance in music, games, sports, education, and professions: a meta-analysis
Brooke Macnamara, David Hambrick and Frederick Oswald collected eighty-eight studies and asked how much of the variation in performance could be explained by the amount of deliberate practice. The answer differed greatly by field.

@bars A12 living_room_day | Share of performance differences explained by practice | percent | Games:26 ;; Music:21 ;; Sports:18 ;; Education:4 ;; Professions:1
In games like chess, practice explained about a quarter of the differences between people. In music, a bit more than a fifth, and in sport a little less than a fifth. In education it was about four percent, and in professions, less than one percent. Practice mattered everywhere, but in every field there was a lot of variation left over.

@char A04 attic_rain | A fair reading | Practice is not everything
What explains the rest? The researchers pointed to things such as the age you started, general ability, and the quality of teaching you had. Ericsson disagreed with some of the methods in that meta-analysis, arguing that many of the studies hadn't measured genuinely deliberate practice. So there is a live debate. A fair reading is that practice is a major factor, and certainly one you can control, but it isn't the only one.

@char A12 living_room_day | The key point | You control the practice
That is rather freeing. You can't change your starting age or your genes. You can change how you practise. And the research on that side, the quality of practice, is encouraging. People who practise with clear goals and feedback improve faster than those who just put in time.

@chapter A13 rooftop_dusk | Ordinary practice versus deliberate practice
Let's make this concrete. Imagine two people learning the guitar. Both play for an hour each evening. The first plays the songs she likes, from beginning to end. It's a pleasant hour. The second picks one chord change that keeps tripping her up.

@list A03 living_room_day | The same hour, two ways | Playing through favourite songs: pleasant, little new learning ;; Isolating the one difficult change: frustrating, steady improvement ;; Recording yourself and listening back: honest feedback ;; Slowing down until it is clean, then speeding up
After a year, the first person is a comfortable player who's more or less the same as she was in month two. The second is a noticeably better one. What separated them wasn't talent or hours. It was whether the hours were aimed at weaknesses.

@char A04 attic_rain | Watching experts | How they fix errors
Descriptions of how expert musicians practise difficult passages tend to agree on one thing. The experts don't simply play the passage again and again. They stop at the error, work out what went wrong, slow down and fix that specific spot. Then they start again from a little before it, so that the repaired bar joins up with what surrounds it.

@char A01 cafe_sunset | In everyday terms | Fix, then return
You can borrow that for anything: a speech, a language, a sport, a spreadsheet formula. When you slip, don't just start again from the top. Find the exact spot where it went wrong, work on that spot, and then put it back into the whole.

@chapter A12 living_room_day | Deliberate practice outside the arts
Does this apply beyond music and sport? Training for doctors, for instance, increasingly uses simulators, where a trainee can repeat one specific skill again and again with immediate feedback, before ever touching a real patient.

@char A04 attic_rain | A fair note | Not every field has feedback
There's a catch. Deliberate practice works best in fields where there is clear feedback, such as chess, where you know whether you won, or piano, where you can hear a wrong note. In messier fields, such as predicting the stock market or reading a stranger's character, experience alone doesn't always build skill, because the feedback is slow, noisy or absent.

@char A12 living_room_day | The principle | Feedback is the engine
The psychologist Daniel Kahneman and Gary Klein made this point in a famous joint article in 2009, where they argued that intuition can be trusted when the environment is regular enough to learn from, and the person has had real chances to get feedback. It's another way of saying that experience is not the same as expertise.

@list A03 living_room_day | How to find feedback when no coach is around | Record yourself and review it ;; Compare your work with an excellent example ;; Test yourself with problems that have answers ;; Ask someone more skilled to point out one thing
There's real comfort in that list. Most of us will never have a Berlin conservatory teacher. But a recording, a model answer and a friendly critic are within reach of almost anyone.

@chapter A06 cafe_sunset | Keeping going
One more point belongs here, and it's about energy. Because deliberate practice is demanding, you can't do it for long. A sensible plan is short, focused sessions, with breaks, rather than marathon sessions where the quality of attention fades.

@char A15 cabin_fireplace | A gentle thought | Short and focused
That fits with something the Berlin violinists did. They practised in the morning, in sessions of up to about an hour and a half, with rest between. Their days weren't endless labour. They were carefully shaped, with room for sleep, for naps and for leisure. Even the elite were not hurrying. They were protecting their attention.

@char A08 cabin_fireplace | Looking back | What the ten thousand hours taught us
So, what's the verdict? That the ten thousand hour figure was an average for top violinists, not a rule. That deliberate practice, with goals, focus and feedback, matters more than raw time. That practice explains a good deal of skill in some fields and much less in others. And that the part you control is the quality of your hours.
