@chapter A06 cafe_sunset | Repair and apology
Sooner or later, a conversation goes wrong. A careless word, a remark that landed badly, a promise broken. We've all been on both sides of it. And then comes one of the hardest conversations of all: the repair. What does the research say about how to apologise well, and how to receive an apology?

@char A12 living_room_day | Why apologies matter | A way back
An apology does something unusual. It acknowledges that a harm happened, and that the person responsible takes it seriously. For the person who was hurt, that acknowledgement can be the thing that makes forgiveness possible. Without it, the injury often stays raw, and the relationship stays stuck.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Fehr, Gelfand & Nag | Psychological Bulletin, 2010 | The road to forgiveness: a meta-analytic synthesis of its situational and dispositional correlates
Ryan Fehr and his colleagues combined results from many studies of forgiveness. They found that, among the situational factors, apologies had a clear link with forgiveness, and that the way people judged an offender, whether as sincere, remorseful and not likely to repeat the offence, was among the strongest predictors. The size of the harm and the closeness of the relationship also mattered.

@char A04 attic_rain | What this suggests | Sincerity is the key
Notice that it isn't merely the act of saying sorry that matters. What counts is whether the other person believes that you mean it, and that you understand what you did. A hollow apology can do more harm than none, because it adds an insult to the injury.

@chapter A13 rooftop_dusk | The anatomy of an apology
Which parts of an apology matter most? Researchers at Ohio State University, led by Roy Lewicki, decided to find out. They looked at the common components of an apology and asked people which ones they valued, in different kinds of scenarios.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Lewicki, Polin & Lount | Negotiation and Conflict Management Research, 2016 | An exploration of the structure of effective apologies
They identified six components of an apology. An expression of regret, an explanation of what went wrong, an acknowledgement of responsibility, a declaration of repentance, an offer of repair and a request for forgiveness. People rated the effectiveness of apologies with different combinations. The more components an apology contained, the better it was rated, and two mattered most: acknowledging responsibility, and offering to make amends.

@list A12 living_room_day | The six parts of an apology | Regret: I'm sorry ;; Explanation: here's what happened ;; Responsibility: it was my fault ;; Repentance: I won't do it again ;; Repair: how can I make it right? ;; Request: will you forgive me?
It's helpful to see them laid out. Many everyday apologies contain only the first. I'm sorry you feel that way is regret at most, and it doesn't take responsibility for anything. The research suggests that the others, especially responsibility and repair, are what turn words into repair.

@list A03 living_room_day | Apologies that tend to fail | I'm sorry if you were offended ;; I'm sorry, but you also... ;; Explaining at length, without owning the act ;; Demanding forgiveness right away
Each of these shifts the focus away from the person who was hurt. A but turns the apology into a defence. A long explanation can sound like an excuse. And pressing for forgiveness treats it as a transaction. The better course is to say what you did, own it, and give the other person time.

@chapter A12 living_room_day | Receiving an apology
The other side of the conversation is less discussed. How should we respond when someone apologises to us? It can be hard, because the hurt is still there. Researchers on forgiveness suggest that it helps to see forgiveness as a process, not a switch, and not as an obligation.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | McCullough, Worthington & Rachal | Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 1997 | Interpersonal forgiving in close relationships
Michael McCullough and his colleagues found that people are more likely to forgive when the offender expresses empathy and remorse, and when the relationship is close and committed. Forgiveness, they argued, is a motivational change: a reduced wish for revenge and avoidance, and an increased wish for goodwill. It's not the same as excusing the act, or forgetting it.

@char A01 cafe_sunset | An important distinction | Forgiving isn't excusing
It's helpful to separate forgiving from excusing, forgetting and reconciling. You can forgive someone, in the sense of letting go of anger, and still decide not to trust them again, or not to be close. Forgiveness is for the person who is hurt, as much as for the person who hurt them, and it doesn't have to be quick.

@list A03 living_room_day | Words to keep from this chapter | Apology ;; Acknowledging responsibility ;; Forgiveness ;; Repair
Four terms from this chapter. An apology is a statement of regret and responsibility. Acknowledging responsibility means owning what you did. Forgiveness is a reduction in the wish for revenge and avoidance. And repair is the work of making amends, in action as well as in words.

@list A12 living_room_day | Making a good apology | Name exactly what you did ;; Say that it was your responsibility ;; Say how you think it affected them ;; Say what you'll do differently ;; Ask if there's anything you can do now
If you owe someone an apology, here's a simple structure from the research. Name exactly what you did, without softening it. Say that it was your responsibility. Say how you think it affected them. Say what you'll do differently. And ask whether there's something that you can do now to help. Then stop talking, and let them respond. You don't need to be eloquent, only honest, and willing to hear the answer.

@photo lf25_b14_01.jpg | A letter and some wildflowers
A letter and a few wildflowers in a jar. Sometimes an apology is easier to write than to say. The research doesn't mind how you do it. What matters is that you mean it, and that you name what happened.

@list A19 living_room_day | When apologies are hard | Shame makes us defensive ;; We fear that admitting fault will be used against us ;; We think the other person should apologise first ;; We hope time will fix it
Here are four reasons why apologies are hard, and why we avoid them. Shame makes us defensive, because admitting a fault can feel like being a bad person. We fear that it'll be used against us. We tell ourselves that the other person should apologise first. And we hope that time will fix it, without anyone having to say anything. All of these are understandable. But research on forgiveness suggests that the person who apologises first usually helps more than they lose, and that unspoken hurts rarely heal themselves. You can separate the act from your worth. You did a hurtful thing, which is different from being a hurtful person, and being able to say it is a sign of strength.

@char A15 cabin_fireplace | A gentle thought | It's not too late
Many people believe that it's too late to apologise, that too much time has passed. But studies suggest that people often welcome an apology even years later, and that the person who offers it often feels lighter afterwards. If someone has been on your mind for a long time, it may be worth writing the first line.

@char A08 cabin_fireplace | Looking back | What repair taught us
So what did we learn? That sincerity is the key to an effective apology. That acknowledging responsibility and offering to make amends matter most. That a but or an if turns an apology into a defence. And that forgiveness is a process, and not a duty.
