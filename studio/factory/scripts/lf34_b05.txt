@chapter A19 cabin_fireplace | Mice and a maze
More than a hundred years ago, two American psychologists ran an experiment that is still quoted in offices, classrooms and sports changing rooms today. Most of the people who quote it have never read it. And the original turns out to be a little different from the famous version.

@study A18 living_room_day | Robert Yerkes and John Dodson | Harvard University, 1908 | The relation of strength of stimulus to rapidity of habit formation
In 1908, Robert Yerkes and John Dodson, working at Harvard, published a study on mice. The mice had to learn to choose between two little compartments, a white one and a black one. Choosing the wrong one gave them a mild electric shock. The question was simple. Does a stronger shock make them learn faster?

@char A13 cafe_sunset | The answer | It depends
The answer was, it depends. When the task was easy, when the white and the black were very clearly different, stronger shocks seemed to speed up learning. But when the task was hard, when the two compartments looked much more alike, the mice learned best with a medium shock. Too weak, and too strong, both did worse.

@curve A03 living_room_day | The famous curve | pressure | performance | 0.5 | best performance at a moderate level
Over the following decades, that finding was turned into a famous picture. An upside down U. On one side, too little pressure, and you're bored and sluggish. On the other side, too much pressure, and you fall apart. And somewhere in the middle, there's a sweet spot, where a little stress helps you do your best.

@char A10 cabin_fireplace | A tidy story | Maybe too tidy
It's a lovely, tidy idea, and it often matches how things feel. A little nervousness before a talk can sharpen you. Panic can wreck you. But psychologists who went back to the history found that the curve had travelled a long way from the original mice.

@study A19 living_room_day | Karl Halvor Teigen | University of Oslo, 1994 | Yerkes-Dodson: a law for all seasons
In 1994, the Norwegian psychologist Karl Halvor Teigen traced how the so-called Yerkes Dodson law had been described over the years. He found that it had been stretched and reshaped again and again, to mean almost whatever people needed it to mean. The neat upside down U, applied to human stress in general, was far more confident than the original data.

@char A12 cafe_sunset | What we can keep | A rough rule
So we can keep the curve as a rough, gentle rule of thumb, not a law of nature. Some arousal can help. Very high stress often hurts, especially for difficult tasks. And for a clearer picture of why, we need to look inside the brain, at the part that does your most careful thinking.

@photo lf34_b05_01.jpg | Where careful thinking happens
Imagine a quiet desk in the early morning. A notebook, a pencil, a lamp. It's the kind of place where careful thinking happens. Your brain has its own version of this desk, right behind your forehead.

@chapter A05 autumn_overlook | The front of the brain
It's called the prefrontal cortex. It's the part of the brain that helps you plan, hold several things in mind at once, keep your attention where you want it, and stop yourself from saying the first thing that comes into your head. In a sense, it's the calm manager of the mind.

@study A03 cafe_sunset | Amy Arnsten | Yale University, 2009 | Stress signalling pathways that impair prefrontal cortex structure and function
In 2009, the neuroscientist Amy Arnsten at Yale published an influential review of what stress does to this region. Her conclusion was striking. Even fairly mild stress, when it feels uncontrollable, can quickly weaken the working of the prefrontal cortex. The chemicals that flood the brain during stress seem to take the calm manager partly offline.

@quote A18 living_room_day | Stress switches the brain from reflective to reflexive | A summary of Arnsten's 2009 review
One way to put the idea is that stress shifts the brain from a reflective mode to a reflexive one. Slower, careful thinking steps back. Faster, older, more automatic responses step forward. Habits, instincts, the things you've practised so often you don't need to think.

@char A13 cabin_fireplace | Why that makes sense | In real danger
Again, if you were facing a real physical danger, that would be useful. You don't want to sit and weigh up options while something is chasing you. You want to run, and you want to do it now. The trouble is that a meeting room at nine o'clock is not that kind of danger.

@char A10 living_room_day | Jonah's first line | Gone
And this is exactly what Jonah fears. He's practised his opening sentence at least twenty times. He knows it. And yet he can picture himself standing at the front, opening his mouth, and finding nothing there. That blank moment has a name in psychology. People call it choking under pressure.

@study A19 cafe_sunset | Sian Beilock and Thomas Carr | Psychological Science, 2005 | When high-powered people fail: working memory and choking under pressure in math
In 2005, Sian Beilock and Thomas Carr studied choking with difficult maths problems. They found something surprising. The people who were hit hardest by pressure were often the ones with the most working memory, the people who usually did best. Pressure, and the worries it brings, seemed to use up the very mental space they normally relied on.

@char A12 rooftop_dusk | Worries take up room | A crowded desk
A useful way to picture it is that worries take up room. If your mental desk is covered with thoughts about failing, about the late train, about what your manager will think, there's less space left for the task in front of you. The problem isn't that you're not clever enough. The desk is just crowded.

@char A05 cafe_sunset | Clearing the desk | One small study
And Beilock's group later tried a simple way to clear some of that space. We'll come back to it near the end of our night, because it involves nothing more than a pen and a few minutes before the test. For now, just remember the picture of the crowded desk.

@chapter A15 autumn_overlook | Students before an exam
So far, we've been talking about stress that lasts a morning. But what about stress that lasts a month? A team of researchers found a natural, and rather relatable, way to find out.

@study A03 living_room_day | Conor Liston, Bruce McEwen and BJ Casey | Weill Cornell and Rockefeller University, 2009 | Psychosocial stress reversibly disrupts prefrontal processing and attentional control
In 2009, Conor Liston, Bruce McEwen and BJ Casey studied medical students in New York who were preparing for an important exam. For weeks, these students lived with heavy, ongoing stress. The researchers compared them with similar people who were not under that kind of pressure, using brain scans and a task that measured how easily they could shift their attention.

@char A13 cabin_fireplace | What they found | Stuck attention
The stressed students had more trouble switching their attention from one thing to another, and their brain scans showed weaker connections in prefrontal networks linked to that kind of flexible attention. They reported more stress, and their thinking had become a little less nimble, a little more stuck.

@stat A20 cafe_sunset | Recovery | 1 | month of lower stress, and the differences faded
And then came the hopeful part. The researchers tested the students again after about a month of lower stress, once the pressure had passed. The differences in attention, and in the brain scans, had largely disappeared. The changes had been real, but they were not permanent.

@char A05 living_room_day | A gentle lesson | The brain bounces back
That word in the title of their paper, reversibly, is worth holding on to. The brain under stress changes, but it is not broken. When the pressure eases and rest returns, it can find its way back. It's a reminder that hard seasons, like exam season, are seasons. They pass.

@char A10 cabin_fireplace | Rest as part of the work | Not a luxury
It also turns rest into something more than a luxury. When we push through long stretches of pressure, we tend to think of rest as time taken away from the important work. But if stress makes thinking stiffer, and rest helps it become flexible again, then rest is part of the work. It's how the tools get sharpened.

@list A18 cafe_sunset | Stress and thinking | Some arousal can sharpen you ;; Strong stress weakens careful, flexible thinking ;; Old habits take over ;; Worries crowd your working memory ;; With rest, the brain recovers
Let's gather what we've learned. A little arousal can sharpen you. Strong stress weakens careful, flexible thinking. Older habits and quick reactions take over. Worries crowd the space you think with. And when the pressure passes and you rest, the brain can recover.
