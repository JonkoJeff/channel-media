@chapter A04 attic_rain | The myth of the stages
You've probably heard of the five stages of grief: denial, anger, bargaining, depression and acceptance. It's perhaps the best known idea in the psychology of loss. It appears in films, in advice columns, in conversations at funerals. People often ask themselves which stage they're in, and worry that they're doing it wrong if they skip one. So where did it come from, and does the evidence support it?

@char A12 living_room_day | The origin | A book about dying
The stages come from Elisabeth Kübler-Ross, a Swiss-American psychiatrist, and her 1969 book On Death and Dying. She had interviewed terminally ill patients about their experience of facing death. Her five stages described how many of them seemed to respond. They were drawn from patients, and not from the bereaved, and she later said that they weren't meant to be a rigid sequence.

@char A04 attic_rain | A twist of history | A description became a rule
Over time, the five stages were applied to every kind of loss, including bereavement, divorce and illness. And they were taken as a roadmap, a series of steps that everyone must pass through in order. Kübler-Ross herself wrote that they were not meant to tuck messy emotions into neat packages. But the neat version was the one that stuck.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Maciejewski, Zhang, Block & Prigerson | Journal of the American Medical Association, 2007 | An empirical examination of the stage theory of grief
Paul Maciejewski and his colleagues at Yale studied two hundred and thirty-three people who had lost a family member to natural causes, interviewing them at several points over two years. They measured disbelief, yearning, anger, depression and acceptance. If the stage theory were right, these should peak in sequence, in that order.

@stat A14 conspiracy_board | Bereaved people in the Yale study | 233 | interviewed several times over the two years after a death
What they found only partly fit. Acceptance was the most common feeling from the very first months, and not the last. Yearning, the longing for the person who had died, was the strongest negative feeling at all time points. And rather than one stage replacing another, the emotions overlapped, with different feelings rising and falling over time. There was no clear march from one stage to the next.

@list A03 living_room_day | What the Yale study found | Acceptance was common early, not only at the end ;; Yearning was the strongest negative emotion ;; Emotions overlapped, with no fixed order ;; Most people moved towards acceptance over time
It's a gentler picture than the stages, in a way. It says that you don't have to pass an exam in grief, and that feelings can come and go in no particular order. Most people did find themselves in a calmer place with time, but they got there along different paths.

@chapter A13 rooftop_dusk | Another view of grief
Researchers who study loss have offered other models. One that has gained wide support is the dual process model, developed by Margaret Stroebe and Henk Schut in the Netherlands. It starts from an observation: grieving people don't move in a straight line. They swing.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Stroebe & Schut | Death Studies, 1999 | The dual process model of coping with bereavement: rationale and description
Stroebe and Schut proposed that bereaved people alternate between two kinds of coping. Loss-oriented coping means facing the pain: crying, remembering, longing. Restoration-oriented coping means dealing with the changes of daily life: new roles, new tasks, distraction and taking a break from grief. Healthy adjustment, they argued, involves moving back and forth, called oscillation, between the two.

@list A12 living_room_day | Two kinds of coping | Loss-oriented: remembering, crying, missing them ;; Restoration-oriented: practical tasks, new routines, time off from sadness ;; Healthy grieving moves between the two ;; Getting stuck in either one can cause difficulty
This gives permission to do something that many grieving people feel guilty about. To laugh at a dinner, to take a day off from sorrow, to throw yourself into work. According to the model, those moments aren't avoidance or betrayal. They're part of the rhythm. And so are the evenings when the loss comes back in full.

@chapter A06 cafe_sunset | Grief has no timetable
Another popular belief is that there's a normal time for grief, such as a year, after which a person should be over it. Research doesn't support a fixed timeline. Most people feel intense grief in the first months, which then gradually softens, but waves can return for years, particularly on anniversaries, birthdays and other reminders.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Bonanno, Wortman, Lehman, Tweed, Haring, Sonnega, Carr & Nesse | Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 2002 | Resilience to loss and chronic grief: a prospective study from preloss to 18 months postloss
George Bonanno and his colleagues followed older married people before and after the death of a spouse. They found different patterns. Some people were resilient, showing little disruption. Some had chronic grief, with long-lasting distress. And a group of people who had been depressed before the loss improved afterwards. The picture was far more varied than any single set of stages could capture.

@char A01 cafe_sunset | The variety | There is no one way to grieve
That's one of the most important findings in this field. Grief doesn't follow a single path or timetable. Some people cry for months. Some feel calm, and are worried by it. Some feel relief, especially after a long illness. All of these can be normal. And all of them can be accompanied by love.

@list A03 living_room_day | Words to keep from this chapter | Stage theory ;; Dual process model ;; Oscillation ;; Prolonged grief
Four terms from this chapter. Stage theory is the idea that grief follows fixed phases. The dual process model describes swinging between facing loss and coping with life. Oscillation is that swinging itself. And prolonged grief is intense longing and distress that lasts and interferes with daily life, which is recognised as a condition that can be treated. Picture this scene. A woman sits at the kitchen table a month after her father's funeral. Her sister calls and asks how she is getting on, and she says she laughed at a joke on the radio that morning and felt terrible about it. Her sister tells her that she has done nothing wrong. Grief is not a test, and a laugh is not a betrayal. It is the swing between loss and life that researchers describe, and it is usually a sign of a mind doing its quiet work. Notice, too, that her sister did not offer a theory or a stage. She offered permission, and permission is often what grieving people need most.

@list A12 living_room_day | Being kind about your own grief | You don't have to feel what others expect ;; You can have good days without guilt ;; You can have a hard day long after ;; Seek help if it feels stuck or unbearable
Here is how the research might shape the way you treat your own grief, or someone else's. You don't have to feel what other people expect, or in the order they expect it. You can have good days without guilt, and you can have a terrible day a long time later. Both are normal. And if the pain feels stuck, or unbearable, or if you're having thoughts of harming yourself, please talk to a doctor, a counsellor or a crisis line in your country. Prolonged grief is a recognised condition, and there are treatments that help. Asking for help is not a sign that you've failed at grieving.

Waves, coming in and going out. The dual process model describes grief in just this way, not as a staircase to climb but as a tide that moves in both directions, and that gradually, in its own time, settles.

@char A15 cabin_fireplace | A gentle thought | You are not doing it wrong
If anyone has ever told you that you should be over it by now, or that you're stuck in the wrong stage, the research is on your side. There's no required order, no deadline and no single way. What you're feeling is what grief looks like in you, and it deserves patience.

@char A08 cabin_fireplace | Looking back | What the stages taught us
So what did we learn? That the five stages came from dying patients, not from the bereaved. That a study of two hundred and thirty-three bereaved people found no fixed order. That grief often swings between facing loss and getting on with life. And that there's no standard timetable.

In the next chapter, we'll look at a finding that surprised many researchers themselves. Most people who go through something terrible turn out to cope far better than anyone, including them, expected.
