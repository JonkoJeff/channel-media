The path bends under a low stone bridge, and for a few steps the sound of Hugo's footsteps echoes back at him. On the far side, an old lamp throws a circle of yellow light on the water. He's thinking about that steam engine image, the idea that tears let something harmful out. It's a comforting thought. Something builds up, and crying lets it go.

@char A03 autumn_overlook | The belief | Tears flush out toxins
Here's the second belief in its most popular form. Crying, the story goes, is the body's way of flushing out stress hormones and toxins. That's why you feel lighter afterwards: the bad chemicals have literally left your body in your tears. You'll find this idea in magazine articles, on wellness websites and in friendly advice from people who mean well.

@char N20 attic_rain | Where did it come from? | A real scientist's hypothesis
The idea didn't come from nowhere. It grew partly out of the same work we met a moment ago. In 1985, William Frey proposed that crying might remove stress hormones and other substances from the body. It was a genuine scientific hypothesis, put forward by a researcher who had actually collected and analysed tears, and it was a bold and interesting one.

@list A03 cafe_sunset | An old belief, long before 1985 | Roman poets wrote of tears as relief ;; Darwin noted children may cry for relief ;; Breuer and Freud linked expression to release, in 1895 ;; Popular magazines praised a good cry for over a century
In fact, the idea that crying brings relief is much older than any chemistry. A review in the journal Frontiers in Psychology traces it back to classical antiquity, including the Roman poet Ovid. Charles Darwin, writing in 1872, noted that children may cry for relief. In 1895, Josef Breuer and Sigmund Freud argued that expressing emotion brings release, and that holding it in could harm health.

@study A15 street_day | A researcher named Cornelius | Published in 1986 | Popular magazines from 1848 to 1985
Then there's a lovely piece of detective work. A researcher named Cornelius looked through popular American and British magazines published between 1848 and 1985, searching for what they said about crying. Across those many decades, the message was remarkably steady. Crying was widely described as good for you, both for your mind and for your body, and holding back tears was often treated as risky.

@char A13 bedroom_night | A belief that feels true | Because it often does feel good
So the toxin idea arrived in a world already convinced that crying is healthy. It offered a tidy, physical explanation for something people already believed. And it matches an experience many of us know: after a long cry, the body often does feel calmer, emptier and heavier, almost as if something has been drained away. But a feeling of relief isn't proof that anything has left in the tears.

@char A05 living_room_day | Hugo's mother | Have a good cry
Hugo's mother believed this completely. When he was small and came home upset, she'd sit him on the kitchen stool and tell him to let it all out, because tears washed away the hurt. He always did feel better afterwards, though looking back, that may have had more to do with her arm around his shoulders and the biscuit that usually followed than with anything leaving through his eyes.

An old iron lamp reflected in the still water of a canal at night, its light stretched into a long golden line. It's a fitting picture for this belief, because what we see in the water looks solid and real, but it's a reflection of something somewhere else. The relief after crying is real. The question is where it actually comes from.

@char N14 bedroom_night | Testing the idea | What would we expect to see?
If tears really carried away stress hormones in meaningful amounts, a few things should follow. The hormones should be measurable in tears. People who cry more should clear more of them. And, importantly, animals that don't shed emotional tears shouldn't show the same drop in stress hormones after distress. That last test turns out to be a revealing one.

@study A10 cabin_fireplace | Vingerhoets and Kirschbaum | Reported in 1997 | Saliva samples and crying intensity
In 1997, Ad Vingerhoets and a colleague named Kirschbaum reported that the stress hormone cortisol, measured in saliva, fell more in women who said they had cried more intensely. At first glance, that looks like support for the flushing idea. More crying, less stress hormone. But a drop in cortisol after an emotional event can happen for many reasons, and it doesn't tell us that the hormone left through the eyes.

@study N20 autumn_overlook | Bayart and colleagues | Reported in 1990 | Young rhesus monkeys after separation
And here's the revealing test. In 1990, a team led by a researcher named Bayart reported a similar fall in cortisol in young rhesus monkeys after they had been separated from their mothers. These monkeys call out in distress, but they don't produce emotional tears at all. If the same drop in stress hormone happens without any tears, then tears can't be the main route by which it leaves.

@list A03 living_room_day | What reviewers concluded | Hormone removal by tears seems unlikely ;; The volume of tears is tiny ;; Other body changes may explain the calm ;; The idea is still repeated as fact
The researchers who wrote that Frontiers in Psychology review, Asmir Gračanin, Lauren Bylsma and Ad Vingerhoets, considered the removal of hormones through tears unlikely. They suggested that if crying soothes, it probably does so through other routes, such as changes in the nervous system, in breathing, or in how other people respond. We'll look at those routes later tonight.

@stat N07 rooftop_dusk | A single tear contains about | 0.3 mg | of salt, by one hospital estimate
It helps to remember just how small tears are. That hospital estimate we met earlier puts the salt in a single tear at around a third of a milligram. Asking such a small amount of liquid to carry a meaningful share of the body's stress hormones away is asking rather a lot of it.

@char N20 cafe_sunset | The verdict | A myth, but a kind one
So what's the verdict on the second belief? As a description of what's happening inside the body, the toxin story is very probably a myth. There's no good evidence that tears wash out stress chemicals in any amount that matters. But the experience it tries to explain, feeling calmer after crying, is real for many people, at least some of the time. We'll come back to why.

@char A20 rooftop_dusk | Hugo and the engine | A better picture
Hugo smiles to himself. He spent forty years with engines, and he knows the steam image never quite fitted. Engines don't calm down because they leak. They calm down because something changes in how they're running. Perhaps crying is more like that, he thinks. Not a leak, but a shift in gears. It's not a bad guess at all.

On the far side of the bridge, a dog barks somewhere behind a garden wall, and then whines, a thin high sound. Hugo pauses. Do dogs cry? He's seen his neighbour's spaniel with wet eyes. Our third belief is about exactly that: the idea that animals weep, in sadness, just as we do. Let's find out what the evidence says.

@list A10 autumn_overlook | The belief on trial | Tears remove stress chemicals ;; Stress hormones can fall after crying ;; But they also fall without tears ;; The relief likely comes from elsewhere
Before we leave this stop, let's lay out the pieces once more. The belief says that tears remove stress chemicals. It's true that stress hormones can fall after a cry. But they also fall in animals that shed no emotional tears at all. So the relief, where it happens, very likely comes from somewhere other than the tears themselves.
