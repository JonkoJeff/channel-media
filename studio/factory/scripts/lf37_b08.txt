@chapter A13 cafe_sunset | Introvert or extravert?
Myth number five: you're either an introvert or an extravert, so pick one. It's one of the most popular ideas about personality there is. People put it in their profiles, mention it on first dates, and use it to explain why they left a party early. And it does capture something real. But the way we usually say it, as two separate camps, isn't quite what the research shows.

@char A12 living_room_day | Two teams | Or one long line?
The myth imagines two teams. On one side, the quiet readers who recharge alone. On the other, the lively talkers who light up a room. You belong to one team or the other, and that's that. It's a tidy picture. It's also the same kind of box we met at the start of the evening, just with a single question instead of four.

@char A03 attic_rain | What the research says | A dimension, not a choice
So what does the research actually say? Extraversion is a dimension, one of the five dials we just met. When large groups of people take careful personality tests, their scores don't split into two separate piles. Most people land somewhere around the middle, with fewer and fewer people the further out you go toward either end. The two teams are really one long line.

@char A15 cafe_sunset | Nora in the middle | Both and neither
Nora, our imagined friend, is a good example. She loves a lively dinner with friends. She also loves a long, silent Sunday morning. Some quizzes call her an introvert, others call her an extravert, depending on the day she takes them. That isn't her being confusing. It's what being near the middle of a dial looks like.

@char A10 rooftop_dusk | A curious question | What if you just acted it?
Now here's where things get interesting. If extraversion is a dial, can we turn it a little, just for an afternoon? And if we did, what would happen to how we feel? A group of researchers decided to find out, not by asking people what they're like in general, but by checking in with them again and again during ordinary days.

@study A13 living_room_day | Fleeson, Malanos and Achille | Studies of everyday mood and behaviour | published in 2002
William Fleeson, Abigail Malanos and Noelle Achille published their work in two thousand and two. In one part, people reported several times over a stretch of days how they were behaving and how they felt. In another part, people were asked to act more extraverted, or more introverted, during a small group discussion, and then to say how they felt.

@char A19 autumn_overlook | The finding | A small mood lift
The pattern was surprisingly clear. In the moments when people were acting more extraverted, talking more, being more bold and lively, they tended to report more positive feelings. And here's the part that surprised many readers. This was true even for people who scored as introverted. Acting a little more outgoing seemed to come with a lift in mood, for most people.

@char A20 cafe_sunset | Not a rule | Just a tendency
That doesn't mean every introvert should force themselves into every party. It's an average pattern, and averages hide a lot of variety. It also doesn't say people were pretending to be someone else. Most of us act more lively at some moments and more quiet at others. The study simply looked at what those moments tend to feel like.

@char A05 cafe_sunset | But there's a catch | What about later?
But maybe you're thinking, wait a moment. A lively evening might feel good while it's happening. What about afterwards? Anyone who has driven home after a busy gathering, quietly exhausted, knows that question. And a later study looked at exactly that. Not just how people feel in the moment, but how they feel a few hours down the road.

@study A12 living_room_day | Sointu Leikas and Ville-Juhani Ilmarinen | A study of mood and tiredness | published in 2017
Sointu Leikas and Ville-Juhani Ilmarinen published their study in two thousand and seventeen. Its title asks the question nicely. Happy now, tired later? Like the earlier work, it followed people through their daily lives, checking in on what they were doing and how they felt, both straight away and a few hours later.

@char A18 rooftop_dusk | Happy now | Tired later
What they found fits both sides of the story. Acting more extraverted again came with a lift in mood in the moment. But a few hours later, it tended to come with more tiredness. The good feeling was real. So was the bill that arrived afterwards. Being lively, it seems, uses up a bit of energy, for many of us.

@quote A13 cabin_fireplace | Happy now, tired later. | The title says it all
Happy now, tired later. It's almost a little poem about a certain kind of evening. And it gently explains why so many people feel both things at once. They enjoyed the party, and they're glad to be home. Neither feeling cancels out the other. Both are simply part of how a dial in the middle works.

@chapter A10 cafe_sunset | The middle has an advantage
So far, we've seen that most people sit near the middle of this dial. But is there anything special about the middle? Or is it just a sort of in-between place, neither one thing nor the other? One study, in a rather unexpected setting, suggests the middle might have some real advantages of its own.

@study A03 living_room_day | Adam Grant | A study of people in sales jobs | published in 2013
Adam Grant, an organisational psychologist, published the study in two thousand and thirteen. It looked at people working in sales, a job where many assume that the most outgoing, talkative person always does best. He measured where each person sat on the extraversion dial, and then looked at how well they did at their sales calls.

@char A15 autumn_overlook | The ambivert advantage | Not the loudest
The result went against that popular picture. The people who did best weren't the most extraverted, and they weren't the most introverted either. It was the people in the middle, sometimes called ambiverts, who did better at their sales calls than either extreme. Grant called it the ambivert advantage.

@char A19 cabin_fireplace | Why the middle? | Talking and listening
Why might that be? One gentle idea is balance. People in the middle can be warm and talkative when a conversation needs energy. But they can also stop, listen, and leave room for the other person. Someone at the far end might talk a little too much. Someone at the other end might hold back a little too long. The middle can move both ways.

@list A12 cafe_sunset | What we've found | A dial, not two teams ;; Most people sit near the middle ;; Acting lively often lifts mood ;; It can bring tiredness later ;; The middle has strengths too
So let's gather it up. Extraversion is a dial, not two teams. Most people sit near the middle. Acting a bit more lively often comes with a lift in mood, even for introverts. That lift can bring some tiredness a few hours later. And the middle of the dial isn't a muddle. It has real strengths of its own.

@char A20 living_room_day | Both ends are fine | No wrong setting
And none of this means one end of the dial is better than the other. People far toward the quiet end often bring focus, depth, and careful listening. People far toward the lively end often bring energy, warmth, and courage in a crowd. The research doesn't rank them. It simply shows that most of us live somewhere between.

@char A05 cafe_sunset | Nora's answer | Somewhere in the middle
So the next time a quiz asks Nora whether she's an introvert or an extravert, she might smile at the question. The honest answer is neither, and both. She lives somewhere in the middle, like most people do. Lively at dinner, quiet on Sunday. Happy at the party, glad to be home. And that's not indecision. That's simply her.
