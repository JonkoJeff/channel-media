So let's go back, gently, to the lecture hall. Ivy was nineteen. A tall boy two rows down waved cheerfully in her direction, and she lifted her hand and waved back with a big smile, just in time to see him greet the girl sitting behind her. She remembers lowering her hand very slowly, and pretending she'd been fixing her hair. It's been about ten years.

@char A12 cafe_sunset | Back then | The first week was the worst
Here's something interesting. If you'd asked Ivy about that wave on the evening it happened, she'd have described a hot, crushing feeling. She told no one. She avoided that row for a week. But if you ask her about it now, in daylight, the answer is different. She might wince, and then laugh a little. The memory is still there. The feeling attached to it has changed.

@char A03 living_room_day | That isn't luck | It's a pattern
This isn't just Ivy being lucky, or particularly easygoing. Psychologists have found that it's a fairly reliable pattern in how human memory works. The feelings attached to our memories don't all stay the same strength. They fade over time. And for most people, they don't fade evenly. Some kinds of feelings fade faster than others.

@study A19 cabin_fireplace | Walker, Skowronski and Thompson | Review of General Psychology, 2003 | Life is pleasant, and memory helps to keep it that way
In 2003, the psychologists Richard Walker, John Skowronski and Charles Thompson published a review in the Review of General Psychology with a lovely title. Life is pleasant, and memory helps to keep it that way. They brought together studies in which people kept diaries of real events in their lives, rated how pleasant or unpleasant each one felt at the time, and then rated the same events again weeks, months or even years later.

@list A10 attic_rain | What the diaries showed | Feelings faded over time ;; Unpleasant feelings faded faster ;; Pleasant feelings lasted longer ;; The memory of the event stayed
The pattern they found has a name now. The fading affect bias. Affect is simply the psychologist's word for feeling. In these studies, the feelings attached to memories faded over time. But the unpleasant feelings tended to fade faster than the pleasant ones. The good memories kept more of their warmth, while the bad ones lost more of their sting. And the memory of the event itself often stayed.

@char A18 cafe_sunset | Real lives, real time | Not a laboratory trick
What makes this work so convincing is that it wasn't built on made-up situations in a laboratory. These were real days in real lives. A missed bus. A kind word from a stranger. An argument with a flatmate. A wonderful meal. People wrote them down while they were still fresh, and then came back to them later, so researchers could see how the feelings had changed with their own eyes.

@char A13 attic_rain | Not for everyone | A pattern, not a promise
It's worth being careful here. This is a tendency found across many people, not a promise for every single memory. Researchers have found that the effect can be smaller in people who are going through a period of low mood, for instance. And some memories, especially of truly serious events, can work differently. But for everyday slips like a misplaced wave, the pattern fits most of us remarkably well.

@quote A12 living_room_day | The event stays, but the sting fades faster than the warmth. | The fading affect bias, in one line
So here's the fading affect bias in one line. The event stays, but the sting fades faster than the warmth. Think about your own memories for a moment. A holiday that went wrong in a dozen small ways. A disastrous first attempt at baking bread. Many of them have turned, without your permission, into stories you rather enjoy telling.

@char A15 autumn_overlook | Ivy's own collection | Old stings, now stories
Ivy has a few of these herself. The time she walked confidently into a glass door at a train station. The school play where she forgot her only line and simply said, um, very loudly. Each of those burned once. Now they're the stories her family brings up at birthdays, and she's usually the one laughing hardest. The sting left. The story stayed.

@chapter A18 cafe_sunset | Memories are rebuilt | Not played back, but put together
But why would feelings fade at all? Part of the answer lies in how memory works underneath. Remembering isn't quite the same as playing back a recording.

@study A03 living_room_day | Frederic Bartlett | University of Cambridge, 1932 | People retold a strange folk tale again and again
One of the earliest people to show this was the British psychologist Frederic Bartlett, at the University of Cambridge. In his book Remembering, published in 1932, he described asking people to read an unfamiliar folk tale, called The War of the Ghosts, and then retell it, again and again, over time. With each retelling, the story changed. It grew shorter, and odd details slipped away or were replaced.

@char A15 autumn_overlook | Making sense of it | The mind fills in the gaps
What Bartlett noticed was that the changes weren't random. People tended to reshape the strange story into something that made more sense to them, closer to the kinds of stories they already knew. He concluded that remembering is an act of reconstruction. Each time, we build the memory again, from a few kept pieces and a lot of sensible guessing.

@char A01 street_day | Ivy's version | It has been rebuilt many times
Now think of Ivy's wave. She has remembered it many times over the years. Each time, she rebuilt it a little. Some details have blurred. She's no longer sure what the boy was wearing, or which lecture it was. And with each rebuilding, she was a slightly older, slightly kinder version of herself, who knew a little more about how small these moments really are.

If she pictures the lecture hall now, it comes back in warm, slightly hazy colors, like an old photograph. The light through the tall windows. The creak of the wooden seats. The room has become a kind of memory postcard. And the embarrassing moment is one small detail inside it, rather than the whole picture.

@char A03 living_room_day | The edges blur | And that's a kindness
There's a quiet kindness in that blurring. The parts that hurt the most, the exact look on the boy's face, the heat in her cheeks, have become a little hazy. What's left is mostly the shape of the story. A young woman, a mistaken wave, a hand slowly lowered. It's almost the plot of a gentle comedy now, rather than a scene she has to relive.

@chapter A19 cabin_fireplace | A window for change | What happens when we recall
And researchers have found something even more striking about what happens in the moment we bring a memory back.

@study A10 attic_rain | Nader, Schafe and LeDoux | New York University, published in Nature, 2000 | Rats with a learned fear memory
In a study published in the journal Nature in 2000, Karim Nader, Glenn Schafe and Joseph LeDoux worked with rats that had learned to associate a sound with an unpleasant shock. When the rats were reminded of that memory, and the scientists then briefly blocked a process the brain needs to store memories, the old fear memory was weakened. Without the reminder, the same treatment had no such effect.

@char A18 cafe_sunset | Reconsolidation | Memories open up when we recall them
The idea that came out of this, and many later studies, is called reconsolidation. When a memory is brought back, it seems to become briefly changeable again, before it is stored once more. This was work in rats, under very controlled conditions, and nobody should take it as a do-it-yourself method. But it adds to the picture that Bartlett painted. Memories aren't sealed away. They open up when we use them.

@char A13 bedroom_night | Which works both ways | Recalling can sharpen or soften
That openness works in both directions, and this is the part that matters for Ivy tonight. If she brings the memory back while panicking, adding harsh words about herself, she may store it again with a little extra sting. If she brings it back with a softer, wider view, maybe even a smile, the version she stores might be a little gentler. How we remember can shape what we keep.

@quote A03 rooftop_dusk | Each time we remember, we have a small chance to tell the story a little more kindly. | A gentle way to think about it
This is a gentle thought, and it's worth stating carefully, because the science is still being worked out. But here it is. Each time we remember, we may have a small chance to tell the story a little more kindly. We can't erase the wave. Nobody can, and that's all right. But we can choose the tone of voice we use when it comes back.

@char A12 cafe_sunset | Tonight, a kinder telling | The same story, a softer voice
So tonight, when the wave comes back, Ivy can try telling it a little differently. Not, I can't believe I did that. But something more like, oh, that eager nineteen-year-old, so ready to be friendly. It's the same story. She isn't changing the facts. She's only changing the voice that narrates it, from a stern critic to a fond old friend.

@chapter A12 living_room_day | From cringe to comedy | When it becomes a funny story
And you may have noticed this already in your own life. Many embarrassing moments go through a particular journey.

@list A15 cafe_sunset | The journey of an embarrassing moment | It burns ;; It aches when it returns ;; It makes you wince ;; It makes you laugh ;; It becomes a story you share
At first, it burns. Then, for a while, it aches whenever it comes back. Later, it only makes you wince a little. Then one day, it makes you laugh. And eventually, for many people, it becomes a story worth sharing with friends, the kind that begins with, oh, you'll love this, I once did the most ridiculous thing.

@char A01 street_day | Where Ivy is now | Somewhere between wince and laugh
Ivy's lecture hall wave is somewhere in the middle of that journey. It still makes her wince at half past one in the morning. But in daylight, she can almost laugh at it. And the moment with the waiter tonight, the little 'you too', is right at the start of the journey. Give it a few weeks, and it may well be a story she tells her friend over coffee.

@char A08 cabin_fireplace | Tonight's memory is young | And young memories are loud
That's a useful thing to remember on nights like this. A new embarrassment is like a young memory, still loud and raw. It hasn't yet had time to travel. It hasn't been rebuilt by a calmer, more rested self. Of course it feels big right now. It's supposed to, at this stage. And almost everything we know about memory suggests it won't stay this size.
