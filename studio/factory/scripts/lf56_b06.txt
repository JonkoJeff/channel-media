@chapter A19 cabin_fireplace | The laughing rats
The animal was the rat. In the late 1990s, the American neuroscientist Jaak Panksepp was studying the sounds rats make during play. Young rats love to play-fight, rolling and pinning each other. And while they do, they make high-pitched chirps, far above the range that human ears can hear. Panksepp wondered what those chirps might mean.

@char N01 living_room_day | A surprising idea | Tickling a rat
So he tried something that sounds almost like a joke. Using a special detector that could pick up these ultrasonic sounds, he and his colleagues gently tickled young rats with their hands, imitating the rough play the rats enjoy with each other. And the rats responded with a burst of those high chirps.

@study N09 bedroom_night | Jaak Panksepp and Jeffrey Burgdorf | Physiology and Behavior, 2003 | Laughing rats and the evolutionary antecedents of human joy?
Panksepp and his colleague Jeffrey Burgdorf described this work in several papers, including a review published in 2003 with a question in its title: laughing rats and the evolutionary antecedents of human joy? They found that tickled young rats made a particular chirp, at around fifty kilohertz, far above human hearing, especially when tickled on the back of the neck and the belly.

@list A20 rooftop_dusk | What the tickled rats did | Chirped at high pitch ;; Followed the tickling hand ;; Came back for more ;; Chirped less when frightened
The rats didn't just chirp. They followed the tickling hand around the cage and came back for more, as if they'd learned that the hand meant fun. And when they were frightened or uncomfortable, they chirped much less. It looked a great deal like play, and the chirps looked a great deal like a sign of enjoyment.

@char N14 neon_alley_rain | Laughing rats? | Careful words
Panksepp was willing to call these chirps a kind of laughter, and the phrase laughing rats caught the public imagination. Other scientists were more cautious, and with good reason. A rat's chirp sounds nothing like a human laugh, and we can't ask a rat how it feels. But the parallels with play and tickling in children were hard to ignore.

@study N20 bedroom_night | Shimpei Ishiyama and Michael Brecht | Science, 2016 | Neural correlates of ticklishness in the rat somatosensory cortex
In 2016, two researchers in Berlin, Shimpei Ishiyama and Michael Brecht, went further. They tickled adult rats while recording from cells in the part of the brain that processes touch. Rats, it turned out, are very ticklish, especially on the belly, and they chirped happily while being tickled. Some even jumped for joy, little leaps of excitement.

@char N07 street_day | Tickling in the brain | Cells that fired with the fun
They found cells in the touch area of the brain that fired strongly during tickling, and also while the rats played chasing games with the researcher's hand. When the researchers stimulated those cells directly, the rats chirped, even without being touched. Ticklishness, it seemed, had a home in the brain, closely tied to the urge to play.

@char A03 attic_rain | Mood matters | Anxious rats were less ticklish
And then a telling detail. When the rats were placed somewhere that made them anxious, high up on a platform under bright light, they were much less ticklish. They chirped less, and the tickle cells fired less. Fear switched off the fun. It's the same pattern we see in people: we laugh at tickling from a friend, but not when we're frightened.

@list A10 cafe_sunset | What the rat studies suggest | Laugh-like play sounds exist beyond primates ;; They're linked to play and tickling ;; Fear and anxiety shut them down ;; Their meaning for the rat is still debated
So what do the rat studies suggest? That something resembling laughter, a play sound tied to tickling and fun, may exist well beyond the primates. That it's closely linked to play. That fear and anxiety shut it down. And that how much it resembles our own laughter, inside the animal's experience, is still a matter of debate.

@char A13 cabin_fireplace | Other playful animals | Sounds of play
Researchers have also described play sounds in a range of other animals, though the evidence is patchier and the interpretation harder. What seems clearer is the general pattern. In many social mammals, play comes with its own special signals, faces, postures or sounds, that help keep rough play friendly. Human laughter may be our own, unusually loud, version.

@char N27 autumn_overlook | Why that's comforting | We're not alone in this
There's something rather comforting in this. When Nadia laughed until she cried at a teapot lid, she was taking part in something very old and very widely shared. The joy of play, and the sounds that go with it, seem to run deep in the animal world. We may be the only animals who laugh at jokes, but we're not the only ones who laugh at all.

@char A04 attic_rain | A word on the evidence | Still a young field
As always, a word on the evidence. The rat studies are careful and often repeated, but they're done in laboratories with a small number of research groups. Their interpretation, whether this truly counts as laughter in any sense we'd recognise, is still argued about. It's best to hold the phrase laughing rats lightly, as a useful idea, not a settled fact.

@char A19 autumn_overlook | Back on the street | A tickle memory
As Nadia walks, she remembers being tickled as a child by her father, squealing and begging him to stop while laughing too hard to mean it. It's such a strange thing, when you think about it. She was pleading for it to end, and at the same time, she didn't want it to end at all. Tickling is full of contradictions.

@char N09 cafe_sunset | The tickle puzzle | Why can't you do it yourself?
And there's one contradiction that almost everyone has noticed at some point. Try to tickle yourself, and nothing happens. Your own fingers, in the same place, at the same speed, simply don't work. Why should that be? It turns out to be one of the neatest little puzzles in the science of laughter, and that's where we're going next.
