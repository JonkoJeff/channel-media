@char N02 living_room_day | An old question | Is a dream a wandering thought?
There's an old suspicion in psychology that dreams and daydreams are related. Both are made of images, stories and feelings that aren't tied to the room you're in. Both seem to draw on memories and worries and hopes. And both arrive without being asked. So could dreaming be a kind of mind wandering, in a different state?

@char N19 cafe_sunset | A modern version | The default network in sleep
That idea has been taken up by researchers using brain imaging. If the default mode network is central to mind wandering, as we've seen, then it's natural to ask whether it's active during dreaming, too. And there is some evidence that it is, particularly during rapid eye movement sleep, when vivid dreams are most common.

@study N07 autumn_overlook | Kieran Fox and colleagues | Frontiers in Human Neuroscience, 2013 | A review of brain imaging and dream reports that compared dreaming with waking mind wandering
In 2013, Kieran Fox, Savannah Nijeboer, Elizaveta Solomonova, William Domhoff and Kalina Christoff, published a paper in Frontiers in Human Neuroscience, with the title Dreaming as mind wandering. They compared studies of brain activity during dreaming with studies of mind wandering when awake, and also compared how people described their dreams and their wandering thoughts.

@char N23 rooftop_dusk | What they found | A striking overlap
They found an overlap, in both the brain and the experience. Regions of the default network were active in both states. And dreams and waking mind wandering shared a similar quality, with a flowing sequence of images and scenes, often about the dreamer's own concerns and relationships, and not tightly controlled by any goal.

@char N10 living_room_day | The differences too | Not the same thing
They also pointed to differences. Dreams are usually more vivid and more hallucinatory than a daydream, and during most dreams we're not aware that we're dreaming. A daydream, even a deep one, is usually felt as something we're imagining. So the researchers suggested that dreaming may be a more extreme and immersive relative of mind wandering, not a twin.

@char N02 cabin_fireplace | Another line of evidence | The brain's hot zone
Another line of work comes from the neuroscientist Francesca Siclari and her colleagues. In a 2017 paper in Nature Neuroscience, they woke people at intervals and asked whether they'd been dreaming. They found that a region at the back of the brain, which they called a posterior hot zone, seemed to be more active when people reported dreaming, whether or not it was REM sleep.

@char N19 street_day | What that suggests | Dreaming is not only REM
It's a fascinating finding because it suggests that dreaming isn't confined to a single stage of sleep, and that it's linked to specific patterns of brain activity. It also fits the picture of an inward-turned brain, producing experience without much input from the outside.

@char N09 autumn_overlook | A practical question | Do dreams follow the day?
Another long-standing idea, called the continuity hypothesis, is that dreams tend to reflect what a person thinks about and does when awake. Researchers such as Michael Schredl have studied this for many years, and in a 2003 paper with Hofmann, in the journal Dreaming, they reported that a lot of dream content does relate to the dreamer's daily life.

@list N11 cafe_sunset | How dreams and daydreams resemble each other | Both are built from memories and imagination ;; Both often concern personal worries and relationships ;; Both involve the default mode network ;; Neither is tightly controlled by a goal ;; Both can be vivid, but dreams are more immersive
Laid side by side, the likenesses are striking. Both are woven from memory and imagination. Both often concern personal worries and relationships. Both involve the default network. And neither is held under tight control. The main difference is how immersive the experience becomes.

@char N10 cabin_fireplace | A hint from the lab | Dreaming of a task
There's one more result worth mentioning, with some caution. In 2010, Erin Wamsley and colleagues published a study in Current Biology, in which people learned to find their way through a virtual maze and then took a nap. Those who reported dreaming about the maze during the nap tended to do better on it afterwards. It was a small number of people, so it should be treated as a curiosity, not a conclusion.

@quote N27 rooftop_dusk | Perhaps the mind keeps working on what matters, even when we're not looking. | a gentle reading of the maze study
That's a gentle way of reading it, though no more than that. It suggests that the night mind may not be idle, and that what it does may be connected to what we care about.

@char N02 living_room_day | A stubborn thought | The white bear in the night
There is, however, a darker side to the night mind, and it ties in with what we said earlier about loops. In 2004, Daniel Wegner, David Wenzlaff and Megan Kozak published a paper in Psychological Science with the title Dream rebound. They asked volunteers to think about a person who'd been on their mind, and then to either suppress thoughts of that person or mention them freely, for several minutes before bed.

@study N07 autumn_overlook | Daniel Wegner, David Wenzlaff and Megan Kozak | Psychological Science, 2004 | People who tried to suppress thoughts of a person before bed reported dreaming about that person more than those who did not
The people who'd tried to suppress thoughts of the person dreamed about them more than the people who hadn't, and more than those who'd simply thought about them freely. The effect was strongest in people who tended to suppress their thoughts in general. Wegner called it dream rebound.

@char N19 rooftop_dusk | What to make of it | Pushing away can backfire
It's a small study, based on people's reports of their own dreams, so it's best treated as suggestive. But it fits with a much older line of work from Wegner, in which trying not to think of something makes it come back. And it hints that the night may return what the day tried to push away.

@char N10 attic_rain | A kinder lesson | Let it be said
If so, the kindest approach to a troubling thought before sleep may be to give it a hearing, in some way, and let it be said, rather than to push it away. We'll look at some ways to do that in the next block, with evidence for what seems to help.
