@chapter A12 cafe_sunset | Not alone
So far tonight, we've mostly looked at stress as something that happens inside one head, one body. Jonah on the platform, Jonah on the train, Jonah's heart thumping on its own. But people almost never face stress completely alone. And it turns out that other people change what stress does to us, sometimes in surprisingly physical ways.

@char A10 living_room_day | Back in the office | A quick word
Let's go back to Jonah. It's ten to nine. He has made it to the office, a little out of breath, and he's standing outside the meeting room with his notes in his hand. A colleague walks past, sees his face, stops, and puts a hand on his shoulder for a second. You've got this, she says. Then she's gone.

@char A13 cafe_sunset | A tiny moment | Does it matter
It's a tiny moment. Three words and a hand on the shoulder. And yet Jonah feels something loosen, just a little. His breath goes a bit deeper. Is that just a nice feeling, or is something real happening in his body? Psychologists have spent a long time trying to answer exactly that question.

@chapter A05 autumn_overlook | Fight, flight, or friends
For most of the twentieth century, the classic picture of stress was the one we met at the start of the night. Fight or flight. An animal faces a threat, and it either stands its ground or runs. It's a powerful picture. But in the year 2000, a group of researchers pointed out that it was missing something rather important.

@study A03 living_room_day | Shelley Taylor and colleagues | University of California, Los Angeles, 2000 | Tend and befriend: a second pattern in how we respond to stress
Shelley Taylor and her colleagues at the University of California, Los Angeles, published a long paper in Psychological Review. They noticed that many of the classic stress studies had mostly looked at males, both animals and humans. And they proposed that there is a second common pattern alongside fight or flight. They called it tend and befriend.

@list A01 cafe_sunset | Tend and befriend | Tend: protect and care for others ;; Befriend: reach out and connect ;; Especially studied in women ;; A theory, still debated
The idea goes like this. When stress arrives, many of us don't only fight or flee. We tend, meaning we protect and look after the people who depend on us. And we befriend, meaning we reach out and gather with others. Taylor's team suggested this pattern might be especially strong in women, and that hormones like oxytocin might play a part. It was a proposal, not a final answer, and researchers still argue about the details.

@char A15 autumn_overlook | Something familiar | You've seen this
But you've almost certainly seen it in your own life. After a frightening piece of news, people phone each other. After a hard day, someone wants to talk it through over dinner. When a storm is coming, neighbours check on each other. Reaching for other people isn't a weakness in the stress response. It may be one of its oldest parts.

@chapter A19 cabin_fireplace | A hand to hold
So what happens inside the brain when someone is there with us? Not just in our feelings, but in the actual activity of the brain, at the very moment a threat appears? One of the most elegant answers comes from a small experiment with a simple, rather touching design.

@study A18 living_room_day | James Coan, Hillary Schaefer and Richard Davidson | University of Wisconsin, 2006 | Lending a hand: holding a hand under threat of a mild shock
In 2006, James Coan, Hillary Schaefer and Richard Davidson published a study they called Lending a hand. They invited married women to lie in a brain scanner. Every so often, a symbol on a screen warned them that they might receive a mild electric shock to the ankle. It wasn't dangerous, but it was unpleasant, and not knowing exactly when it would come made it stressful.

@stat A10 cafe_sunset | Married women in the scanner | 16 | took part in the study
Sixteen women took part. During the threat, each of them was in one of three situations. Sometimes she held her husband's hand. Sometimes she held the hand of a stranger, a member of the research team she had never met. And sometimes she held no hand at all.

@char A13 rooftop_dusk | The result | A calmer brain
When the women held no hand, the brain areas linked to threat and alarm were busy, as you'd expect. Holding a stranger's hand calmed some of that activity. But holding their husband's hand calmed it the most. The threat was exactly the same. The shock was exactly the same. What changed was who was there.

@char A12 living_room_day | One more detail | The quality matters
And there was one more detail that the researchers found especially interesting. The calming effect of the husband's hand was stronger for women who rated their marriage as happier. It wasn't simply any hand that mattered, or even any familiar hand. It was a hand that felt safe. The feeling of being with someone you trust seemed to reach all the way down into the brain's alarm system.

@char A03 cabin_fireplace | Sharing the load | A brain that expects company
Coan later developed this into a wider idea. Our brains, he suggested, seem to expect other people to be around. When someone we trust is close, the brain doesn't have to spend quite so much effort preparing for danger on its own. Some of the load is shared.

Think of it like two cups on a table at the end of a long day. Nothing is solved by sitting down together. The problem is still the problem. But the weight of it feels different when someone else is sitting across from you.

@chapter A20 cafe_sunset | Friends and hormones
Brain scans are one way to look at this. Another is to measure the stress hormone we met earlier, cortisol, and see whether support from another person changes how much of it the body releases.

@study A01 living_room_day | Markus Heinrichs and colleagues | University of Zurich, 2003 | Social support and oxytocin before a stressful public test
In 2003, Markus Heinrichs and his colleagues in Zurich used the Trier Social Stress Test, the same nerve-racking routine we met earlier, with a speech and mental arithmetic in front of a stony-faced panel. Before the test, some of the men in the study spent time with their best friend, who had come along to support them. Others prepared alone.

@stat A18 cafe_sunset | Healthy men in the study | 37 | some supported by their best friend
Thirty seven healthy men took part. And there was a second twist. Before the test, each man was also given a nasal spray. For some, it contained oxytocin, a hormone involved in social bonding. For the others, it was a placebo, a spray with nothing active in it.

@list A03 living_room_day | What happened to cortisol | Alone with placebo: the highest rise ;; Friend or oxytocin: a smaller rise ;; Friend and oxytocin together: the lowest
The men who prepared alone and got the placebo had the biggest rise in cortisol. Having their best friend there lowered it. Oxytocin alone tended to lower it a little too. And the men who had both, the friend and the oxytocin, had the lowest cortisol of all, and they also said they felt calmer and less anxious during the test.

@char A19 cabin_fireplace | A careful note | Small studies
Now, a careful note. Both of these studies were small, and the story of oxytocin in particular has turned out to be more complicated than the early headlines suggested. Later research on oxytocin sprays has produced mixed results. So it's wiser to hold on to the broader idea, that support seems to soften the stress response, than to any one hormone.

@chapter A15 autumn_overlook | Over a lifetime
That broader idea also shows up when researchers zoom out, from one stressful afternoon in a lab to whole lives. Instead of measuring a single hormone on a single day, they ask a much bigger question. Over the years, do the people around us change how our lives unfold?

@study A13 living_room_day | Julianne Holt-Lunstad, Timothy Smith and Bradley Layton | Brigham Young University, 2010 | A meta-analysis of social relationships and survival
In 2010, Julianne Holt-Lunstad, Timothy Smith and Bradley Layton combined the results of many earlier studies that had followed people over years, recording how connected they were to others and how long they lived. Some studies asked about marriage or living alone, others about friendships, family contact or taking part in community life.

@stat A10 cafe_sunset | Studies combined | 148 | following people over the years
They brought together one hundred and forty eight studies, with more than three hundred thousand people in total. On average, people with stronger social relationships had about a fifty percent greater chance of still being alive by the end of the follow-up period than people with weaker ones.

@char A13 attic_rain | Not cause and effect | Just a strong link
These were observational studies, so they show a link, not proof of cause and effect. People who are healthier may also find it easier to keep friendships going, for example. But the link was strong, and it held across many different countries and kinds of studies.
