@chapter A13 attic_rain | When time stretches
So far tonight we've looked at things in space: colours, faces, hands, gorillas. But there is one more thing your brain builds that we almost never question. It's time itself.

@char A10 cabin_fireplace | A familiar story | Everything slowed down
You may have heard someone describe a near accident like this. The car skidded, and suddenly everything went into slow motion. I could see the glass, the tree, my own hands on the wheel, all happening so slowly. Maybe it has happened to you. A fall on the stairs, a glass slipping off the table, a bike that wobbles at just the wrong moment.

@char A19 living_room_day | The belief | Danger slows the clock
It's a very common belief. When we're in danger, time itself seems to stretch, as if the brain switches into a high speed mode, taking more snapshots per second, so that we have more time to react. It's a lovely idea. It would be a useful superpower. But is that really what happens?

@list A03 cafe_sunset | Two possibilities | Your brain really sees faster ;; Or it only remembers more ;; Both would feel the same afterwards ;; Only a test can tell them apart
There are really two ways to explain the slow motion feeling. One is that, in the moment, your brain truly speeds up, like a camera switching to a higher frame rate. The other is that the moment is simply remembered differently, packed with more detail, so that looking back, it feels longer. From the inside, after the fact, these two would feel exactly the same. So how could anyone tell them apart?

@chapter A12 autumn_overlook | A scientist with a falling idea
The neuroscientist David Eagleman had a memory of his own. As a boy, he fell from the roof of a house, and he remembered the fall as strangely slow and long. Years later, he decided to test the feeling properly.

@study A18 rooftop_dusk | Stetson, Fiesta & Eagleman | Baylor College of Medicine, published 2007 in PLoS ONE | Volunteers dropped backwards from a tall tower into a net, then judged how long their own fall had taken
In 2007, Chess Stetson, Matthew Fiesta and David Eagleman published a study in the journal PLoS ONE. The question was simple. Does time really slow down during a frightening event? To find out, they needed something truly frightening, but safe.

#ai lf30_b13_01.jpg | Editorial still-life of a large safety net stretched beneath a tall steel tower at an empty amusement park, early evening light, calm and quiet mood, muted colours, no people, no text, no logos

@photo lf30_b13_01.jpg | A fall into a net
They chose a ride at an amusement park in Texas, a kind of suspended catch air device. Volunteers were lifted high up, then dropped backwards, in free fall, into a net far below. There were no ropes and no harness to slow them. The fall was about 31 metres, roughly the height of a ten storey building. And it is, by every account, terrifying.

@stat A03 cafe_sunset | Their own fall felt longer | 36% | how much longer people judged their own fall than other people's (Stetson et al., 2007)
After landing, the volunteers were asked how long their own fall had lasted. Then they watched other people fall the same distance and judged those falls too. On average, people judged their own fall to have lasted about 36 percent longer than the falls they watched. So the feeling was real. Their own fall truly seemed longer.

@char A13 attic_rain | But the real test | Could they see faster?
But that only shows the memory of the fall was long. The real question was whether, during the fall, people actually saw the world faster. If the brain had switched into slow motion, they should be able to take in details that are normally too quick to see.

@chapter A12 living_room_day | A number on the wrist
So the researchers came up with a clever little gadget.

@study A10 cabin_fireplace | The perceptual chronometer | Worn on the wrist during the fall | A small screen flashed a number and its negative image, too fast to read at normal speed
They strapped a small display to each volunteer's wrist. It showed a number, then quickly the same number in reverse colours, switching back and forth. At normal speed, the flicker is just a little too fast for your eye to read the number. It all blurs into an even grey. If time really slowed down during the fall, the volunteers should suddenly be able to read it, the way you can read a spinning wheel in a slow motion film.

@list A03 cafe_sunset | What they found | No one read the fast numbers ;; Accuracy matched resting levels ;; Seeing did not speed up ;; Only the memory stretched
They couldn't. During the fall, people were no better at reading the flickering numbers than they were when they were standing safely on the ground. Their seeing had not sped up at all. What had changed was the memory of the fall afterwards. The brain doesn't run in slow motion during danger. It records more richly, and the recording feels longer when we play it back.

@chapter A05 autumn_overlook | Why memory stretches
Why would a frightening moment leave such a long, detailed memory?

@char A19 living_room_day | Fear records everything | A careful notebook
One idea is that strong emotion, especially fear, makes the brain lay down memories much more thoroughly. Many parts of the brain involved in emotion, like the amygdala, help strengthen memories of important events. So in those few seconds, the brain stores far more detail than usual. When you remember it later, your brain sees all that detail and concludes that a lot of time must have passed.

@quote A18 cabin_fireplace | More memories feel like more time | A simple rule your brain seems to follow
Put simply, the brain often judges time by how much it remembers. More memories feel like more time. Fewer memories feel like less.

@char A12 cafe_sunset | You do this too | Holidays and ordinary weeks
You may recognise this from everyday life. The first day of a holiday in a new place, full of new streets, new food, new sounds, can feel enormous when you look back. A routine week at home, where every day looks the same, can vanish almost without a trace. While it's happening, a boring hour drags. But afterwards, the exciting days are the ones that seem long.

#ai lf30_b13_02.jpg | Editorial still-life of an old brass pocket watch lying open on a wooden table beside a small stack of travel postcards, warm afternoon window light, cosy calm mood, no people, no text, no logos

@photo lf30_b13_02.jpg | Time, rebuilt from memory
So your sense of how long something took is not a reading from a clock inside your head. It's a reconstruction, built afterwards, from what you stored. Like the colours, the edges and the hands we met earlier, time is something your brain assembles.

@list A18 cabin_fireplace | Ways to make time feel fuller | Visit somewhere new ;; Try a different route ;; Notice small details ;; Write a line about your day
If more memories make time feel longer, that suggests a gentle little idea for everyday life. When the weeks seem to slip away too fast, you can give your memory more to hold. Visit somewhere new, even a nearby park you've never walked through. Take a different route home. Notice small details, the colour of a door, the smell of rain. Or write a single line each evening about what was different today. None of this changes the clock. But it may change how full your days feel when you look back on them.

@char A12 living_room_day | Why years seem to speed up | A common feeling
Many people also feel that the years go faster as they get older. Psychologists have offered several possible explanations, and no one is completely sure. But one idea fits neatly with what we've just seen. Childhood is full of firsts, and firsts leave rich memories. Later on, when more days follow familiar patterns, there may simply be fewer new memories to fill the years, so looking back, they seem shorter.

@chapter A10 cabin_fireplace | The myth versus the science
Let's set the belief and the evidence side by side.

@list A13 attic_rain | Time in danger | Belief: danger switches on slow motion ;; Science: perception stays the same speed ;; Belief: you see more in the moment ;; Science: you remember more afterwards
The belief says danger switches the brain into slow motion, so that you see more in the moment. The science says your perception keeps its normal speed, and what changes is the memory. You don't see more. You remember more, and memory is what makes the moment feel long.

@char A19 bedroom_night | A gentle caution | One study, one kind of fear
As always, it's worth keeping a little humility. This was one study, with a small number of brave volunteers, and one kind of fear, a short fall. Other kinds of danger might work differently, and scientists are still studying how the brain keeps track of time. But it's a beautiful example of how a feeling we're all sure about can turn out to be a story the brain tells afterwards.

@char A03 living_room_day | A bridge onward | Not everyone's world is the same
And that leads us toward our last belief of the night, perhaps the biggest of all. If your brain builds colour, sound, touch, pain and even time, then a natural question follows. Is the world your brain builds the same as the one built by the person next to you?
