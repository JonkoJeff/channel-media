@chapter A05 autumn_overlook | Wanting and liking
Let's step inside the brain now, and look for the place where rewards happen. The story starts with a mistake, in a laboratory in Montreal, in the early 1950s.

@study A12 rooftop_dusk | James Olds & Peter Milner | McGill University, 1954 | Found that rats would press a lever again and again to receive mild electrical stimulation in certain areas deep in the brain
In 1954, James Olds and Peter Milner, at McGill University, were placing tiny electrodes in the brains of rats. One electrode, it seems, landed in a slightly different place than intended. And the rat that had it behaved very strangely. It kept returning to the corner of the box where it had been stimulated.

@char A16 street_day | A lever | Pressed over and over
So Olds and Milner gave the rats a lever. Each press sent a small pulse of electricity to that spot. And the rats pressed, and pressed, and pressed. For some brain areas, they pressed at very high rates, for long stretches of time, as if nothing else in the box mattered.

@char A13 cabin_fireplace | The pleasure centre? | A catchy name
The newspapers, and many scientists, quickly called these spots pleasure centres. It seemed obvious. Why else would an animal press a lever so many times, unless it felt wonderful? The name stuck for decades. As you'll hear in a moment, it may have been the wrong word.

@char A03 living_room_day | A chemical messenger | Dopamine
Later work linked many of these sites to a chemical messenger called dopamine, released by a small group of cells deep in the brain. Dopamine became famous as the pleasure chemical. You've probably heard it described that way. Tonight, we'll see why that description is, at best, half the story.

@study A12 autumn_overlook | Wolfram Schultz & colleagues | Recordings in monkeys, 1980s and 1990s | Recorded single dopamine cells while monkeys learned that a light or sound predicted a drop of juice
In the 1980s and 1990s, Wolfram Schultz and his colleagues recorded the activity of single dopamine cells in monkeys. The monkeys sat calmly and learned a simple pattern. A light came on, or a sound played, and then, a moment later, a drop of fruit juice arrived.

@char A20 cafe_sunset | At first | The juice was a surprise
At first, before the monkeys had learned the pattern, the dopamine cells fired when the juice arrived. A little burst of activity. That fits the pleasure idea nicely. Juice arrives, the cells celebrate.

@char A04 attic_rain | Then it moved | From the juice to the light
But as the monkeys learned that the light meant juice was coming, something odd happened. The burst moved. The cells now fired when the light came on, and barely responded to the juice itself. The juice hadn't become less tasty. It had become expected.

@char A13 rooftop_dusk | And when it didn't come | A dip
And here's the most telling part. If the light came on, and then the juice didn't arrive, the cells did something else. At the moment the juice should have come, their activity dipped below its normal level. As if to say, that was worse than I thought.

@study A05 autumn_overlook | Wolfram Schultz, Peter Dayan & Read Montague | Science, 1997 | Showed that these dopamine signals look like a prediction error: the difference between the reward expected and the reward received
In 1997, Schultz teamed up with two computational scientists, Peter Dayan and Read Montague, and published a paper in Science. They showed that the cells' behaviour matched something from learning theory, called a prediction error. Better than expected, a burst. Exactly as expected, nothing much. Worse than expected, a dip.

@char A15 cafe_sunset | A teaching signal | Not simply pleasure
So dopamine, in this picture, is less like a reward and more like a teacher. It tells the brain when the world has turned out better or worse than predicted, so the brain can update its expectations. That's a very different job from simply feeling good.

@char A10 living_room_day | In everyday life | The second time
You can feel a version of this in everyday life. The first time a friend surprises you with your favourite cake, it's a delight. By the fifth birthday in a row, you still enjoy the cake, but the spark of surprise has gone. You expected it. The world turned out exactly as predicted, and there was nothing new to learn.

@study A12 rooftop_dusk | Kent Berridge & Terry Robinson | Published in 1998 | Found that rats with most of their dopamine removed still showed signs of liking sweet tastes, but no longer worked to get food
And then came the finding that gives this chapter its name. In 1998, Kent Berridge and Terry Robinson, at the University of Michigan, reviewed their experiments with rats whose dopamine systems had been largely destroyed. These rats would no longer seek out food on their own. They had to be cared for.

@char A04 neon_alley_rain | But they still liked it | Sweet is still sweet
Yet when a little sugar water was placed in their mouths, the rats still made the same pleased little faces that rats, and human babies, make for sweet tastes. Rhythmic licking of the lips, small tongue movements. Their liking seemed intact. What had gone was the wanting.

@list A03 living_room_day | Two different things | Wanting: the pull towards something ;; Liking: the pleasure when you get it ;; They usually travel together ;; But they can come apart
Berridge and Robinson argued that wanting and liking are two separate processes in the brain. Wanting is the pull towards something. Liking is the pleasure you feel when you get it. Most of the time they travel together. But they can come apart.

@char A10 bedroom_night | You've felt this | Wanting without liking
You've probably felt them come apart yourself. Reaching for your phone for the tenth time in an hour, without enjoying any of it. Or a snack you keep returning to, long after it stopped tasting good. Strong wanting, very little liking. Berridge's work suggests dopamine is closely tied to the first, and much less to the second.

@char A13 rooftop_dusk | Small and fragile | Where liking lives
Berridge's later work suggested that liking depends on a few small hotspots in the brain, which use other chemical messengers, such as the brain's own opioids. Wanting, by contrast, seems to be produced by a much larger and sturdier system. So, it seems, it's easier to make the brain want something than to make it truly enjoy it.

@study A13 cabin_fireplace | John Salamone & Merce Correa | Neuron, 2012 | Reviewed evidence that lowering dopamine in rats makes them choose easy, low-effort options over harder, more attractive ones
John Salamone and Merce Correa took this further. In a 2012 review, they described experiments in which rats could press a lever many times for a tasty food, or simply eat ordinary food lying freely nearby. With normal dopamine, many rats chose to work for the treat. With dopamine lowered, they tended to choose the easy food instead.

@char A15 autumn_overlook | Still hungry | Just less willing
Those rats weren't less hungry. They still ate. They simply became less willing to put in the effort. So dopamine seems to be bound up with whether a reward feels worth the work. That's a very useful way of thinking about mornings when you can't get going.

@photo lf32_b03_01.jpg | The pull comes before the taste
Picture a glass of juice on a counter, catching the light. The wanting arrives the moment you see it. The liking only comes later, when you actually take a sip.

@char A20 cafe_sunset | The joy of waiting | Anticipation
This also helps explain something many people notice. Looking forward to a holiday, or a visit, or a meal, can be wonderful in itself, sometimes as enjoyable as the thing when it arrives. The brain's wanting system is busy, and warm, long before anything actually happens.

@char A13 rooftop_dusk | Careful with the labels | Not a single chemical
A word of care, though. Dopamine is involved in movement, attention, learning and much more, and it works alongside many other chemicals. So calling it the wanting chemical is also a simplification. It's just a better simplification than calling it the pleasure chemical.

@char A15 autumn_overlook | What this means for us | Wanting can be borrowed
There's a hopeful side to all this. If wanting is driven by prediction and by cues, then it can be shaped. A small cue that reliably comes before something you enjoy, a particular song, a certain cup, a familiar chair, can start to carry some of that pull on its own. Over time, the cue itself begins to say, this is going to be good.

@list A03 autumn_overlook | The second stop | Rats pressed levers for brain stimulation ;; Dopamine tracks surprise ;; Wanting is not liking ;; Effort depends on dopamine
So that's our second stop. Rats pressed levers for stimulation deep in the brain. Dopamine cells track surprise, not simple pleasure. Wanting and liking are not the same. And the willingness to make an effort seems to lean heavily on dopamine.

@char A12 living_room_day | The next question | How rewards shape us
Which brings us to rewards themselves. If the brain is always learning from what turns out better or worse than expected, then the way rewards arrive should matter a great deal. And a century of experiments, starting with some very clever cats, shows that it does.
