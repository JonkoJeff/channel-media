@chapter A06 cafe_sunset | The rescuers
Some acts of helping are so costly that they test everything we have discussed so far. During the Second World War, in countries occupied by Nazi Germany, a small number of people hid, fed and helped Jewish neighbours, strangers and children at enormous risk to themselves and their families. Social scientists have asked a hard question. What was it about those people?

@char A04 attic_rain | A note of care | A sensitive subject
This is a topic that deserves care. The men and women who were hunted, and those who died, are not material for a puzzle. We approach this chapter with respect, and with the knowledge that the research can only tell us a little about something so large. What it can do is help us to understand how ordinary people came to do extraordinary things.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Oliner & Oliner | Free Press, 1988 | The altruistic personality: rescuers of Jews in Nazi Europe
Samuel Oliner, who survived the war as a child in Poland with the help of a Polish family, and his wife Pearl Oliner, interviewed hundreds of people. They talked to several hundred rescuers and to a comparison group of people who had lived in the same circumstances but had not helped. They asked about childhood, family, values and the moment of decision.

@list A03 living_room_day | What the Oliners looked for | How rescuers were raised ;; What values they described ;; How they explained their decision ;; How they compared with non-rescuers
Their findings were not simple, and the authors were careful to say that no single feature explained everything. But a few patterns stood out, and they have been discussed ever since.

@char A12 living_room_day | The pattern | Care, not just obedience
Rescuers more often described parents who had explained their reasons, rather than relying on punishment, and who had stressed caring about people beyond the family. Many said that they helped because it was the only thing they could do, that someone was in need and they were there. Few described it as a heroic decision.

@chapter A13 rooftop_dusk | A second look
Other scholars have explored similar ground. Kristen Monroe, a political scientist, interviewed rescuers, bystanders and others, and arrived at a related conclusion from a different angle.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Monroe | Princeton University Press, 1996 | The heart of altruism: perceptions of a common humanity
Monroe argued that what set altruists apart was a particular way of seeing themselves in relation to others. Many of the people she interviewed saw all people as fundamentally the same, as part of a common humanity, and felt they had no real choice. For them, helping was a matter of who they were, and not a calculation.

@list A12 living_room_day | Themes in rescuers' own accounts | I did what anyone should have done ;; There was no choice, I could not turn them away ;; I saw a person, not a category ;; I had been taught to care about everyone
Notice the connection with our last chapter. The rescuers' sense of us included people that their society told them to exclude. Their circle of belonging was unusually wide, and it held under extreme pressure.

@char A01 cafe_sunset | Cautions | Interviews look back
It's worth adding some cautions. These are accounts given years later, and memory shapes stories. The comparison groups were not always equal, and critics have asked whether the family patterns were as clear as they seemed. Not every rescuer fitted the pattern, and not every person who fitted it was a rescuer.

@chapter A19 living_room_day | Gradual steps
There is another thing that emerges from many accounts. Few rescuers began by making a huge commitment. Often, they began by helping once, for a night or a week, and then found themselves taking on more. A small act opened the door to a larger one.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Freedman & Fraser | Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 1966 | Compliance without pressure: the foot-in-the-door technique
Jonathan Freedman and Scott Fraser found that people who first agreed to a small request, such as displaying a small sign, were much more likely to agree to a larger one later. The pattern, called the foot-in-the-door effect, suggests that small acts of commitment can shift how people see themselves, as someone who helps.

@char A12 living_room_day | The link | Identity grows with action
This fits the rescuers' stories. After the first act, they saw themselves as people who help. The next request fitted that self-image. It's a hopeful idea, because it implies that the path to extraordinary kindness may begin with ordinary kindness, repeated.

@list A03 living_room_day | Words to keep from this chapter | Altruistic personality ;; Common humanity ;; Foot-in-the-door ;; Self-image
Four terms from this chapter. The altruistic personality is the set of features associated with those who help at a cost. Common humanity is seeing others as sharing your fundamental nature. The foot-in-the-door effect is the way a small commitment leads to a larger one. And self-image is how we see ourselves.

@list A12 living_room_day | Small commitments that build a helping identity | Agree to one small, regular act of help ;; Do it for a few weeks ;; Notice how it feels ;; Then ask whether you could do a bit more
Here is how you might use that insight. Agree to one small, regular act of help, such as checking in on a neighbour once a week. Do it for a few weeks. Notice how it feels. Then ask yourself whether you could do a little more. Picture this scene: a woman begins taking a neighbour's bins out on a Tuesday, because he has a bad hip. After a month, she also brings a loaf of bread. After a year, they are good friends, and she is the first person he calls when he is unwell. Nothing dramatic happened on any single day. A helping identity was built from Tuesdays.

@list A12 living_room_day | Pause and reflect | Who were the quiet helpers in my life? ;; What did they do first? ;; What small thing have I said yes to? ;; What would I like to become known for?
A pause to think about the quiet helpers in your own life. Almost everyone has one: a teacher who stayed late, a neighbour who watched your house, an aunt who always had a spare bed. Think about what they did first. Usually it wasn't a grand gesture. It was something small that they kept doing. The rescuers' stories had this quality, too. They began with a single night, a single meal, a single yes, and the habit of helping grew from there. Now think about what small things you have said yes to in the past year, and how they have shaped what you are asked to do. And ask yourself the gentle question: what would I like to become known for among the people close to me? Not as an ambition, but as a direction. A helping identity is built from many small yeses, and you can begin with the next one.

A candle in the window of a stone house at night. It's a quiet image for a subject of great weight. The people in this chapter are remembered because, when asked, they opened a door. We should hold the stories of rescuers with humility. We cannot know how any of us would have acted. But we can learn from the ordinary habits that surrounded their courage, and begin building them where we are.

@char A15 cabin_fireplace | A gentle thought | Extraordinary begins small
Few of us will ever face such a test. But the research on rescuers suggests that the habits behind great courage are ordinary ones: seeing people as people, explaining rather than commanding, and saying yes to small things.

@char A08 cabin_fireplace | Looking back | What the rescuers taught us
So what did we learn? That rescuers often came from families that stressed explanation and care. That many saw a common humanity and felt they had no choice. That small first acts led to larger ones. And that interviews looking back must be read with caution.
