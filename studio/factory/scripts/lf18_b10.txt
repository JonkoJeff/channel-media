@chapter A06 cafe_sunset | The strange stretch of time
You've probably had both experiences. A dull afternoon in a waiting room, in which a single hour seems to last half a day. And a weekend with friends that vanishes in what feels like a few minutes. The clock says that the hours were the same length. Your experience says that they were nothing of the sort.

@char A12 living_room_day | The oddity | Time has no sense organ
Time is a strange thing to perceive. For sight, you have the eyes, and for hearing, the ears. But there is no organ in your body that detects time. Instead, the brain has to estimate it, using a mixture of signals, and as a result, our sense of time can stretch and squeeze depending on what is going on.

@char A04 attic_rain | The question | Does time slow down in danger?
Many people who've been in a car crash or a fall say that time seemed to slow down. The moment was stretched, as if in slow motion. It's a common report, and it's been told in a thousand stories. So a neuroscientist in Texas decided to find out whether the brain really does speed up in a crisis.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Stetson, Fiesta & Eagleman | PLoS ONE, 2007 | Does time really slow down during a frightening event?
David Eagleman at Baylor College of Medicine and his colleagues took volunteers to an amusement park, where there is a ride called Suspended Catch Air Device, or SCAD. You are dropped backwards from a height of about thirty metres, falling freely for three seconds into a net. It's frightening, and nobody expects it to be pleasant.

@char A10 attic_rain | The test | A fast-flashing watch
The volunteers wore a special wristwatch that flashed numbers at a rate that was just a bit too fast to read at normal speed. The idea was that if the brain really sped up during the fall, like a camera running faster, then the numbers should become readable. The researchers wanted to see if anyone could read the numbers during the drop.

@stat A14 conspiracy_board | Result of the fall | No speed-up | the numbers were no easier to read during the fall than on the ground
It turned out that nobody could read them any better. The brain's moment-to-moment sampling of the world did not speed up. But when the volunteers were asked to estimate how long the fall had lasted, they said, on average, that it had taken about a third longer than it really did, when compared to watching someone else fall.

@list A03 living_room_day | What the study suggests | The brain does not run in slow motion in danger ;; But memories of frightening events are richer ;; A rich memory feels like a long event, looking back ;; The slowing is in the remembering, not the living
This is a lovely example of the difference between experience and memory. When you recall a terrifying moment, there's a thick layer of detail. The brain recorded more, and so, afterwards, it seems to have lasted longer. The slowness is a trick of hindsight.

@chapter A13 rooftop_dusk | A stretched moment
There's a simpler way to experience the stretching of time, and you can try it at home. Watch a series of identical pictures flash on a screen, one after another. Each lasts the same length. Then suddenly, an odd one, perhaps a different picture, appears in the series. That one seems to last longer, and you'd swear that it did.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Tse, Intriligator, Rivest & Cavanagh | Perception & Psychophysics, 2004 | Attention and the subjective expansion of time
In 2004, Peter Tse and colleagues at Dartmouth College tested this, and found that the surprise item was judged to be longer by a significant amount, sometimes by a third or more. They suggested that attention, when it is suddenly captured, stretches the experience of time, a phenomenon they called the subjective expansion of time.

@char A04 attic_rain | The oddball | Novelty feels long
It's known as the oddball effect. New or unexpected things seem to last a bit longer than repeated ones. That's one reason why the first day on holiday can feel so long, and the fifth so short. Novelty makes the brain pay attention, and attention gives time more weight.

@char A12 living_room_day | Why a busy day feels short | Many little moments
Another way to put it is that your sense of time depends on how much your brain records. A day packed with new things leaves behind many memories, and later seems long. A day of routine leaves behind very few, and later seems to have vanished. In the moment, though, an exciting time can pass quickly, because you were absorbed.

@list A03 living_room_day | The two clocks | Time in the moment: shaped by attention ;; Time in memory: shaped by how much was recorded ;; Busy and absorbed: fast now, long later ;; Bored and watching the clock: slow now, short later
That second pair is the paradox. A boring day is long while you live through it, but it leaves almost no trace, so afterwards it seems to have been short. A day full of novelty flies by, and then, in memory, is rich and long. That is why holidays seem so long when we look back on them.

@chapter A10 attic_rain | Does life speed up with age?
Most adults will say that time seems to move faster as they grow older. A year seems shorter at fifty than at ten. People have been saying this for centuries. Does the research back it up? The answer is interesting, and a little surprising.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Wittmann & Lehnhoff | Experimental Aging Research, 2005 | Age effects in perception of time
Marc Wittmann and Sandra Lehnhoff in Germany asked nearly five hundred people, aged between fourteen and ninety-four, how fast they felt that time was passing. For the last week, month or even year, they found hardly any difference between the age groups. But when asked about the last ten years, older people were more likely to say that it had gone fast.

@char A01 cafe_sunset | A hint | Looking back over decades
That suggests that the speeding up isn't about how each day feels. It's about how a long stretch looks in memory. As we get older, fewer things are new, and so there are fewer distinct memories to mark the years. Looking back, a decade that was full of routine is compressed into a short story.

@list A12 living_room_day | A few things that may stretch the years | Learn something new ;; Travel, even a short way ;; Change small routines ;; Notice and write down the details of a day
There's some logic to this list, based on the idea that novelty creates memories. It's not a promise, but it's a gentle suggestion. If you want a year to feel like a year, give it a few things to remember.

@char A04 attic_rain | A caution | Not a precise law
It's worth saying that there are other ideas about why time seems to speed up, including the proportion theory, in which a year is a smaller fraction of a long life. These ideas have not been tested to a settled conclusion. The honest answer is that memory and attention matter, and that the full story is still being worked out.

@chapter A12 living_room_day | Fixing the order of events
Time perception contains another curious trick. Your brain has to decide what happened first. When you press a button and a light flashes, you feel the press and the flash as simultaneous, even though the signals take different times to travel through the nervous system. How does it manage this?

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Stetson, Cui, Montague & Eagleman | Neuron, 2006 | Motor-sensory recalibration leads to an illusory reversal of action and sensation
In 2006, Eagleman's team showed that the brain recalibrates. Volunteers pressed a button, and a light flashed after a tiny delay. After they'd done that many times, the brain adapted to the delay and treated it as the normal state. Then, when the light was flashed without a delay, they reported that it came before they pressed the button. Cause and effect seemed to run backwards.

@char A10 attic_rain | What it means | Time is stitched
The effect shows that the sense of simultaneity is something that the brain adjusts. It isn't reading a clock. It's lining up signals from different senses and different speeds into a single, sensible story. You can notice the effect when sound and picture are slightly out of step on a video call, and you've adapted to it after a while.

@char A19 cabin_fireplace | Another echo | The chronostasis of the first chapter
You might remember the stopped clock in the second story, when the second hand seemed to hang for a moment after a glance. That was the same kind of editing. The brain is continually tidying the timeline, and most of the time, it's so good at it that we never notice.

@stat A06 cafe_sunset | The speed of the sound and the light | Different by a lot | light reaches you almost at once, sound takes about three seconds per kilometre
This is why a thunderstorm gives you a little lesson. You see the lightning first and hear the thunder later, and the gap tells you how far it is. At short distances, the brain nudges the two together, a bit like a good film editor, so that a hand clapping a few metres away looks and sounds simultaneous.

@list A03 living_room_day | What the brain tidies up | Differences in signal speed ;; Gaps during eye movements ;; The order of cause and effect ;; Small delays that get adapted away
It's a very helpful service, and mostly invisible. A world in which every signal arrived on its own schedule would be bewildering. We experience the present as a single, shared moment, but that moment is stitched together a fraction of a second after the fact.

@list A12 living_room_day | Ways to make a day feel longer | Try something new ;; Take a different route ;; Pay attention to small details ;; Write down one memory each evening
If time feels as though it's racing, the research on memory suggests a few gentle remedies. Novelty creates distinct memories, which later stretch a period of time. A different route, a new recipe or a small adventure each week gives your memory more to hold. It won't add hours, but it can make the weeks feel more like your own.

@list A12 living_room_day | Words for what you just learned | Chronostasis ;; Oddball effect ;; Temporal recalibration ;; Retrospective duration
Four terms from the time chapter. Chronostasis is the stopped clock illusion. The oddball effect is the stretching of a surprising moment. Temporal recalibration is the brain's adjustment of delays between action and result. And retrospective duration is how long something seems to have lasted when you look back.

@char A15 cabin_fireplace | A gentle thought | Making time feel full
If time is something that the brain builds, then you have some say in how it feels. Paying attention to a good moment, putting the phone away, noticing the colour of the evening: these small acts give the moment more weight. They don't give you more hours, but they can make the hours feel more like yours.

@char A08 cabin_fireplace | Looking back | What time taught us
So what did we learn about time? That the brain doesn't speed up in danger, but memory makes danger seem long. That novelty and attention stretch moments. That our sense of the years depends on how much we remember. And that the order of events is stitched together by an invisible editor.
