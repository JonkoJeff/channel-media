In July of 1961, a few months after the trial of Adolf Eichmann began in Jerusalem, a psychologist at Yale University named Stanley Milgram started advertising for volunteers. He offered a small payment to take part in what the notice called a study of memory and learning. What he really wanted to know was darker, and he wanted it to be real.

@char A12 living_room_day | The question | How far would you go?
The question was this. During the Second World War, people had carried out terrible acts and later said they were only following orders. Was that a flaw in a particular nation, a particular culture? Or could ordinary people, in an ordinary American city, be led to do something harmful by a calm, official voice?

@char A04 attic_rain | The setup | Two volunteers, one draw
Here's how the experiment worked. Two people arrived at the laboratory at the same time. One was told he would be the teacher, and the other the learner. The drawing seemed random, but it was not. The learner was an actor, a mild, middle-aged man, and the draw was fixed so that the real volunteer was always the teacher.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Stanley Milgram | Yale University, 1961 to 1962 | Volunteers asked to give increasing electric shocks to a learner
The learner was strapped into a chair in the next room, with an electrode on his wrist. The teacher sat in front of a machine with thirty switches, labelled from fifteen volts up to four hundred and fifty. Each time the learner got a word pair wrong, the teacher was told to give a shock, one level higher each time.

@char A10 attic_rain | Nobody was actually hurt | The machine was fake
Let's say clearly: there were no real shocks. The machine was a prop, and the learner's cries were pre-recorded. But the teacher didn't know that. As the voltage rose, the learner grunted, then protested, then shouted that his heart was bothering him, then screamed, and finally went completely silent.

@char A08 cabin_fireplace | The voice | A researcher in a grey coat
Next to the teacher stood the experimenter, in a grey laboratory coat. If the teacher hesitated, he didn't threaten or shout. He used four calm, scripted prompts. Please continue. The experiment requires that you continue. It is absolutely essential that you continue. You have no other choice, you must go on.

@list A03 living_room_day | The four prompts | Please continue ;; The experiment requires that you continue ;; It is absolutely essential that you continue ;; You have no other choice, you must go on
Read them again, slowly. There's no anger in any of them. That's part of why the experiment is so unsettling. Nobody is being forced at gunpoint. The pressure comes from a quiet, polite authority in a quiet, polite room.

@char A04 attic_rain | What people expected | A tiny number
Before running the study, Milgram asked a group of psychiatrists to predict how many people would go all the way to the highest voltage. They guessed that only a tiny fraction, perhaps one person in a thousand, would. Most people, they thought, would stop long before the end.

@stat A14 conspiracy_board | Went to 450 volts | 65% | in the basic version, 26 of 40 men
In the basic version of the experiment, twenty-six of forty men, sixty-five percent, continued all the way to the final switch. Not one of them stopped before three hundred volts, the point at which the learner pounded on the wall. These results were published in 1963 in the Journal of Abnormal and Social Psychology.

@char A15 cabin_fireplace | Not cold-hearted | They were in distress
It would be wrong to picture the volunteers as cruel. Milgram's films show them sweating, trembling, laughing nervously, biting their lips and arguing with the experimenter. Many of them protested and asked to stop. They simply did not stop. Obedience and distress existed side by side.

@chapter A13 rooftop_dusk | Changing the room
Milgram ran many variations, and they are the most interesting part. When the learner was in the same room as the teacher, obedience fell. When the teacher had to push the learner's hand onto a shock plate, obedience fell further. And when the experiment moved from Yale to a shabby office building in a nearby town, it dropped again, but not to zero.

@bars A05 autumn_overlook | How obedience changed | % who went to the top | Learner in another room:65 ;; Same room:40 ;; Hand on plate:30 ;; Experimenter by phone:21
These are rounded figures from Milgram's own variations, and they tell a consistent story. The closer the victim, the lower the obedience. The more distant the authority, the lower the obedience. When the experimenter gave orders by telephone instead of standing right there, many people quietly gave lower shocks than they claimed.

@char A01 cafe_sunset | When authority fell apart | Two rebels
In one version, two other actors, posing as fellow teachers, refused to go on. And with that, nearly everyone else refused too. Just as in the room with the lines, the presence of other people who said no made saying no feel possible. Almost every version shows the same lesson about company.

@char A10 attic_rain | The part people forget | Ethics and doubts
Now for the second chapter of this story. The experiments were controversial from the start. Volunteers were deceived, and some were visibly upset. Milgram did debrief them, and in a follow-up questionnaire, most said they were glad they had taken part. But those studies could not be run today, and most would not pass modern ethics review.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Gina Perry | Behind the Shock Machine, 2012 | A critical look at Milgram's archive
In 2012, the Australian psychologist Gina Perry published a book based on Milgram's archives at Yale. She found that the experimenter sometimes went beyond the scripted prompts, and that some volunteers said afterwards they did not fully believe the shocks were real. These doubts do not erase the findings, but they complicate the neat story.

@char A12 living_room_day | A modern replication | Burger, 2009
In 2009, the psychologist Jerry Burger at Santa Clara University ran a careful, ethically modified version. To protect participants, he stopped the experiment at a hundred and fifty volts, the point where Milgram's learner first cries out. Then he compared the results with the original. At that point, a similar proportion of people were willing to continue.

@stat A06 cafe_sunset | Willing at 150 volts | About 70% | in the 2009 partial replication, similar to Milgram's
So the basic pattern seems to survive, even in the twenty-first century. The most important reading is also the more modest one: situations can pull on people, authority can be persuasive, and many of us are less able to say no than we hope. But, as Milgram's own variations showed, it's also easy to change.

@char A19 cabin_fireplace | A common misreading | Not just following orders
It's also worth saying what the experiment does not show. It does not show that every person will obey every command. And later historians and psychologists, including Alexander Haslam and Stephen Reicher, have argued that people obey not out of blind submission but because they come to believe in the cause.

@list A03 living_room_day | Two ways to read the study | People obey authority almost automatically ;; People follow when they believe the cause is worthy ;; Both can be true at different moments ;; Both leave room to say no
Neither reading is comfortable, and neither is hopeless. If people can be led by a calm voice, they can also be led by a calm voice in a good direction. What matters is whether we pay attention to what we're being asked to do, and whether anyone is hurt.

It's strange to think that a plain room and a plain coat could carry so much weight. But that is rather the point. Authority isn't always loud. Often it looks like a clipboard and a polite voice, and the first step is simply to notice when you're being asked to continue.

@char A08 cabin_fireplace | Looking back | What Milgram left us
So what did Milgram leave us? A reminder that ordinary people can do things that trouble them when someone in charge says it's necessary. A reminder that the distance from the person we might harm matters. And a reminder that one voice saying no, a refusal, can change everything.

@char A15 cabin_fireplace | A gentle thought | The right to pause
If you take anything from this story, take this: you're allowed to pause. You're allowed to ask what the purpose is, who is affected, and whether this is something you'd be willing to explain to the person on the other side of the wall. That question alone has stopped many people from going further.

@char A01 cafe_sunset | A small echo | Everyday obedience
Most of us will never be asked to press a switch in a laboratory. But obedience is woven through ordinary life. We follow a manager's instruction, a doctor's advice, a sign on a door, a stranger in a high-visibility jacket. Nearly always, that's sensible, because institutions could not work otherwise. Milgram's lesson isn't that obedience is bad. It's that it can slip past our judgement unnoticed, especially when the person giving the instruction sounds calm and certain, and when the cost lands on someone we cannot see.

@char A10 attic_rain | The quiet exits | How people said no
It's also worth looking at how the people who stopped did it. Many of them didn't make a speech or storm out. They asked a question, said they were worried, and then, at some point, simply declined to go on. In Milgram's films, the moment of refusal is often quiet and polite. That's useful to know. Saying no to a person in authority doesn't require a dramatic scene. It can be a calm sentence, spoken early, before the pressure has had time to build.

@char A12 living_room_day | The wider debate | Obedience and responsibility
Milgram's results also raised a difficult question about responsibility. When a person acts on instruction, who is responsible for the outcome? Courts, armies and companies have wrestled with it for a long time. The modern answer in many places is that following an order is not a full defence, and that each person keeps some duty to ask whether an instruction is lawful and humane. Milgram gave that principle a vivid, uncomfortable illustration.
