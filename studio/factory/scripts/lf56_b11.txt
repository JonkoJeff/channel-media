@chapter A12 autumn_overlook | The sound of friendship
Nadia's phone buzzes in her pocket. It's a message in the group chat from the five of them, a single photo of the teapot lid in the sugar bowl, captioned simply, never forget. She laughs out loud, alone on the empty street, and the sound surprises her. It's the first time tonight she's laughed with nobody beside her. Except, of course, she isn't really alone.

@char A13 rooftop_dusk | Laughter as glue | Holding friendships together
Earlier we heard Provine's idea that laughter is mostly about relationships. If that's right, then laughter should be closely tied to friendship itself. It should help make friends, keep them, and even reveal them to people listening from outside. That last idea, that strangers might be able to hear friendship in a laugh, was tested in a remarkable study.

@study A19 cabin_fireplace | Gregory Bryant and colleagues | Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 2016 | Detecting affiliation in colaughter across 24 societies
In 2016, Gregory Bryant and a large international team recorded pairs of American university students laughing together in conversation. Some pairs were close friends. Others had only just met. The researchers then cut out very short clips of the two people laughing at the same moment, usually lasting about a second, and played them to listeners.

@stat A20 street_day | Societies where listeners took part | 24 | in Bryant and colleagues, 2016
The listeners came from twenty-four different societies around the world, from large cities to small hunter-gatherer and farming communities. None of them knew the people in the recordings, and many didn't speak English. For each short clip of shared laughter, they were asked a simple question. Are these two people friends, or strangers?

@char N27 cafe_sunset | Friendship can be heard | Even in a second of laughter
Across all twenty-four societies, listeners did better than chance at telling friends from strangers, from a second or so of shared laughter alone. Friends' laughter, it seemed, sounded different: often more spontaneous and more relaxed. People everywhere, it appears, can hear something of friendship in the way two people laugh together.

@char N01 living_room_day | Why that matters | Laughter as a public signal
That's a striking result. It suggests that shared laughter isn't only something friends enjoy privately. It's also a signal to everyone around them, broadcasting, in a sense, these two belong together. For our ancestors, living in groups where alliances mattered, being able to hear who was close to whom could have been genuinely useful.

@study N09 bedroom_night | Laura Kurtz and Sara Algoe | Personal Relationships, 2015 | Putting laughter in context: shared laughter as behavioral indicator of relationship well-being
Shared laughter also seems to matter inside relationships. In a study published in 2015, Laura Kurtz and Sara Algoe recorded romantic couples talking about how they first met. They then measured how much each couple laughed together, at the same moment, during the conversation. Each partner also reported how they felt about the relationship.

@char N29 autumn_overlook | Laughing together | Linked with feeling close
Couples who shared more laughter during the conversation tended to report feeling closer and more satisfied, and more supported by their partner. It's important to say this was a correlation. It doesn't prove that laughing together causes closeness. Happy couples may simply laugh more. But shared laughter does seem to go hand in hand with feeling good about each other.

@study N20 bedroom_night | Alan Gray, Brian Parkinson and Robin Dunbar | Human Nature, 2015 | Laughter's influence on the intimacy of self-disclosure
Another study, published in 2015 by Alan Gray, Brian Parkinson and Robin Dunbar, looked at strangers. Groups of people who didn't know each other watched either a comedy clip or a neutral video together. Afterwards, each person wrote a short message introducing themselves to another participant, to help them get to know each other.

@char A15 rooftop_dusk | More open after laughing | Sharing a little more
The people who'd laughed together at the comedy shared more personal information in their messages than those who'd watched the neutral video. Interestingly, they didn't seem to realise they were doing it. Laughter, it appeared, quietly lowered their guard, making them more willing to open up, which is one of the first steps in becoming friends.

@char A03 attic_rain | Laughter and making friends | A shortcut to closeness
Put these studies together and a picture begins to form. Laughter may help strangers open up. It seems to go with closeness in couples. And it's audible to outsiders as a sign of friendship. It's no wonder that when people describe their closest friends, they so often say something like, we just laugh so much together.

@char N14 conspiracy_board | A caution | Not a magic formula
A caution, though. None of this means you can build a friendship by forcing laughter. Forced laughter, as we've seen, is often easy to hear. And laughing at someone, rather than with them, can do the opposite of bonding. The laughter that seems to bring people together is usually shared, relaxed and real, and it grows from feeling safe, rather than causing it from nothing.

@list A10 cafe_sunset | What shared laughter seems to do | Signals friendship to others ;; Goes with closeness in couples ;; Helps strangers open up ;; Works best when real and shared
So what does shared laughter seem to do? It signals friendship to others, even across cultures. It goes hand in hand with closeness and satisfaction in couples. It may help strangers open up to each other. And it seems to work best when it's real, shared and relaxed. Not a trick, but a sign of something already growing.

Within seconds, the replies come in, more laughing faces, a photo of the empty restaurant, a promise to meet again before Christmas. Twenty years of friendship, she thinks, held together partly by moments like tonight.

@char A13 cabin_fireplace | Laughing alone, but not quite | A memory of company
And her laugh on the street, the one that surprised her? It wasn't really a solitary laugh at all. It was laughter at a message from friends, about a moment shared with friends. Even when we laugh alone, it's often because other people are present in some way, in a memory, a message or a voice in our heads.
