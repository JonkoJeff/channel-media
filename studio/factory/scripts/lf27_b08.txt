@chapter A19 living_room_day | Whose side are we on?
Here is a question that every one of us can answer from experience. Are we more likely to help someone who seems to be one of us? Most people say yes. And the research agrees, with a twist that is both sobering and hopeful.

@char A04 attic_rain | The setting | A football ground
The experiment took place in the north of England, where football runs deep. Mark Levine and his colleagues at the University of Lancaster recruited supporters of Manchester United, and asked them to fill in a questionnaire about their feelings for the club. Then they were asked to walk to another building to watch a video.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Levine, Prosser, Evans & Reicher | Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin, 2005 | Identity and emergency intervention: how social group membership and inclusiveness of group boundaries shape helping behavior
On the path between the buildings, a jogger ran past, slipped and fell, grabbing his ankle and groaning in pain. He was a research assistant. In one version, he wore a Manchester United shirt. In another, a Liverpool shirt, the club of their great rivals. In a third, a plain sports shirt. The question was whether the fans would stop.

@stat A14 conspiracy_board | Fans who helped the fallen jogger | Over 90% vs about 30% | when he wore a Manchester United shirt versus a Liverpool or plain shirt
The difference was dramatic. Almost all of the fans helped when the jogger wore their own club's shirt. When he wore the rival club's shirt, or a plain one, only about a third did. The shirt, a piece of cloth, had changed who counted as one of us.

@char A12 living_room_day | The sobering part | In-group bias
This result fits a long line of research on group loyalty. Even small, arbitrary differences between groups can shape how people treat each other. Henri Tajfel's famous experiments in the 1970s showed that people divided into groups on trivial grounds soon began to favour their own group.

@chapter A13 rooftop_dusk | The hopeful twist
But the Lancaster team did not stop there. They asked a second question. What if the fans were reminded of a different shared identity? Instead of thinking about being a United supporter, what if they thought about being a football fan in general?

@list A03 living_room_day | The second version of the study | A new group of fans filled in a different questionnaire ;; It asked about being a football supporter, not a United supporter ;; Then they met the same fallen jogger ;; He wore a United shirt, a Liverpool shirt or a plain one
The result was striking. When the fans had been prompted to think about the broader category of football supporters, they were about as likely to help the jogger in the Liverpool shirt as the one in the United shirt, while the jogger in the plain shirt was less likely to be helped. A wider sense of us had expanded who deserved help.

@char A04 attic_rain | The conclusion | Boundaries can be redrawn
The researchers concluded that helping is shaped by how people categorise others. When the categories are narrow, help tends to flow inward. When the categories are broader, help can reach farther. The lesson is that the boundaries of us are not fixed. They can be redrawn, sometimes in a single sentence.

@char A04 attic_rain | The wider research | Common identity
This idea has support elsewhere. Samuel Gaertner and John Dovidio developed a model of common in-group identity, which proposes that intergroup bias can be reduced when people come to see themselves as members of a shared larger group. Experiments in the laboratory and in schools have found evidence for this effect.

@study A04 conspiracy_board | Gaertner & Dovidio | Psychology Press, 2000 | Reducing intergroup bias: the common ingroup identity model
Gaertner and Dovidio described how recategorisation, shifting from us and them to a single we, can improve attitudes and behaviour between groups. They noted that the approach has limits, because people also value their distinct identities, and a shared identity works best when subgroup identities are respected rather than erased.

@list A12 living_room_day | Practical ideas from the common identity model | Look for what you share before what divides ;; Keep subgroup identities, don't erase them ;; Use inclusive language: we, us, our ;; Create shared tasks and goals
The last point echoes a classic study you may remember: the Robbers Cave experiment, in which rival groups of boys were brought together by shared goals. We covered it in an earlier video. Common tasks do what slogans can't: they give people a reason to depend on one another.

@chapter A06 cafe_sunset | Everyday boundaries
It's tempting to see in-group bias as something that happens in stadiums. But the same process works in offices, in neighbourhoods, in schools and in families. Who is one of us? Who gets the benefit of the doubt? Who is invisible?

@char A01 cafe_sunset | An honest question | Who do I overlook?
It is uncomfortable but useful to ask. Whom do I help without thinking, and whom do I pass by? The answer is rarely about conscious dislike. It's usually about who feels familiar. And familiarity can be widened, by contact and by curiosity, as we'll see.

@list A03 living_room_day | Words to keep from this chapter | In-group ;; Out-group ;; Minimal group ;; Common ingroup identity
Four terms from this chapter. The in-group is the group we belong to. The out-group is a group we do not. A minimal group is one created on trivial grounds. And a common ingroup identity is a shared, larger category that includes both groups.

@list A12 living_room_day | A habit for widening your circle | Notice who you automatically help ;; Ask who you tend to overlook ;; Find one thing you share with them ;; Start with a small, concrete act
Here is a habit for widening your circle. Notice who you help without thinking. Ask yourself who you tend to overlook. Find one thing that you share with them. And start with a small, concrete act. Picture this scene: a man in a new office realises that he always chats to the people who went to the same university, and that the quiet colleague by the window never gets invited for lunch. One day, he asks her about the plant on her desk. They discover that they both grow tomatoes. By the end of the month, they eat together on Fridays. The shared fact did the work that no speech could.

@list A12 living_room_day | Pause and reflect | Who counts as us for me? ;; Whom do I help without thinking? ;; Whom do I overlook? ;; What is one thing I share with someone different?
Here is a pause about the edges of us. Everyone has a circle within which help comes easily. It might be your family, your friends, your colleagues, your team, or people from your town or country. The shirt experiment showed how quickly the edge of that circle can move. Think about whom you help without thinking. Then think about whom you might overlook, and ask yourself why. Usually it isn't dislike. It is unfamiliarity. We don't see them, and so we don't notice what they need. A good way to widen the circle is to find one thing you share with someone who seems different, a hobby, a worry, a favourite food, a hometown. It doesn't have to be deep. The researchers found that a single shared category was enough to change what people did. What could that shared thing be for you?

#ai lf27_b08_01.jpg | Editorial still-life illustration of two scarves in different colours hanging side by side on a coat rack in a hallway, soft light, muted palette, calm documentary mood, no people, no text, no logos

@photo lf27_b08_01.jpg | Two scarves, one rack
Two scarves in different colours, hanging on one rack. The studies suggest that the colours matter less than we think, and that the rack matters a lot. A shared place, a shared task or a shared label can change whom we see as one of us. The lesson of the shirt is both uncomfortable and freeing. It is uncomfortable because our loyalties can narrow our care. It is freeing because the same research shows that a wider sense of we can be reached with a single reminder.

@char A15 cabin_fireplace | A gentle thought | Circles can grow
If you notice that your circle of help is narrow, that's not a verdict on your character. It's a starting point. The research suggests that circles widen when we find the thing we share, and act on it. Nobody expects you to like everyone. The research asks for something smaller and more doable, which is to notice when your circle has shrunk without your meaning it to, and to find one honest thing you share with someone outside it.

@char A08 cabin_fireplace | Looking back | What the jogger taught us
So what did we learn? That football fans helped their own side far more than rivals. That a broader shared identity erased most of the gap. That a common ingroup identity can reduce bias. And that the boundary of us is something we can redraw.
